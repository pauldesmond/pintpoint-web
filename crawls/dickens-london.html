CrawlLondon, City to Southwark

The Dickens crawl

Five pubs, about four miles, from an alley off Cornhill to a galleried yard on Borough High Street. These are the houses every Dickens walk visits, in the order the ground suggests. What the brass plates say and what the record supports are two different things, so each stop below carries both.

The short version

One of these five has a visit Dickens described in his own words. One appears in none of the books about him at all. The most famous of them has the thinnest case of the lot.

None of that is an argument against the walk. They are five good pubs and two are among the best buildings left in London. It is an argument against believing the plaque on the way in.

The five

1
The George & Vulture
3 Castle Court, EC3V 9DL
A house in a novel

Mr Pickwick’s headquarters, and B. W. Matz, who wrote the standard books on Dickensian inns, treats it as exactly that. Across four period books there is no claim that Dickens himself drank here. The floor shown to visitors is the floor tradition has decided was Mr Pickwick’s bedroom.

A beautiful pub in an alley off Cornhill all the same. Note that there is a second George and Vulture in Hoxton: this is not it.

2
Ye Olde Cheshire Cheese
145 Fleet Street, EC4A 2BU
Famous, and the thinnest case here

Matz, in 1922, could not find it in the books at all. Never mentioned in any one of Dickens’s books by name, he writes, nor alluded to or described under an assumed name. He adds that it is known Dickens visited, and offers no letter, no diary and no Forster for it. What he produces is a menu card showing Dickens and Thackeray at a table, which is a souvenir rather than a source.

A warren of dark rooms off Fleet Street, and worth the stop on its own account.

3
Lamb & Flag
3 Rose Street, WC2E 9EB
Nothing at all, and go anyway

It appears in neither of Matz’s books, nor in Hughes in 1891, nor in Miltoun in 1903. The negative is checked in all four.

It is a lovely pub, and that is reason enough to drink in it.

4
The Red Lion
48 Parliament Street, SW1A 2NH
The one he described himself

In David Copperfield a small boy puts his money on the counter and asks for a glass of the Genuine Stunning. The landlord calls his wife, she opens the half-door of the bar, gives the boy his money back and kisses him.

It happened to Dickens, in the blacking-factory years, and he said so himself in the account Forster printed in his Life. He does not name the house. He describes it, at the corner of the short street leading into Cannon Row, and Matz identifies this one from the description. The building he walked into came down in 1899; there is a bust of him in a niche on the one that replaced it.

5
The George Inn
75 Borough High Street, SE1 1NH
Where Matz loses patience

The legend is that the George is the original of the White Hart in Pickwick. Matz goes through it, rejects it, and on the writer who pointed out the seat Dickens used to sit in as a boy in Lant Street says flatly that it is sheer nonsense.

Then he is scrupulous about what survives. Dickens may have known the house as a boy and was unlikely to have frequented it; in later years he may well have visited, and the hostess told Matz so on the authority of a carman who said he had chatted with him. Thin, and honestly thin.

The galleries are the thing. The last galleried coaching inn in London, with its outside wooden galleries still standing, which is a better fact than any of the stories.

Walking it

The order above runs west and then south: Cornhill to Fleet Street, Fleet Street to Covent Garden, Covent Garden down Whitehall, then over the river to Southwark. About four miles with the river crossing at the end, and every leg of it walkable in twenty minutes or so.

Two of the five keep City hours rather than pub hours, so a weekday walk is a different proposition from a Saturday one. Check before you set out, and start early if you want the George and Vulture open.
