A Postcard from London: The City That Taught the World to Brew, Then Started Again

London has been brewing for centuries. The version you can drink now dates from 2009.

London holds porter, IPA, cask ale, tied houses, historic pubs, railway arches, modern taprooms, specialist bars, bottle-shop pours, hype breweries and quiet locals.

The pubs were always there. The cask tradition never fully disappeared. But the modern London craft-beer scene, the one built around Bermondsey arches, Hackney taprooms, specialist bars and constantly rotating keg lines, dates from 2009.

The city that had already been a beer capital

Porter was a London beer, a style the city effectively invented in the 18th century. India Pale Ale became globally famous through British brewing and trade.

Through the 19th and early 20th centuries the city was a brewing power: Charrington's at Mile End, Ind Coope at Romford, Watney Combe Reid, Whitbread on Chiswell Street, Truman's on Brick Lane, Courage on the south bank. Whitbread was the largest brewery in the world by the 1780s, and Truman's one of the largest by the end of the 19th century.

Then the shape changed. Consolidation, closures, property pressure, and changing drinking habits thinned out the city's brewing identity. Des de Moor's list of London brewers counts 11 commercial breweries at the end of 1971, none of them brewpubs. Only Fuller's in Chiswick, Young's (soon to leave Wandsworth), and a handful of family operations held the line.

How India Pale Ale came out of London

In the late 18th and early 19th centuries London Pale Ales were already being exported to British India. IPAs were formulated to survive the long voyage better than other styles; they were only slightly stronger than other beers of the time, but more fully fermented and heavily hopped. Hops and alcohol are both natural preservatives.

George Hodgson's Bow Brewery, on the Middlesex-Essex boundary, was among the first brewers known to export beer to India. Burton-upon-Trent later became the dominant production centre because the mineral-rich Burton water suited the style, and names like Bass and Ind Coope's Burton operation turned IPA into a mass export.

The rebuild

Evin O'Riordain founded The Kernel on Druid Street, Bermondsey, in 2009. Within ten years it had helped inspire around a hundred new microbreweries in the capital.

Other breweries followed The Kernel into the arches for the relatively cheap rent, and the Bermondsey Beer Mile grew from there.

Logan Plant started Beavertown in December 2011, in the kitchen of Duke's Brew & Que, his barbecue restaurant in De Beauvoir Town. Gamma Ray, Neck Oil and 8 Ball were first brewed there.

In Hackney, Five Points has brewed since 2013 and Howling Hops is in Hackney Wick. Pressure Drop brewed in Hackney from 2013 until it moved to Tottenham Hale in 2017.

The specialist bars are The Rake, Euston Tap, Mother Kelly's and Craft Beer Co, among others.

Bermondsey changed the map

The Bermondsey Beer Mile deserves its reputation, though residents have criticised the crowds, pub crawls and stag dos it draws.

It worked because it gave London a simple beer route in a city that is otherwise too large to summarise.

The Kernel, Partizan, Anspach & Hobday, Brew By Numbers and the wider Bermondsey cluster made craft beer feel physical.

Hackney made it feel mature

Five Points, Howling Hops, Pressure Drop (by then at Tottenham Hale) and the wider east London cluster showed that London craft beer did not have to live only in Saturday-afternoon railway-arch crawls. It could sit inside neighbourhood drinking, food, markets, music, pubs, and regular weekends.

What makes London different

London's beer is spread across pubs, taprooms, specialist bars, bottle shops, cask houses, railway arches and neighbourhood locals:

  • Historic pubs that still know how to keep cask properly.
  • Modern taprooms pouring beer close to where it was made.
  • Specialist bars with short, sharp, constantly changing lists.
  • Bottle shops and hybrid spaces that blur retail and drinking.
  • Neighbourhood pubs that also keep craft beer on.

The honest critique

London's beer scene is not as wild as it was in 2013 or 2014.

The first wave had more surprise. New breweries felt like discoveries. Taproom weekends felt less formal. The Beer Mile had more rough edges and fewer stag parties. Some breweries closed, some changed, and some sold: Heineken bought a minority stake in Beavertown in 2018 and took it over in 2022.

London is also expensive. The city's scale means the scene is fragmented, and a visitor can easily waste a weekend making the wrong jumps.

A two-day visitor itinerary

If you have a weekend:

  • Saturday afternoon: Bermondsey. Start at Southwark Brewing on Druid Street and walk south past Anspach & Hobday to The Kernel on Spa Road. Saturday is the only day every stop opens.
  • Saturday evening: central. The Rake for tight curation, then a proper cask stop.
  • Sunday afternoon: Hackney. Five Points and Howling Hops in Hackney, then Pressure Drop's taproom at Tottenham Hale.
  • Sunday evening: a real pub. Finish in a pub.

A final word

The scene you drink in now, the arches, the keg lines, the specialist bars and the weekly brewery taprooms, dates from 2009.

Frequently asked questions

Is London a good craft-beer city?

Yes. It combines modern breweries, specialist bars, historic pubs, cask culture, taprooms and neighbourhood drinking in one city.

What is the Bermondsey Beer Mile?

A cluster of breweries and taprooms under and around the railway arches in south-east London between London Bridge and South Bermondsey. The Kernel opened first in 2009; Anspach & Hobday, Southwark Brewing and others followed. Saturday is the only day every taproom is open.

Is London better for pubs or breweries?

Both. That is what makes London different. The city has serious modern breweries and taprooms, but its pub culture, cask ale and tied houses and the social logic of the local, remains central to how the city drinks.

What beer style is London known for?

Historically, London is associated with porter (a London invention in the 18th century), stout, cask bitter, and the export-grade Pale Ale that evolved into India Pale Ale. The modern scene is much broader: pale ales, hazy IPAs, mixed-fermentation, stouts, modern lager, and cask ale still poured alongside it.

What happened to the historic big London breweries?

Most of the historic big brewers (Charrington, Ind Coope, Watney Combe Reid, Whitbread, Truman's, Courage) closed, merged, or moved production out of London through the 20th-century consolidation. Only Fuller's, Young's, and a handful of family operations remained.

Where does India Pale Ale come from?

IPA evolved from late-18th and early-19th century London Pale Ales brewed for export to British India. They were only slightly stronger than other beers of the time, but more fully fermented and heavily hopped. George Hodgson's Bow Brewery was among the first brewers known to export beer to India. Burton-upon-Trent later became the dominant production centre because of its mineral-rich water.
