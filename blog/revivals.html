Revivals, recreations or just echoes?

Why do I want these beers back?

The interesting question isn't whether the beer is authentic. It's whether anyone is being honest about what came back.

Partly it's nostalgia: the thought that I might be drinking something resembling what my father drank. I know perfectly well that most 1970s beer probably wouldn't suit taste buds ruined by years of heavily hopped West Coast IPAs. But I'll still buy the pint.

The question is: what exactly am I buying?

Because British beer is currently having a revival revival. Old names are appearing on bars again. Some have been painstakingly reconstructed. Some have been reinvented for modern drinkers. Some are entirely new beers wearing dead men's clothes. And perhaps that's fine.

The revival spectrum

What actually survived? The name, the recipe, the yeast, the people, the place, or merely the memory? British beer revivals fall into four rough shapes. The spectrum measures what came back; the thesis under it measures the separate question of whether anyone is being honest about it — which is why Hofmeister, scoring nothing on continuity, still lands in the most honest quadrant of the piece.

A revival that produces a better beer than the original is not a failure. A revival that pretends the original is what it is bringing back, when it isn't, is.

Two ways to bring back a beer

Two revivals. Same broad heritage. Opposite philosophies.

Allsopp's Double Diamond (the reinterpretation) relaunched in October 2024 as a 3.8% pale ale "inspired by" the 1922 recipe. Hops: Citra, Columbus, Mosaic — the 2010s American craft palette. Jamie Allsopp's own launch text is candid, if not by intention: "Revived as a 3.8% pale ale that drinks like a lager."

The historical Double Diamond ran at two strengths depending on format: bottled at roughly 4.8% (a fuller malt version, which Pellicle’s history of Double Diamond and Ian Webster’s Beertonian piece has as the best-selling bottled beer in the UK by 1958) and draught keg at roughly 4.0% (the industrial version that arrived in the 1960s and drove sales down 60% between 1972 and 1978). The modern 3.8% is essentially the draught keg strength. Which is the interesting rhetorical move: Allsopp's cites 1922 (bottled-era heritage) while delivering something closer to the 1960s keg spec.

To be fair to Allsopp's, the older Double Diamond was not automatically beloved. Comments under the trade-press coverage of the revival are brutal on the original: "universally loathed", "sickly sweet", "full of caramel and sticky". Roger Protz was blunter: "Double Diamond and other similar beers tasted awful as a result of the manner in which they were produced." The badge is iconic; the liquid may still have been bad. Allsopp's failure is not that it made a modern beer. It is that it asks the archive to do more rhetorical work than the recipe can support.

Burton Bridge DBA (the continuity) launched in March 2015. The brewers had worked on the original Ind Coope Draught Burton Ale before Carlsberg discontinued it. Their own framing is unmistakably humble: "It would be impossible to brew an exact replica, but we are sure it is as close as possible." 4.8%. Drinkers who remember the original describe it as "smooth, sweet, a little bitter", "absolutely gorgeous, rich and almost sticky on the palate". One revival is selling you a 1922 ledger and a 2024 hop bill. The other is saying: we worked on this once, we know what it tasted like, we got as close as we could.

The cleanest benchmark for the badge type is Hofmeister, of all things. Discontinued in 2003, revived from 2015 by two ex-Heineken employees who bought naming rights from Heineken and brewed a proper Bavarian Helles in Market Schwaben. It won Best Lager at IWSC 2017. And the brewery's own account of what it did is exactly the sentence Allsopp's should have used: "The permission to use the name and the bear's likeness was about all they wanted of the old Hofmeister: the beer itself was to be completely new." That is badge-honesty done properly.

Which era are you reviving?

This is the axis that saves the piece from soft-focus nostalgia. A 1970s brand can revive the 1970s recipe — usually cold, carbonated, industrial keg — or it can leapfrog the bad years and revive the beer that corporate brewing interrupted.

David Behrens's Stones Bitter is the clearest case. Writing in the Yorkshire Post, he remembers the 1970s Stones as "ghastly" and "gassy", the "gaudy orange badge on the pump" that "embodied everything that was wrong with Britain at that time". But he also blesses the True North Brew Co revival in Sheffield, on precisely the ground that True North is not returning to the keg era. They are returning to the beer that pre-dated it: cask, drawn from the wood, with the recipe adjusted to replenish the salt Sheffield steelworkers lost sweating in the foundries.

"Its rebirth brings it full circle," Behrens writes. The pre-industrial version had a purpose. The industrial one did not. The revival worth having is the one that returns to purpose.

The absentee landlords

The people who own the dead names are often not the people capable of bringing the beers meaningfully back. Heineken holds Watney's and Truman's. Carlsberg-Marston’s has the Ind Coope / Jennings / Banks’s / Bombardier trail, a lineage Des de Moor has mapped in detail. AB InBev retains Boddingtons. Molson Coors runs Carling. The genuinely interesting work happens when the IP is licensed out — or abandoned — into the hands of people with a regional or technical reason to care. Burton Bridge, Jennings under the Canfields, True North, JW Lees.

Carlsberg keeps turning up at the scene. It discontinued Ind Coope's Draught Burton Ale before Burton Bridge picked it up. It closed Jennings Brewery in Cockermouth in November 2022 before the Canfields bought it back. Its Marston's operation has axed Banks's Mild and Bombardier in recent years.

None of that is a moral verdict on the aggregators. It's a structural observation: honesty gets easier the further the brewer sits from the boardroom.

The archaeology

The archaeology type is where the honesty question gets tested in the most literal way possible. Did anyone manage to preserve a piece of the actual thing — a yeast strain, a bottle, a technical fragment — rather than just a name and a rough recipe?

Truman's is the London example. The Brick Lane brewery closed in 1989, but the strain had already been deposited at the National Collection of Yeast Cultures in 1958. Fifty-five years later, the insurance paid out: two young professionals revived the brand in 2013 and simply asked for the yeast back. Most of the recipes are new; the brand has since been sold to the property company that owns the Brick Lane site; but the yeast is the actual one. Yeast-authentic, recipe-modern, ownership awkward — but the biology is real.

Tennent's did it with a bottle instead of a strain. In the 1970s a Geelong diver named Jim Anderson pulled a bottle of 1868 Tennent's stout from the wreck of The Light of The Age, which foundered off Port Phillip Heads near Melbourne in January 1868. Anderson flew it back to Wellpark Brewery in Glasgow 150 years later, in November 2018 — "one of the oldest bottles of beer in Britain", pre-dating Tennent's Lager (1885). Tennent's then brewed a commemorative stout using archive recipes anchored by what the surviving bottle told them. The artefact came first; the beer followed it.

Project Jurassic Beer

The archaeology case that goes furthest is Guinness, and it fits this piece's argument in an unexpected way. In 2025 the Belgian underwater photographer Stefan Panis and his diving partner Eddie Huzzey recovered an intact bottle of Guinness from the wreck of the sailing barque Mindoro, which sank in the English Channel about three miles off Dover on 27 November 1864. The bottle survived the sinking and 162 years underwater. Panis's colleague Pawel Truszynski contacted Kevin Verstrepen, a microbiologist specialising in beer at KU Leuven University in Belgium. The divers themselves named the effort Project Jurassic Beer. Panis's ambition is direct: "There is always a chance that we can recover the complete DNA, and then we can do a small re-brew of the exact beer that was found in the 1860s."

Diageo, which owns Guinness, has been in touch with the divers via the Guinness Archive team. Its full spokesperson statement, per the BBC: "Discoveries like this provide a fascinating window into our past and help build a deeper understanding of our heritage. Currently, our Guinness Archive team is liaising with the divers to gather more information and evaluate the find." Which is telling. Diageo is engaged, but the science and the naming and the ambition sit with the divers and KU Leuven, not Dublin. If a biological continuity attempt at Guinness scale actually succeeds, it will have been driven by a Belgian underwater photographer who is by his own admission "not that big" a Guinness fan, his colleague, and Kevin Verstrepen at KU Leuven. Which is the pattern this piece has been describing all along. The interesting work happens outside the boardroom.

The duty scale is the invisible editor

Since August 2023, UK alcohol duty is charged proportional to strength rather than by product category. Higher-ABV beer costs the brewer more duty per hectolitre; lower-ABV beer costs less. That single change has been quietly reshaping recipes. Belhaven Twisted Thistle IPA came back at 4.0% versus an original 5.6%. Marketing text will call this "evolving the range" or "designed for today's beer market" or "drinks like a lager". Sometimes the real editor isn't the archive. It's the tax scale.

The hop list is the receipt

Within seconds of reading a revival's spec sheet, the hops tell you almost everything. Modern American C-hops — Citra, Centennial, Columbus, Cascade, Mosaic, Galaxy — are the 2010s craft-IPA palette. Classic English hops — Fuggles, Goldings, Challenger, Bramling Cross, Target, Pilgrim, plus Central European Celeia and Saaz — are the nineteenth- and twentieth-century British palette. A heritage revival that uses American C-hops while claiming "as it was in 1922" is either being honest about reinventing the beer or pretending the historical anchor is real when it isn't.

Compare the specs. Allsopp's Double Diamond, claiming 1922 inspiration: Citra, Columbus, Mosaic. Jennings, reviving its own 1990s core: Fuggles, Goldings, Challenger and Celeia hops, with Maris Otter malt and, of course, the brewery's own Cumbrian well water. That last part is not a marketing point. It is the recipe. Burton Bridge DBA: traditional Burton-Ale style. Modern hops are not bad; there is nothing wrong with a Citra/Mosaic pale ale. The problem is only rhetorical — if the archive is what is being sold, the hop list has to match the archive.

Jennings is worth pausing on because it does the disclosure gracefully. Sneck Lifter — Northern dialect for a last sixpence, the money to lift a pub-door latch. Cumberland Ale, the North West bestseller. Cocker Hoop, a cask-spigot joke. And "Back Yam", introduced in 2026 to mark the revival and openly labelled as new, not smuggled in as heritage. That single word is the discipline the revival category needs more of.

The Boddingtons paradox

Boddingtons is the corporate-licensed continuity in its cleanest form. AB InBev owns the brand. JW Lees brews the cask revival in Manchester. The editorial jewel of the licence agreement — described by JW Lees' production director Michael Lees-Jones — is that the brewery's own house yeast is a "non-negotiable" clause. Corporate IP, local biology, cask handpull. It is a specific answer to "which era": pre-corporate, pre-keg, pre-Interbrew acquisition.

The argument CAMRA’s What’s Brewing makes for it is generous but not naive. Boddingtons is not reinventing the wheel. There are more interesting pints at any given handpull, JW Lees's own included. But a familiar name lowers the barrier for younger drinkers who find a row of unknown handpulls intimidating — and the numbers seem to back it. Bass Pale Ale has reportedly grown 19% year-on-year; early Boddingtons demand in Manchester outstripped supply. Heritage brands are turning out to be a cask-beer gateway drug.

The cost is real too. The world's largest brewer is muscling back onto bar space that would otherwise go to smaller independents. Two things at once: familiar names bring new drinkers to cask, and they crowd out the brewers who kept cask interesting while the big brands were away. The revival argument does not have to resolve that tension. It just has to name it.

The shelf-presence defence

None of this rules out simply wanting the badge on the shelf. A flawed Double Diamond next to a John Bull cartoon is a better Tesco end-cap than another row of identical craft-lager cans that nobody will remember in two years. That is a real editorial position, and it survives the honesty audit — as long as no-one is pretending the liquid is what it isn't.

But some beers will never get a licensed revival. No corporate owner will bring them back; no supermarket will make room for them. Those beers belong somewhere else: in archive recipes of the kind Ron Pattinson keeps publishing at Barclay Perkins, old brewery booklets, homebrewers' sheds, and memory. Which is where Romford's John Bull Bitter — and a few other ghost beers — may have to live.

Though sometimes the archive answers back. In October 2026 Harvey’s of Lewes released Lost Brew No 156, a 7.7% Victorian strong ale brewed from an 1890 journal that Pattinson himself turned up in 2019 in the archives of the former Beard’s Brewery, the other brewery in the same town. It had been filed under Beard’s, which is why nobody had found it, but the pages are headed Harvey & Son, Bridge Wharf Brewery. Beer Today reports that the journal is thought to have belonged to Henry John Barrett, son of Harvey’s third head brewer, and that head brewer Peter Yarlett’s team brewed it in the same tower it first came out of, with heritage British barley, Sussex Fuggles and Kent Goldings. Put that against the questions this piece keeps asking and it does unusually well. The recipe survived. The brewery survived. The man who wrote the thing down was the son of the man who ran the place. The yeast, as ever, is the open one. Five more recipes from the same journal are promised.

The ghosts are welcome

I still want them back. Double Diamond, Stones, Boddingtons, John Bull and whatever other ghosts the British beer trade chooses to summon next. Partly because I want to know what they tasted like, and partly because there's something irresistible about ordering a pint my father might once have ordered.

I don't need it to be identical. In most cases it can't be. And occasionally, as Stones demonstrates, perhaps we should be grateful that it isn't.

Just tell me what survived.

The recipe. The yeast. The water. The brewery. The people. The badge.

Or nothing but the name.

The ghosts are welcome. Just call them by their proper names.

Update: the Famous Original

On 15 October 2026 Allsopp’s launched Allsopp’s IPA, a 4.4% cask beer brewed with Kirkstall Brewery in Leeds. Golden Promise malt, Challenger hops, on-trade only, in nines.

The hop list passes. One English hop against an English pale malt is the nineteenth and twentieth-century palette, and it is a real change from the Citra, Columbus and Mosaic that sat behind Double Diamond’s 1922 claim. Jamie Allsopp’s line to Gary Lloyd of the Morning Advertiser is close to the one we borrowed from Hofmeister and said Allsopp’s should have used: “We did not want to produce a museum piece.”

The badge is another matter. Allsopp’s own site calls the beer the Famous Original. “In 1822, Samuel Allsopp brewed a beer in a teapot, a beer designed specifically to survive the long sea journey to India. In so doing, he perfected the style we now call India Pale Ale, and a legend was born. That’s why we call our IPA the Famous Original.” The front page has the brand returning “to the beer that made its fortune” and says it has “revived the Famous Original”.

So the overclaim has not been dropped. It has been promoted, out of the launch copy and into the product’s name. Double Diamond asked the archive to vouch for a date the recipe did not match. This asks it to vouch for the word original, on a 4.4% cask beer brewed in Leeds in 2026.

Both things went out in the same week, to different rooms. The trade press got the contemporary cask beer. The public site got the teapot.

There is a third voice in the same range. Allsopp’s already sells an India Pale Ale at 5%, which is in our catalogue, and the brand’s description of that one reads: “Samuel Allsopp was the first to perfect Burton-brewed India Pale Ale. Some 200 years later, Allsopp & Sons invite you to savour this faithful recreation of the complex, beguiling, original Burton IPA.” A faithful recreation, a Famous Original, and a beer that is expressly not a museum piece, all under one badge.

One thing in their favour, since the badge has taken a beating here. The site’s own line on Double Diamond is “Reborn for the modern era”, which is the honest version, and a better sentence than the 2024 launch managed. They can write it when they want to.

Two things for the record. The ledger, which the launch calls the “historic-underpinning” of the whole revival and which was found in a Leeds pub with more than a hundred handwritten recipes in it, appears nowhere on Allsopp’s own site. And Hodgson’s Bow Brewery had been supplying the India trade out of London for decades before Burton went near it, which is the usual trouble with the word first.

Call them by their proper names. This range is going to need three.

Update: the archive in the glass

Added 11 November 2026. This belongs in the archaeology row above, and it is the strongest example of that type we know of. It was on sale before this piece was published on 17 August 2026, so the omission was ours. It is kept separate so you can tell which is which.

Innis & Gunn released 1875 Arctic Ale in December 2025, brewed with Allsopp’s. The original was brewed by Allsopp’s in Burton for Sir George Nares’s attempt on the North Pole, and before that for the expeditions that went looking for Franklin in the 1850s. STV’s report of the day has Dougal Gunn Sharp, who founded Innis & Gunn, bidding £3,000 at auction for one of five known surviving bottles, after it turned up in a garage in Shropshire, and opening it at the Perth brewery on 13 November 2025; Beer Today dates the purchase to 2015. A portion of it went into the new brew. Roger Protz has the recipe: Maris Otter and Chevallier pale malts, amber and crystal malts, Fuggles and Goldings hops, and ten per cent brewing sugar to reach 9.5%. On the question this piece keeps asking, Protz is unambiguous: “Allsopp’s original yeast culture was used.” He puts the original itself above ten per cent, hedged as what is thought rather than anything recorded. Jamie Allsopp, to Protz: “We were pouring history into the new brew.” Beer Today has the release at 250 bottles, balloted, with draught at the Edinburgh and Glasgow taprooms.

Against the archaeology row it scores where nothing else does. Truman’s had a strain in a collection. The Mindoro divers are still trying to raise a yeast. Harvey’s had the paperwork. This one had the recipe, the yeast and a quantity of the 1875 beer, and the last of those is in the glass. Which is awkward for the argument this piece makes, because it is the same Jamie Allsopp. The charge against Double Diamond and the Famous Original was never that Allsopp’s cannot do this properly. It is that it claimed the archive where it did not have one. Here it had one.

Reference: recreating from the records

The archaeology above turns on a surviving fragment: Truman’s had a yeast strain in a collection, Tennent’s had a bottle off a wreck. There is a quieter version where nothing physical survives and the paperwork does, and it belongs beside the archaeology rather than inside it.

Fuller’s Past Masters. Head brewer John Keeling and brewing manager Derek Prentice worked from the brewery’s own leather-bound 19th-century brew books, in copper-plate, with the brewing historian Ron Pattinson. Willard Clarke reported it for Roger Protz’s site in February 2011: XX Strong Ale at 7.5%, bottle-conditioned, from the 1890s, brewed with Plumage Archer barley grown from kept seed, Fuggles and Goldings, and the invert sugar the period demanded. The 1893 price list Prentice produced ran from about 5.6% to 8.2%, so the weakest beer Fuller’s sold then was stronger than anything they sell on draught now.

And it doubles as a test of what a house yeast does. Boak & Bailey drank XX Strong Ale and Double Stout in August 2011 and led with the yeast: “Despite a different variety of malt and a very different method of production, that XX is a Fuller’s beer is made obvious by the aroma and tang of orange marmalade which assails you as you drink it.” Different barley, different method, a recipe from another century, and the brewery still announced itself. Their own definition of the exercise is the useful one: it is not an antique, it is a fresh, clean beer made to an old recipe.

Moor Rebirth is the same move run on a living beer. In 2021 Justin Hawke rebuilt the original 1971 ESB with Fuller’s help, and told Protz the 1971 recipe was radically different from today’s: maize in the grist, since dropped, which Keeling said let the malt open up and breathe, and a hop bill spanning Britain, America, Australia and Slovenia.

The contrast that makes it a category. In the same 2011 report, Meantime recreated an 18th-century Imperial Russian Stout on what the piece calls “educated guesswork”, because Barclay Perkins and Courage are gone and their records went with them. Fuller’s could read the answer; Meantime had to infer it. A brewery that never closed keeps its books, and the books turn out to be the fragment.
