Before Kick-Off: Why English Cask Has the Advantage

The football is heading to the business end. The Beer World Cup is heading the same way. And for the first time in a long time, English cask has a credible case to be the format that brings a trophy home — sixty years on from Wembley, with the Perfect Pint Promise barely a week old and the heritage cask category visibly back on the rise.

The argument works because it is not just a joke. Cask is the one beer format England can still claim without squinting: local, fragile, pub-dependent, and impossible to separate from the person keeping it properly in the cellar.

That is why the case for English cask at the Beer World Cup lands. Not because cask is cleaner, colder, stronger or more technically perfect than anything else. Because cask is the format that turns a pub into part of the beer.

So: who'd be in the line-up? An eleven-strong squad of properly kept cask beers. Why does that matter? Because warm, flat, hand-pulled bitter is the one beer category no other country dares compete in. Abroad it is a novelty. In Blighty it is a speciality.

Warm and flat, and neither

Cask is unfiltered, unpasteurised beer that finishes fermenting in the pub cellar, poured at cellar temperature with the carbonation that secondary fermentation gives it. Anywhere else those words are a fault report; here they are the format. The long version, with what the trade’s own numbers say about who is drinking it now, is in Cask Ale Week: the Format Nobody Else Can Pour.

Somebody checks the cellars

The Cask Marque Trust has been inspecting pub cellars for nearly thirty years, twice a year, by trained assessors — the plaque on the door, the sticker in the window, the listing in CaskFinder. Its beer-quality accreditation now trades under plainer language as the Perfect Pint Promise, and covers all draught rather than cask alone. What that promise is for is set out in Cask Ale Week: the Format Nobody Else Can Pour.

A category with a membership

Cask survived the 1970s because a consumer organisation argued for it: CAMRA, founded in 1971, and the Good Beer Guide, the festivals, the Pub of the Year network and fifty years of policy work that came with it. That membership is the moat, and no other beer category has one.

The Cask XI

So if England is going to make a serious case at a Beer World Cup, the team cannot be a squad of lager clones and international pale-ale lookalikes. Other countries do those brilliantly. Some do them better. England's strongest hand is older, stranger, softer and more pub-shaped: cask ale.

That is not just nostalgia. Cask carries English beer's heritage, history and stubborn local memory in a way no single modern style can. It depends on cellars, handpulls, throughput, publicans, regulars, regional breweries and the quiet skill of serving beer alive rather than merely cold. It is fragile, unfashionable in places, and easy to get wrong. But when it is right, it is the thing nobody else can quite copy.

That is why the England XI is all cask. Not because every English beer should be cask, and not because keg, lager or modern pale ale do not belong in English pubs. They do. But if the question is what England can send out against the world with the deepest roots and the clearest identity, the answer comes through a handpull.

So: who would we line up with? Eleven pints that, between them, cover the country properly. Not eleven shouts from the back. Eleven beers that any pub in any region would be lucky to have on. We have stayed away from the obvious pyramid of nostalgia — there are no team-sheet appearances for Greene King IPA or Cloudwater here — and stayed close to beers that still make sense on hand pump, in the kind of cellars Cask Marque exists to protect.

England · Starting XI

  1. Black Sheep Best Bitter — captain. Masham, and the other brewery in Theakston’s town, started by a Theakston who walked out of the family firm.
  2. Wadworth 6X — vice-captain. Devizes. Wiltshire’s everyday bitter, and the West Country’s default pint for a century.
  3. St Austell Tribute — Cornwall’s traveller: the pale that made it out of the county and onto bars everywhere east of it.
  4. Sharp's Doom Bar — Rock, Cornwall. The one Landlord has only just overtaken, and still on more bars than almost anything.
  5. Boddingtons (cask) — Manchester, via J.W. Lees, and back on hand pump since 2025. The keg widget version isn't this beer.
  6. Adnams Ghost Ship — Southwold. Suffolk's quiet diplomat, and the one that wins the under-thirties.
  7. Theakston Old Peculier — Masham. Fifth-generation family brewing, and the one for a winter goal.
  8. Hook Norton Hooky Bitter — Oxfordshire, from a tower brewery that still runs on steam. Perennial Cask Marque favourite.
  9. Brakspear Bitter — Henley by birth, Witney by address. Bone-dry, double-dropped, faintly orange-peel.
  10. Marston's Pedigree — Burton. The Unions are gone; the beer endures. Pedigree by name and nature.
  11. Adnams Broadside — Southwold's other one. Strong, malty, Trafalgar-named, and there to anchor a back four.

Bench: Fuller’s London Pride, Timothy Taylor’s Landlord, Draught Bass, Harvey’s Sussex Best. The famous four, held back because everyone already knows what they can do. Caledonian Deuchars IPA would have started, but plays for Scotland.

What this team has that others don’t

Look down the eleven. Every one is from a region, a brewery town, a pub network, or a drinker memory that still means something. Several are still family-owned; the rest sit inside larger portfolios where the commercial logic is now plain: do not mess with the thing drinkers came back for.

That is the moat, and it is a matter of format rather than age: Belgium has older brewers and Germany older breweries, but only cask has to be finished in the cellar of the pub that serves it. The full version of that argument, and what it means for who is drinking cask now, is in Cask Ale Week: the Format Nobody Else Can Pour.

And now the big brewers want the old names

Heritage cask has become heritage IP: Damm has bought the Old Speckled Hen family from Greene King, J.W. Lees has Boddingtons back on cask, Allsopp’s and Double Diamond are being revived, and Draught Bass is on more than a thousand hand pumps again. Flattering, and slightly dangerous — the handpull is fashionable again, and the awkward bit is who owns the badge above it.

Where to drink the Cask XI

Most of the eleven travel into London freehouses; the rest are easy to find in their home regions. PINtPOINT lists pubs serving each one by venue — the simplest way to organise an evening is to pick a player and let the venue list do the rest. The harder pints are honest about themselves: Boddingtons in genuine cask is rare, and the Whippet's J.W. Lees-brewed hand pull is one of the few places in London you will get it.

If you want to do it properly, look for the plaque on the way in. With the Beer World Cup conversation already loud, that promise feels overdue.

Sixty years on

Football has not been kind to England for a long time. The Beer World Cup might be kinder, if we write the rules right and play the format we have. Sixty years since 1966 sounds like a long time. It is. It is also the same span of time over which British cask went from a near-extinct format to a community-owned category, defended by a membership organisation, audited by an independent standard, and now relaunched as a consumer promise the rest of the world cannot copy.

That is something to argue for. Loudly. The final whistle is still to come.

P.S. — a clutch of British brewers have brewed tournament-specific specials for this exact moment. None qualify for the heritage Cask XI (they are days old, not decades) but all worth a flag if you spot one on a hand pump in the next few weeks: Greene King's 60 Years of Hurst (4% golden pale, made with Sir Geoff Hurst MBE, 5p from every pint to Diverse Abilities); Iron Pier's 1966 (4.7% Manchester Bitter, historic recipe from England's victorious World Cup year, spotted on hand-pull this week at the Hopper's Hut in Kent); Bradfield's Farmers World Cup Ale (4.2% pale hoppy, sold in 9-gallon casks); Norfolk Brewhouse's Skipper World Cup Special (4.2% best bitter, transatlantic hop blend); Woodforde's Route to 1966 (3.4% session pale, grapefruit and lemon); Saltaire's Golden Boot (4.8% English pale ale, citrus and orange peel, limited edition); and Milestone's World Cup Gold (4.5% golden ale, orange and tangerine notes). The names alone tell you what every British brewer is reaching for: the 1966 anniversary.

FAQ

Why does CAMRA still matter?

CAMRA — the Campaign for Real Ale — has been the consumer organisation that argues for properly kept living beer since 1971. Without it, much of what survives as British cask might not. The Good Beer Guide, local festivals, the Pub of the Year network and decades of policy work on pub protections are CAMRA's work. The community is the reason cask still exists at the scale it does.

Where can I find the Cask XI?

Most of the eleven are well-distributed in their home regions and travel into London freehouses. PINtPOINT lists pubs serving each by venue. Boddingtons in cask is rarer — the Whippet in EC2 keeps a hand-pulled version contract-brewed by J.W. Lees in Manchester.
