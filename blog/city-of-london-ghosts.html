Old Haunts of the Square Mile

Claret, red braces and three-hour lunches beneath the modern City.

Before flat whites, iPhone apps and overbearing regulatory frameworks, the Square Mile ran on basement stairs, expense accounts and wine lists as thick as the Yellow Pages.

Step off the Central Line at Bank on a hot Thursday in July 1994 and what reached you first was cheap cologne, tobacco, ink and old City hands pickled in claret and port.

Welcome to the City of London in the mid-1990s.

The Square Mile operated under a very different set of biological and corporate laws. Compliance and human resources had not yet reached the drinking calendar. The markets were still expanding after the 1986 Big Bang, and whole new classes of trader, broker, lawyer and insurance man were discovering what happened when corporate money met institutional thirst.

Large sums were being made young. There was one obvious thing to do with that capital: diversify into liquid assets between noon and half-three, stumble back upstairs to send a few things into motion, then head back out at 5:01pm to do it all over again.

Five bar empires ran the map, working as unofficial branch offices of the Stock Exchange, LIFFE, Lloyd's and every firm whose stationery could be turned into an expense claim. Between them they covered every shade of financial fauna, from sawdust-and-pewter die-hards to neon-cocktail trailblazers.

The Big Five: colosseums of the Square Mile

Five operators between them defined how the City drank between the Big Bang and the smoking ban.

The Evening Standard London Pub & Bar Guide 1999 named all five among its ten recommended theme bars for the capital.

1. Balls Brothers

Balls Brothers was founded around 1860 as a wine merchant and began concentrating on City wine bars in the 1960s.

The menu was stubbornly British: cold roast beef, heavy slabs of English cheddar, game terrine.

Then and now

  • Then: 14 sites across the City and beyond in 1999.
  • Now: the original company went into administration in November 2010; in March 2011 Novus Leisure bought 17 of its 19 bars.
  • The attempted pivot: in 2006 the group bought the Lewis & Clarke bars.

2. All Bar One

Bass launched All Bar One in 1994 as a “female friendly” bar, following the lead of female-friendly chains such as Pitcher & Piano and Slug & Lettuce. The first opened in Sutton, in December 1994.

Then and now

  • Then: Bass Leisure Retail's 1994 statement of intent, 20+ sites across London by 1999. Floor-to-ceiling windows, women welcome not tolerated, blackboard menus you could actually read. The Evening Standard Guide called it “the success story of the decade.”
  • Now: still trading.

3. Pitcher & Piano

The Cornhill branch was in the former Scottish Widows office on Cornhill.

Then and now

  • Then: City sites included 200 Bishopsgate, 9 Crutched Friars and 28–31 Cornhill.
  • Now: the Bishopsgate site closed in April 2011 and became a Boots. The Crutched Friars bar later became the Bavarian Beerhouse and is now the Munich Cricket Club. The chain still trades under Marston's (2026).

4. Corney & Barrow

Corney & Barrow had sold wine in the City since 1780, and its City bars launched in 1983, feeding and watering the newly minted across the Square Mile and Canary Wharf.

Then and now

  • Then: a slick corporate wine-bar circuit of 10 sites across the City and beyond by 1999, from Broadgate Circle (with summer croquet on the terrace) to Eastcheap.
  • Now: the wine-bar division, eleven bars with ten of them under the Corney & Barrow name, was sold to Drake & Morgan in 2016, and Drake & Morgan said it would keep Corney & Barrow as one of its wine suppliers.
  • The sister chain: Coates, the City wine merchant Corney & Barrow bought in 1959, is covered below.

5. Davy's

Davy's bars kept to a house formula: candles, port bottles racked to head height, house pours in pewter and Old Wallop on hand pump. Names included the Boot & Flogger, Bung Hole Cellars and the Crusting Pipe.

Then and now

  • Then: sawdust, candles, vaulted cellars and Old Wallop, across 30+ sites in London by 1999, per the Evening Standard guide.
  • Now: Davy's still trades; see the Davy's essay for the full account.

Slug & Lettuce changed in the same period, though it was never a City wine bar. After Grosvenor Inns acquired Fast Forward in 1992, the chain was pushed deliberately away from the old pub model: “more of a bar, less of a pub”, younger, more contemporary, less blokey, aiming for an even male-female crowd. Sheila McKenzie, who had founded the female-friendly Pitcher & Piano chain, helped steer that shift.

By 1995 it was being pitched as a “contemporary English bar”, somewhere between a continental café and a classic pub. By 1997 it saw All Bar One and Pitcher & Piano as direct competitors.

The name survived into the Stonegate era.

The Fine Line belongs here as Fuller’s answer to all of it. The first opened in June 1998; three more followed by that September, five more in 1999, and by the summer of 2001 there were eight across London and a ninth in Bristol. That year its operations manager, Rupert Clark, preferred not to call it a retail brand: “I like to think of it as more of a concept.” Deep blue and purple sofas, pale polished wood, one cocktail list with room for local specials.

The Evening Standard went to Battersea in November 1998 and wrote that Fuller’s had adopted “the basic principles of the All Bar One offering” and greatly improved them. Within weeks, the reviewer wrote, it had become “one of the most thumping bars in the area”.

The man running Fuller’s bars division, Harri Owen, had been operations manager at Pitcher & Piano. Rupert Clark came from All Bar One. Other staff came from Slug & Lettuce and Henry’s Café-Bars.

Two of them are still trading in the Square Mile and neither is called the Fine Line any more. The Bow Churchyard one reopened as The Saint in April 2022. The Monument Street one opened in September 1999 and was renamed The Hydrant in 2015, with light fire-brigade theming. Fuller’s still run both.

How a ghost is made

In PINtPOINT a ghost is a closed venue. Ghost Hunter puts them on the radar as purple blips. Tap one and you get the pub as it was, a Street View of what stands there now, the year it closed and its last known tap list, where we have them.

45 London Wall

W. Coates & Co., founded in 1820, had run City wine bars since the second half of the nineteenth century. When Corney & Barrow bought it in 1959 it owned two, one in Old Broad Street and one in London Wall, and the London Wall room became part of the estate that would later become the C&B bar chain proper.

By early October 1986 the room was open as Coates, billed as “the 21st century café.” Nigella Lawson reviewed it for The Spectator on 25 October, “just over a fortnight” after it opened. Tchaik Chassay did the design: granite, maple, merbau, steel and primrose; a bar with a Fifties carpet design; a teeming restaurant beyond; and an overhead Telerate screen giving live foreign-exchange and bond prices. Her verdict:

Here is a place made for the detritus of the Big Bang — in all its gruesome detail.

The lunch menu ran to £5 for two courses. The shop out front sold wine and “breakfast in a briefcase.” Champagne, Perrier, three courses and coffee for two came to £26 with the tip.

By the time of the Evening Standard London Pub & Bar Guide 1999, the same room was doing lunchtime pizza, Young's Ordinary and Scrumpy Jack on cask, karaoke on Wednesdays and Fridays and disco on Thursdays, and the guide relayed a researcher's line in the language of its time: “Essex nymphos by night and an easy place to pull on Thursdays.”

The address passed through the 2000s as Mehek, an Indian restaurant headed by Ashok Kumar, who had worked with Atul Kochhar, and named Best Restaurant in London East at the 2006 British Curry Awards. CAMRA records Mehek as closed in 2015.

Today, 45 London Wall is the Moorgate Post Office.

The room has a page of its own.

Ghosts confirmed in the 1999 book, pinned as a Curated Collection: Old Haunts of the Square Mile.
