Old Haunts of the Square Mile

Claret, red braces and three-hour lunches beneath the modern City.

Before flat whites, iPhone apps and overbearing regulatory frameworks, the Square Mile ran on basement stairs, expense accounts and wine lists as thick as the Yellow Pages.

Step off the Central Line at Bank on a hot Thursday in July 1994 and what reached you first was cheap cologne, tobacco, ink and old City hands pickled in claret and port.

Welcome to the City of London in the mid-1990s.

The Square Mile operated under a very different set of biological and corporate laws. Compliance and human resources had not yet reached the drinking calendar. The markets were still expanding after the 1986 Big Bang, and whole new classes of trader, broker, lawyer and insurance man were discovering what happened when corporate money met institutional thirst.

Large sums were being made young. There was one obvious thing to do with that capital: diversify into liquid assets between noon and half-three, stumble back upstairs to send a few things into motion, then head back out at 5:01pm to do it all over again.

Five bar empires ran the map, working as unofficial branch offices of the Stock Exchange, LIFFE, Lloyd's and every firm whose stationery could be turned into an expense claim. Between them they covered every shade of financial fauna, from sawdust-and-pewter die-hards to neon-cocktail trailblazers.

The Big Five: colosseums of the Square Mile

Five operators between them defined how the City drank between the Big Bang and the smoking ban.

The Evening Standard London Pub & Bar Guide 1999 named all five among its ten recommended theme bars for the capital.

1. Balls Brothers: the subterranean claret fortress

Balls Brothers, which dates itself to 1860, ran basement wine bars on a settled formula: keep the sunlight out, keep the claret flowing, and do not let anyone wear trainers.

Stepping into a Balls Brothers basement in the nineties was like tumbling into a Dickensian gentlemen's club that had collided with a rugby clubhouse. The decor was an unapologetic celebration of mahogany panelling, polished brass rails, framed prints of horses jumping hedges, and leather banquettes rubbed smooth by thousands of pinstriped trousers.

The menu was stubbornly British: cold roast beef, heavy slabs of English cheddar, game terrine, and bottles of Bordeaux broad enough in the shoulder to qualify as colleagues. The service was formal.

If you needed to discuss something commercially sensitive, celebrate a bonus, or simply disappear below street level until the afternoon became someone else's problem, you descended the stairs into Balls Brothers.

Then and now

  • Then: a sprawling City wine-bar network of 14 sites across the City and beyond in 1999, heavy on basements, claret and old-school corporate ritual.
  • Now: the original company went into administration in November 2010, and in March 2011 Novus Leisure bought 17 of its 19 bars; the name survives in a slimmer modern form.
  • The attempted pivot: in 2006 the group bought the Lewis & Clarke bars, a ground-floor, big-windowed format a long way from its own basements.

2. All Bar One: the daylight revolution

Bass launched All Bar One in 1994, following female-friendly chains such as Pitcher & Piano and Slug & Lettuce.

To modern eyes, All Bar One is just part of the furniture. But in the mid-nineties, it was a quiet gender revolution in plate glass and beechwood. Before its arrival, drinking in the City often meant one of two extremes: suffocating in an ancient, male-dominated pub that smelled of stale bitter and carpet, or hiding in a subterranean wine cave where the hierarchy had already taken the good table.

All Bar One smashed that dynamic with floor-to-ceiling windows, light wood, blackboard menus with legible fonts, communal tables, giant jars of marinated olives on the counter, and, critically, a design brief that made women feel welcome rather than tolerated. For the rising cadre of female analysts, traders and lawyers, it mattered. Suddenly, you could order a glass of Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc without needing an invitation to the old-boys' network.

It was modern, clean, visible from the street and un-intimidating, in a City still full of basements and closed doors.

Then and now

  • Then: Bass Leisure Retail's 1994 statement of intent, 20+ sites across London by 1999. Floor-to-ceiling windows, women welcome not tolerated, blackboard menus you could actually read. The Evening Standard Guide called it “the success story of the decade.”
  • Now: still trading, everywhere, no longer surprising.

3. Pitcher & Piano: cathedral cocktails and high-octane glamour

Where All Bar One took the clean slate, Pitcher & Piano took the civic architecture.

Their Cornhill branch, set inside the grand former Scottish Widows office at 28–31 Cornhill, with vaulted ceilings, columns and enough architectural self-confidence to make a junior broker stand up straighter, felt less like a public house and more like the set of an Andrew Lloyd Webber musical about leveraged finance.

Pitcher & Piano worked the whole City day. It could catch you at 8am for espresso when the screens already looked hostile, sell you something vaguely salad-shaped at lunch, and by 6pm transform into an ear-splitting carnival of chrome, glass, bottled lager and fluorescent cocktails.

Sea Breezes, Cosmopolitans, Woo Woos, silver buckets of beer: the nineties cocktail renaissance arrived wearing a shiny shirt and smelling faintly of CK One.

Then and now

  • Then: theatrical vanity dressed in civic architecture, all grand halls, chrome and Sea Breezes at 6pm. Two documented City sites (200 Bishopsgate and Crutched Friars) plus the Cornhill flagship in the old Scottish Widows office.
  • Now: the Bishopsgate site closed in April 2011 and was converted into a Boots pharmacy; the Crutched Friars bar later became the Bavarian Beerhouse and is now the Munich Cricket Club. The chain still trades under Marston's, quieter and slimmer, with a handful of sites nationally.

4. Corney & Barrow: the slick modern aristocracy

Corney & Barrow carried the pedigree of an old wine merchant and, from 1983, put it to work feeding and watering the newly minted across the Square Mile and Canary Wharf.

Where Balls Brothers was your grandfather's study, Corney & Barrow was the minimalist boardroom with a better cellar. Its City bars were lighter, cleaner and more corporate: built for serious business lunches, client entertaining, and the kind of bill that looked less alarming once it had been stapled to a meeting note.

You brought clients here to suggest that you understood vintage champagne, knew the difference between Left Bank and Right Bank, and had a company expense card.

Then and now

  • Then: a slick corporate wine-bar circuit of 10 sites across the City and beyond by 1999, from Broadgate Circle (with summer croquet on the terrace) to Eastcheap.
  • Now: the wine-bar division, eleven bars with ten under the Corney & Barrow name, was sold to Drake & Morgan in 2016; the old C&B fascia has gone from the bar map.
  • Important wrinkle: Drake & Morgan did not simply erase every physical site from scratch. Several locations survived under the new ownership. But the Corney & Barrow bar branding, wine-merchant identity and nineties corporate style were stripped away.
  • The sister chain: Coates, the more accessible offshoot C&B built out of its 1959 W. Coates acquisition, ran alongside the C&B bars through the same period. Its 45 London Wall room gets a century of drinking told in full below.

5. Davy's: sawdust, pewter and stubborn antiquity

While everyone else installed beech wood and halogen downlights, Davy's did not.

Operating under names like The Crusting Pipe, Boot & Flogger and Bung Hole Cellars, Davy's stood as the counter-weight to the modern style bar. Its aesthetic philosophy was aggressively Dickensian: candlelit vaults, brick arches, barrels for tables, sawdust underfoot, portly decanters, plates of Stilton, and the company's dark, mysteriously potent Old Wallop arriving in heavy pewter tankards.

It was a refuge for people who felt the twentieth century had been a terrible mistake and wanted to spend their lunch hour drinking in a theatrical version of the nineteenth.

Then and now

  • Then: sawdust, candles, vaulted cellars and Old Wallop, across 30+ sites in London by 1999, per the Evening Standard guide.
  • Now: still recognisably itself in several locations, even as the City around it has changed beyond recognition.
  • Still trading: see the Davy's essay for the full account.

Slug & Lettuce changed in the same period, though it was never a City wine bar. After Grosvenor Inns acquired Fast Forward in 1992, the chain was pushed deliberately away from the old pub model: “more of a bar, less of a pub”, younger, more contemporary, less blokey, aiming for an even male-female crowd. Sheila McKenzie, already associated with the female-friendly Pitcher & Piano formula, helped steer that shift.

By 1995 it was being pitched as a “contemporary English bar”, somewhere between a continental café and a classic pub. By 1997 it saw All Bar One and Pitcher & Piano as direct competitors.

The name survived into the Stonegate era.

The Fine Line belongs here as the brewer’s answer to all of it. Fuller’s opened the first one in Battersea in June 1998 and declined to call it a brand: a “concept”, they said, because the managers were left to run their own rooms. Three more followed by that September, five more in 1999, and by the summer of 2001 there were eight across London and a ninth in Bristol. Deep blue and purple sofas, pale polished wood, one cocktail list with room for local specials.

The Evening Standard went to Battersea that November and wrote that Fuller’s had taken “the basic principles of the All Bar One offering” and improved them. Within a few weeks it was one of the busiest rooms in the area.

The hiring says the same thing in a colder way. The man running Fuller’s bars division had been operations manager at Pitcher & Piano. The Fine Line’s own operations manager came from All Bar One. Other staff came from Slug & Lettuce and Henry’s Café-Bars.

Two of them are still trading in the Square Mile and neither is called the Fine Line any more. The Bow Churchyard café-bar opened in 1998 and is now The Saint. The Monument Street one opened in September 1999, carried the name for sixteen years, and became The Hydrant in 2015, with fire-brigade theming, craft keg and fifteen draught lines. Fuller’s still run both.

The anatomy of a 1990s session

It is difficult to convey to anyone working in the City today, where people track their REM sleep on an Oura ring and lunch is a Pret sandwich eaten over Microsoft Teams, just how central alcohol was to everyday financial business.

Much of the trade was at lunchtime: people leaving their desks at midday, ordering wine, and charging it to the firm.

The uniform, currency and pacing

  • The uniform: double-breasted suits with shoulders so padded you had to turn sideways through doors; contrast white collars; Albert Thurston silk braces, preferably scarlet; and a mobile phone roughly the size and weight of an everyday masonry brick.
  • The currency: the corporate expense account. If an invoice was not submitted on company paper, did lunch even happen?
  • The pacing: lager to cut the thirst, white wine with starters, red wine with the main event, then Sauternes or tawny port to settle the bill and gently vandalise the afternoon.

How a ghost is made

In PINtPOINT a ghost is a closed venue. Ghost Hunter puts them on the radar as purple blips. Tap one and you get the pub as it was, a Street View of what stands there now, the year it closed and its last known tap list, where we have them.

The question is no longer which basement to disappear into after noon. It is what is left.

If you threw on the red braces today and attempted the ultimate 1996 Square Mile wine-bar crawl, the map would be most of the way to useless. Some names survived. Some rooms survived without the names. Some became restaurants, cocktail bars, offices or development sites. A few simply vanished.

One address deserves to be told properly. 45 London Wall.

The unit ran as a W. Coates wine bar from the second half of the nineteenth century, one of two Coates sites in the City alongside 109 Old Broad Street. When Corney & Barrow bought W. Coates in 1959, the London Wall room became part of the wine-merchant estate that would later become the C&B bar chain proper.

By early October 1986 the room was open as Coates, billed as “the 21st century café.” Nigella Lawson reviewed it for The Spectator on 25 October, “just over a fortnight” after it opened. Tchaik Chassay did the design: granite, maple, merbau, steel and primrose; a bar shaped like a Fifties carpet; a teeming restaurant beyond; and an overhead Telerate screen giving live foreign-exchange and bond prices. Her verdict:

Here is a place made for the detritus of the Big Bang — in all its gruesome detail.

The lunch menu ran to £5 for two courses. The shop out front sold wine and “breakfast in a briefcase.” Champagne, Perrier, three courses and coffee for two came to £26 with the tip.

By the time of the Evening Standard London Pub & Bar Guide 1999, the same room was doing lunchtime pizza, Young's Ordinary and Scrumpy Jack on cask, karaoke on Wednesdays and Fridays and disco on Thursdays, and the guide relayed a researcher's line in the language of its time: “Essex nymphos by night and an easy place to pull on Thursdays.” Thirteen years separate the champagne-and-Perrier lunch from that sentence about the same address.

The address passed through the 2000s as Mehek, an award-winning Indian restaurant headed by Ashok Kumar, who had worked with Atul Kochhar. CAMRA records Mehek as closed in 2015.

Today, 45 London Wall is the Moorgate Post Office.

The room has a page of its own.

The City changes when you know what used to be there

Walk past a modern bar near Liverpool Street and it might just look like another after-work venue.

Knowing it was part of the old wine-bar circuit makes it a continuation, a replacement or a disguise. The City’s old drinking map has been built over, rebranded, thinned out and made respectable.

Ghosts confirmed in the 1999 book, pinned as a Curated Collection: Old Haunts of the Square Mile.
