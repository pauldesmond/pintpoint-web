Season of the Witch

The Green Man carvings are medieval, the pub name is centuries old, and the pagan reading only dates from 1939.

A week before Halloween, the pub has put up cobwebs. There is a plastic skeleton by the fruit machine, something orange on the pump clip and, outside, a face looking down through a mouthful of leaves.

His relatives peer out of the stonework at Exeter, Norwich and Southwell: faces sprouting foliage, oak leaves spilling from their mouths. The usual explanation is that he is a pagan god who survived the conversion, smuggled into the masonry by craftsmen who had not quite let go of the old religion.

He is not even an autumn figure. His best-known modern interpretation ties him to May Day. He is used at Halloween because he looks the part.

This is the first of three posts.

A new history under an old pub name

In 1939, Lady Raglan published “The Green Man in Church Architecture” in the journal Folklore. It began with a face on a chancel corbel at Llangwm, shown to her by the vicar, who suggested that the leaves coming from its mouth represented the spirit of inspiration. Raglan saw a Green Man instead.

She connected the church carvings with the figures on pub signs, Robin Hood and the leafy Jack-in-the-Green of May celebrations. Behind them she discerned the survival of pagan custom.

Her article helped establish the interpretation, although neither the name nor all those connections began in 1939. Raglan said she had been calling the Llangwm face a Green Man since 1931, and a 1932 letter to Folklore from a Miss Durham says of the same carving that “it is thought to be a ‘green man’”. What remains unproved is the leap from a leafy face to a surviving fertility cult.

The pub name was older still. Green-clad foresters and wild men had occupied the signs before the foliate head became their familiar replacement, a sequence David Barnett traced for Prospect.

So what do you actually drink?

Pumpkin beer has a genuine history.

In colonial America, pumpkin was something you could ferment when conventional brewing ingredients were scarce. A 1771 recipe reproduced by Colonial Williamsburg treats it much like an apple for cider: crush it, press the juice, boil, hop, ferment and cask. The historian Cindy Ott, who wrote a book on the pumpkin in American life, is quoted in DCist writing that “pumpkin ale and beer were economical substitutes for more popular malt drinks”.

Bill Owens helped revive pumpkin ale at Buffalo Bill’s Brewery in California during the 1980s. The brewery dates its version to 1986.

Modern pumpkin beers mostly taste of cinnamon, cloves and nutmeg.

Green hop beer is the one tied to the British harvest.

Most brewing hops are dried after picking. Green hop beers use them fresh, while the harvest is happening and before the undried cones deteriorate. Kent’s criteria require them to reach the brew within twelve hours.

By Halloween week, the main celebrations are behind us. The Spa Valley Railway’s festival, with its substantial green hop selection, finished last weekend. There may still be casks to find elsewhere.

Buxton’s Basic Witch is a 4.7% pumpkin spice latte porter, cinnamon and nutmeg and cloves over molasses and chocolate. Vault City’s Boo-Berry Pumpkin Spice Latte is an 8% sour doing the same thing with blueberries. Brew York’s Nightmare of Brew York is a 7.5% imperial milk stout built to taste of a Walnut Whip.

Before the pumpkin, the apple

Daniel Maclise attended a Halloween party in Blarney in 1832 and turned it into Snap Apple Night, exhibited at the Royal Academy the following year. The Metropolitan Museum’s account of a print after the painting records the gathering behind it.

The room is full of music, games and courtship. Apples are caught with the teeth; nuts burn in the grate to test romantic prospects. In the snap-apple game, apples and lit candles were fixed to a suspended, spinning frame. Getting a mouthful of fruit required judgement about which end was coming round next.

The painting shows Halloween as a night of eating, drinking and fortune-telling about marriage.

One cidery has put the pumpkin back. ACE of Sebastopol, founded by the man who spent years getting Fuller’s ESB into American bars before he made anything of his own, makes a pumpkin cider each autumn and calls it the world’s first. Apples and pumpkin, with cinnamon, cloves and allspice.

Even the lantern is a pub story

In a familiar version of the legend, he persuades the Devil to join him for a drink, then proposes a way to settle the bill: turn yourself into a coin. The Devil obliges. Jack pockets him beside a cross and negotiates his release.

Eventually Jack dies. Heaven will not have him; the Devil, bound by their bargain, will not take him. He is left to wander with a burning coal inside a hollowed-out turnip.

The turnip lantern is well documented. The National Museum of Ireland holds a plaster model of one collected in Donegal in 1943, described by its donor as the kind made locally around 1900. Its pinched, miserable face makes the modern pumpkin look positively hospitable.

The legend explains the lantern but is not evidence of where it came from.

The witch and the alewife

You will have met this one. Medieval alewives wore pointed hats so customers could find them at market, hung a broom outside to announce beer for sale, kept a cat for the mice in the grain and brewed in a great pot over a fire.

The story goes that the witch costume comes from alewives.

The historian Dr Christina Wade has examined the claim. Contemporary depictions of brewing women do not establish the supposed pointed-hat uniform, and the evidence does not support tracing the modern witch’s outfit to alewives. Nor were women expelled from the trade en masse through witchcraft accusations.

Women did much of the domestic and small-scale brewing. Their position weakened as production became more commercial, demanding money, equipment and access to opportunities women did not enjoy on equal terms.

The Green Man gets a part

The pub in The Wicker Man is the Green Man. Sergeant Howie walks into a place where the songs are familiar enough to be disconcerting and everyone seems to know something he does not.

Lord Summerisle’s Victorian grandfather encouraged the old gods while introducing new crops. The island’s customs are bound up with a landowner’s experiment in prosperity, maintained by people who have come to believe in them.

The film takes place around May Day, too.

For those of us raised on Shiver and Shake, Monster Fun and Rentaghost, the distance between dread and entertainment was never very great.

Pumpkin beer is fine; green hop and cider are the seasonal choices.
