The Geography of Sobriety

How London’s drinking culture was designed out of existence.

If you wanted to understand the old mechanics of London, you did not look at the architectural elevations of St Paul’s or the balance sheets of the Bank of England. You looked at the floors of its back-alley taverns.

For centuries, London’s working world was governed by a rich, inherited street ecology: a messy, accidental patchwork of medieval courtyards, narrow passageways, gaslit basement vaults and wood-panelled drinking rooms.

These rooms were where information moved and deals were made.

The usual explanation is compliance, HR, digital trading and wellness culture, squeezing the drinking out of the working week over thirty years.

There is a simpler one. The culture was made homeless by relocation.

Two working populations, the print journalists of Fleet Street and the financial operators of the Square Mile, were lifted out of the streets that had grown around them and put down somewhere built to a plan.

Wapping moved the presses. Canary Wharf moved a good share of the people. Neither took the rooms with them.

The two lost ecosystems

Fleet Street and the Square Mile each ran as a self-sustaining urban ecology, not only as an industry.

The Hack Machine: Fleet Street

Around the newspaper headquarters of Fleet Street ran an ink-stained web of dependency: printer’s devils in the mud, late-night copy boys, court reporters, hot-metal typesetters, tipsters, basement lunch counters, and drinking rooms keeping hours that suited the changing night shifts.

Once the office hierarchy had spilled into the bar, proprietors, editors, reporters, printers and mechanics could find themselves drinking side by side. Information travelled as gossip, overheard across the counter.

Val Guest shot part of The Day the Earth Caught Fire on Fleet Street in 1961, and said he “tried very hard to make it as ‘documentary’ as possible”. His reporters drink at Harry's Bar, which the BFI places in St Bride's Avenue, the side street alongside St Bride's Church, diagonally opposite the Express offices. Whether the bar ever existed, the BFI says, “is open to question”; the spot is now a brick wall covered in tiling.

The Broker Machine: the Square Mile

Further east, around Bank and Cornhill, the financial City ran on its own pattern: hidden courtyards, subterranean wine bars, grand banking halls and ancient alleyways.

It was an ecosystem of blue-button runners, Lloyd’s underwriters, old brokers who still knew their clients’ grandfathers, and rigid expense-account rituals. If Fleet Street ran on the frantic pressure of the deadline, the City ran on the mutual trust of the transaction.

Deals were discussed over long lunches. The mahogany basement vaults of Balls Brothers and the candlelit brick arches of Davy’s suited a culture that valued privacy and the unrecorded handshake.

The full inventory of that lost circuit is told in the companion essay. Twenty-eight closed City wine bars from the 1999 Evening Standard London Pub & Bar Guide are pinned on the map.

The eastern exodus

Then the coordinates moved. The papers left Fleet Street from 1986, and Canary Wharf was built from 1988 and grew through the 1990s and 2000s.

Wapping and the murder of Fleet Street

In January 1986 News International moved production of its papers, as the Marx Memorial Library puts it, “overnight from central London’s Fleet Street to a secretly-equipped and heavily guarded plant at Wapping”, a site The Independent describes as “surrounded by high walls topped with razor wire”. Within a few years the other papers had left Fleet Street too.

A journalist at The Sun or The Times went from a dense, centuries-old street with pubs on every side of the desk to an isolated industrial site in the docklands.

There were no alleyways to duck into, and no accidental encounters with rival reporters at the bar of the Cheshire Cheese or El Vino. The move took the presses and the writers out of the street at the same time.

The supporting cast of copy boys, local printers, independent tipsters and pub-borne informants went with them.

Canary Wharf and the sterile force

The City’s version was slower and larger: Canary Wharf.

Construction began in 1988 and the first buildings opened in 1991, on a privately owned estate on the Isle of Dogs. It answered a problem the Square Mile could not: medieval streets too narrow for modern infrastructure, and historic buildings badly suited to the open-plan trading floors global banks wanted.

In solving that, it designed out the conditions the old drinking culture had grown in.

Anatomy of a sterilised space

No rule was needed. The design did the work.

The Canary Wharf architectural filter

Private space, public manners

Unlike the public streets of the City, Canary Wharf is a privately owned estate. The dark corner where a broker could disappear for three hours gave way to bright plazas where every movement is visible.

The friction of height

In the old Square Mile, you were rarely more than two flights of stairs and a short cobblestone walk from a basement wine bar. In Canary Wharf, the casual pint becomes a tactical expedition through lifts, turnstiles and marble lobbies.

Mall-like uniformity

Commercial spaces were leased less to eccentric local landlords or historic wine merchants and more to reliable, high-volume operators. The drinking rooms became clean, bright, high-ceilinged and predictable: useful for corporate entertaining, poor for institutional lingering.

The absence of accident

Pubs worked as collision chambers: dense little rooms where gossip, trust, rumour, rivalry and opportunity ran into one another. Canary Wharf removed the collisions.

The medieval street grid generated accidental encounters. You turned a corner into a blind alley and met a contact, a rival or an old friend. Canary Wharf’s walkways are open and straight, with no inherited corners.

Survivor / Ghost / Re-skinned

A venue might still be trading, physically present but socially absent, closed under scaffolding, or alive as a different business. The status matters as much as the address.

El Vino is the test case. A Fleet Street wine bar patronised by journalists, pulled toward the legal district, and descended from the same wine-merchant trade as the City houses: it is tagged Border Crosser.

The Punch Tavern is another, a few doors from Ludgate Circus, named for Punch and close enough to the City’s western edge to sit on the hinge.

The Tipperary is tagged the same way. A Grade II-listed Fleet Street pub that claims to be London’s oldest Irish pub, it reopened in March 2024 after restoration works, then closed again in December 2024, according to CAMRA’s WhatPub; London Drinker, in January 2025, expected it back in 2027.

The legacy: double-exposed London

Ghost Hunter shows the city in layers: where you can drink now, and where London once did its business, swapped its rumours and performed its rituals.

Walk Fleet Street with the ghost layer switched on and the vanished press city starts to reappear: the old offices, the courts, the legendary pubs, the rooms where gossip became copy and copy became history. Walk the Square Mile the same way and another lost operating system appears beneath the glass towers: wine bars, merchant cellars, lunchtime fortresses and basements where the bill mattered less than the relationship it preserved.

Modern London is cleaner, healthier and less likely to make a decision after a bottle of claret. What went with the drinking was a form of accidental human infrastructure: the unplanned encounter, the inherited room, the landlord who knew too much, the back table where work and life were hard to tell apart.
