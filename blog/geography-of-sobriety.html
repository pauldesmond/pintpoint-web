The Geography of Sobriety

How the moves to Wapping and Canary Wharf changed where London's journalists and City workers drank.

If you wanted to understand the old mechanics of London, you had to forget the elevations of St Paul’s and the balance sheets of the Bank of England, and look at the floors of its back-alley taverns. For centuries, London’s working world ran on an inherited street life: a messy, accidental patchwork of medieval courtyards, narrow passageways, gaslit basement vaults and wood-panelled drinking rooms. It was where information changed hands and how people got by.

The standard narrative tells us that London eventually grew up and sobered up. We are told that corporate compliance, modern HR departments, digital trading and wellness culture slowly squeezed the legendary excess out of the working week. But nobody suddenly decided to prioritise their liver health, and nobody brought in prohibition. The old London drinking culture was made homeless by relocation.

Within a few short decades, London’s two greatest professional tribes, the print journalists of Fleet Street and the financial operators of the Square Mile, were lifted out of the alleys and courts they had grown up in and dropped behind a guarded wall at Wapping and into a privately owned estate at Canary Wharf.

Before the moves

Fleet Street

Fleet Street was a sprawling, ink-stained web of dependency: printer’s devils running through the mud, late-night copy boys, court reporters, hot-metal typesetters, tipsters, basement lunch counters and drinking dens that kept odd hours for the night shifts. Some held special licences for the small hours: the Hoop and Grapes on Farringdon Street, as A London Inheritance records, could open “between two and five in the morning for the convenience of printers who worked in nearby Fleet Street”.

The deadline was the absolute dictator of the clock, and the pub was the decompression chamber.

Peter Corrigan, writing in the Observer’s farewell supplement on 6 March 1988 as the paper left for Battersea, listed what the office local actually did: “an oasis, intensive care unit, ego massage parlour, moneylender, bureau de change, job centre”. A reporter sacked in the morning knew which pubs to trawl for a new job that lunchtime, and an editor set on poaching a rival’s star performer could arrange for him to be ambushed at the hour his resistance would be lowest.

Printers and journalists drank in the same pubs. Peter McKay, quoted by the Guardian from his Fleet Street obituary in the Independent, remembered Fleet Street as “a seething mass of printers, advertisers and journalists, drinking and punching each other every night, all night”. They did not always share a room. Ian Aitken, writing in the London Review of Books, remembered the Kings and Keys standing halfway between the Daily Express and the Daily Telegraph and serving as the office pub for both, with a spit-and-sawdust front bar where “genuine inky printers sank pints of bitter” and only one journalist, with his guests, allowed into it: Arthur Christiansen, the Express editor. Information in those bars meant gossip, overheard whispers across sticky counters, and late-night scoops bought for the price of a double Scotch.

Not every colleague wanted to drink with every other, so each office pub carried satellites for the overspill. Corrigan has most of the Daily Mail staff in the Harrow in Hanging Sword Lane, where Alan Drew was retiring after forty-four years behind the bar, and the rest in the White Swan, known to all as the Mucky Duck, or the Welsh Harp, which had housed a group of Mail men who called themselves the Fingertip Club after the way they were hanging onto their jobs. The Mirror’s own pub was the White Hart, a name by then so thoroughly eclipsed by the Stab in the Back that the nickname had replaced it.

Val Guest shot part of The Day the Earth Caught Fire on Fleet Street in 1961, and said he “tried very hard to make it as ‘documentary’ as possible”. His reporters drink at Harry's Bar, which the BFI places in St Bride's Avenue, the side street alongside St Bride's Church, diagonally opposite the Express offices. Whether the bar ever existed, the BFI says, “is open to question”, and the spot is now a brick wall covered in tiling. Half a century later, reporters kept looking anyway.

El Vino is a Fleet Street wine bar long patronised by journalists, still pulled toward the legal district, and descended from a wine-merchant culture that also belongs to the City, and it has a bar-length history of who was allowed to stand at the counter and who controlled the ritual. The Punch Tavern, a few doors from Ludgate Circus, is associated with Punch magazine and the vanished comic press world, but sits close enough to the City’s western edge to feel like a hinge rather than a museum piece.

The pubs and El Vino alike turned everyone out between three and five in the afternoon, and the gap filled with clubs. One of them was the City Golf Club, a members-only club a few yards off Fleet Street. It had a screen with a fairway projected on it so members could drive a ball and be told how far it would have gone. The curious came in droves and were distracted by the clinking of glasses from the club bar. Cobwebs appeared on the screen, and it was eventually cleared out to make room for another bar. By the time Corrigan wrote, the club had gone altogether and its afternoon trade had moved to Scribes.

The Square Mile

Further east, around Bank and Cornhill, the Square Mile was a tight labyrinth of hidden courtyards, subterranean wine bars, grand banking halls and ancient alleyways.

It was a world of Stock Exchange blue-button runners, Lloyd’s underwriters, old brokers who still knew their clients’ grandfathers, and rigid expense-account rituals. If Fleet Street ran on the frantic pressure of the deadline, the City ran on the mutual trust of the transaction.

Lloyd’s of London worked by standing up. Brokers carried their slips across the underwriting room at One Lime Street and queued at the boxes, and the pubs round Leadenhall Market were where the rest of it happened.

Christopher Fildes watched the old arrangement end. Writing in the Spectator on 25 October 1986, the week before the Big Bang, he had his friend the stockjobber “closed his book, turned his back on his pitch, and walked with me off the Stock Exchange floor, down Throgmorton Street and into Bill Bentley’s fish house. We raised our glasses of Montrachet to the last of the good old days.” On the Monday the man would be at a work-station, one of hundreds in what Fildes called a huge carpeted financial factory.

The habits outlasted the floor. A former Lloyd’s broker, writing in the Evening Standard in 2019, remembered one o’clock as the hour when “most brokers and underwriters would disappear for two hours for a long, boozy meal”, and one underwriter who “would write the risks he took in the afternoon in a red pen, so he knew to check them when he was sober in the morning”. John McCormack, looking back on forty years in the market, remembered how you found a colleague before mobile telephones: the Lloyd’s caller, or “a post-lunch walk around one of the City pubs that the particular colleague was known to visit”.

The futures traders kept their own timetable. Kelvin Robertson told Euromoney in 2001 that “at 11.30 on the dot all the traders got on the escalator to the mezzanine bar for a drink”, then went for a long lunch, and that “the minimum drinking was 5.15 till 8”. His colleague Doug Fisher put the purpose of it plainly: “You’d bawl your head off at someone for two hours, then in the evening you’d go off with him for a drink.”

Deals were hammered out on the back of napkins over multi-course liquid lunches. The dark, mahogany basement vaults of Balls Brothers or the candlelit brick arches of Davy’s provided the perfect physical camouflage for a culture that thrived on privacy and unrecorded handshakes.

An average lunch in the late 1960s, the former banker David Buik told the Guardian, meant “a large gin and tonic, half a bottle of claret and three Grand Marniers. But everybody did that.”

The moves east

The papers left Fleet Street from 1986, and from 1991, the Guardian’s timeline records, banks including Morgan Stanley and Credit Suisse First Boston moved to Canary Wharf, with Barclays leaving Lombard Street in 2005.

Wapping, 1986

The first blow fell with brutal, surgical precision. Rupert Murdoch broke the printing unions and, with them, the physical geography of British journalism.

In January 1986 News International moved production of its papers, as the Marx Memorial Library puts it, “overnight from central London’s Fleet Street to a secretly-equipped and heavily guarded plant at Wapping”, a site The Independent describes as “surrounded by high walls topped with razor wire”.

Journalists at The Sun moved from Bouverie Street, off Fleet Street, to the walled plant, and The Times, Press Gazette records, came from Gray’s Inn Road. A Sun reporter went from a dense, centuries-old urban fabric where ten different pubs sat within a thirty-second sprint of the desk to an isolated industrial site surrounded by bleak docklands.

There were no historic alleyways to duck into. There were no accidental encounters with rival reporters at the bar of the Cheshire Cheese or El Vino. The move to Wapping severed the connection between the writer and the street.

By April 1989, UPI reported, “a 287-year tradition of journalists packed onto a half-mile stretch of Fleet Street ended with the recent departure of the Daily and Sunday Express”. Other publishers moved to Canary Wharf or Southwark, and Reuters, at No. 85, was the last major news outlet to leave, in 2005.

The print workers were not taken to Wapping at all: as the Marx Memorial Library records, “five and a half thousand striking workers were sacked”, and over the next year the print unions picketed the plant.

The publicans had no such transfer. Corrigan, writing two years after Wapping, named the houses it emptied and the ones going the same way as the rest of the Street followed: the Tipperary, the First Edition, the Printers Pie, the Coach and Horses, and Ye Olde Cock, which had been the haunt of the News of the World sports desk. The Telegraph had vacated the King and Keys and The Times the Packenham. Too many thousands had been left jobless by the exodus for much sympathy to reach the landlords, but, he wrote, imagine keeping a house frequented by The Sun and losing overnight “the most lucrative thirsts in Britain”.

Ian Aitken wrote in 1991 that the Fleet Street pubs “now stand relatively empty”, and that many of the new newspaper offices “exist in wastelands where there are few pubs within reasonable walking distance”. The old hack engine had been pulled out of its chassis.

Canary Wharf

While Fleet Street was being abruptly decapitated, the City of London was preparing for a slower, more expansive sterilisation: the rise of Canary Wharf.

Construction began in 1988 and the first buildings opened in 1991, on a privately owned estate on the Isle of Dogs rather than the public streets of the City. It was laid out, its master planners SOM record, with “tree-lined urban boulevards” catering “primarily to pedestrians”. It is heavily patrolled and intensely visible. The dark corner where a broker could disappear for a three-hour session was replaced by bright plazas where every movement feels observable.

After the 1986 Big Bang, The Conversation explains, banks wanted large open-plan trading floors that the City’s space and planning restrictions made hard to build, and Canary Wharf’s Enterprise Zone status “removed virtually all planning constraints”. In solving the spatial problem, Canary Wharf designed out the conditions that had made the old corporate drinking culture possible.

The Guardian walked it in March 1999 and called it “the soulless development, sprawling across 81 acres”, with “no history and little identity” and a wind “sculpted by the sterile blocks”. In 1991, when the tower was built, there had been “none of the restaurants and bars that are vital to business”. By 1999 there were, and Davy’s would follow the banks east too. The paper found a banker perfectly content in the Corney & Barrow bar overlooking the dock, saying he liked it there. What the place did not have, it decided, was “sufficient cachet for a really good lunch”.

Canary Wharf did not explicitly ban the long lunch, nor did it post signs prohibiting daytime champagne. It did not have to. The move broke up the familiar routes between desk, pub and contact. Whatever drinking continued, it no longer happened in the same network of rooms.

Whatever started it, the move east finished it for the ones who went. In the old Square Mile, you were rarely more than two flights of stairs and a short cobblestone walk from a basement wine bar. In Canary Wharf, the casual pint becomes a tactical expedition through lifts, turnstiles and marble lobbies.

Pubs were collision chambers: dense little rooms where gossip, trust, rumour, rivalry and opportunity smashed into one another until something new appeared. The medieval street grid was a machine for generating accidental encounters. You turned a corner into a blind alley and met a contact, rival or old friend. Canary Wharf has no inherited corners, and nowhere for old, inefficient rituals to hide. It removed the collisions, and London never found its Higgs boson.

What was left behind

Lloyd’s was not first. In May 1999 Halifax stopped two hundred treasury staff on Old Broad Street drinking at lunchtime, thought to be the first ban of its kind in the City, and the Independent put it down to the arrival of the big Wall Street banks and “the American work ethic, which frowns on mid-day drinking”. A banker told the paper: “Alcohol is the oil which lubricates the City’s wheels. I have never heard of anybody trying to ban it.”

Canary Wharf never had to write the rule down. Lloyd’s of London, still on Lime Street, did. In February 2017 it stopped drinking between nine and five on pain of gross misconduct, in a memo to its 800 staff that was leaked to the Evening Standard. Behind it was an analysis of two years of grievance and disciplinary cases in which “roughly half” turned out to involve alcohol, and the memo conceded that the London market “historically had a reputation for daytime drinking, but that has been changing”. It reached only the staff Lloyd’s employed directly, not the brokers and underwriters who do business there, and in the pubs round Leadenhall Market two of them laughed at it. By one o’clock on the Friday after, the terraces outside were full, mostly men, almost every one holding a pint of lager. Lloyd’s went further afterwards, barring anyone from entering the market under the influence, and One Under Lime, the bar below the building, stopped serving before five.

Buik put the change down to the reforms of the 1980s rather than to any building: the big bang, the leisurely trading banks swallowed by large international corporations, and a market competitive enough that in fifteen years at BCG he could not remember a broker leaving his desk between seven and five. Richard Kingdon of City Beacon, an alcohol counselling service for the Square Mile, told the paper the culture had not gone anywhere. “There’s definitely a drinking culture, it’s just much more prevalent in the evenings now than during the day.”

Then the room closed. Lloyd’s shut the underwriting room at four o’clock on Thursday 19 March 2020, and its chief executive John Neal gave the numbers behind the decision: about 5,000 people came through on a typical day, 1,000 on the Monday of that week and under 200 on the 18th. He said it had been taken “with a heavy heart”. The market kept trading on its electronic platform, which had been stress-tested the Friday before by shutting the room to see whether it could.

The room reopened on 1 September at 45 per cent capacity, with clear screens on the underwriting boxes and a rota by class of business, and a virtual room was launched beside it so that brokers and underwriters could meet online in much the same way.

What came back was a shorter week. Workers in central London now attend the office 2.7 days a week against 3.9 before the pandemic, and the Corporation’s own counts for 2024/25 give average footfall as 329,000 on Mondays, 460,000 on Thursdays and 310,000 on Fridays. Centre for Cities put Friday’s share of city-centre pub and bar spending at 25 per cent in 2019 and under a fifth by 2024. Lloyd’s chairman Bruce Carnegie-Brown called Friday attendance a lost cause.

Some of the places did not survive the wait. One in seven of the City’s pubs and restaurants closed between March 2020 and June 2022, a fall of 14 per cent against 10.5 across London. Simpson’s Tavern on Cornhill had its locks changed by its landlord that October over rent arrears of £336,634.16 built up through the pandemic.

Neither did the emptying wait for the pandemic, nor finish with it. The Independent found the pubs and champagne bars along Cannon Street “noticeably less crowded” in April 1999, the buildings still full, the trading gone electronic. And in May 2026 the Fence found the Lamb Tavern in Leadenhall Market bouncing on a wet Monday, and busier still at lunchtime on the Tuesday.

The City kept its pubs, and is building more of them. The Corporation said in December 2023 that it was negotiating new and restored houses into the developments going up, to draw workers and visitors back after the pandemic, with Transport for London’s journey figures nearing pre-pandemic levels. The Still and Star is to be moved to a prominent position near the old Aldgate gate, the Salisbury Arms rebuilt over several storeys on Salisbury Square, St Brides Tavern given a longer frontage, the White Swan in Fetter Lane re-sited on a corner in blue faience, and the King’s Arms taken out of a 1960s office block and faced in red. The aim, its planning chairman said, is a Square Mile that is “a 7-day-a-week visitor destination”.

What it did not keep were the cellars. Twenty-eight of the City wine bars listed in the 1999 Evening Standard guide have closed.

The Tipperary

The Tipperary at 66 Fleet Street is Grade II listed and claimed, on CAMRA’s WhatPub, as London’s first Irish pub. It reopened after restoration and closed again on 2 December 2024. WhatPub records it as closed long term, future uncertain, and being refurbished, probably for at least two years.

Ghost Hunter

PINtPOINT’s Ghost Hunter layer puts the closed pubs on the radar as purple blips. Tap one and you get a Street View of what stands there now, historical photographs where we have them, the year it closed and its last known tap list.

Walk Fleet Street with the ghost layer switched on and the pubs that served the press city light up. Walk the Square Mile the same way and another lost city appears beneath the glass towers: wine bars, merchant cellars and basements where the bill mattered less than the relationship it preserved.

UK workers, City A.M. reported, “monitor their drinking far more carefully than they did 30 years ago”.

Modern London is cleaner, healthier and probably less likely to make catastrophic decisions after a bottle of claret. That is not nothing. But the old city lost more than alcohol. It lost a particular kind of accident: the unplanned encounter, the inherited room, the landlord who knew too much, the back table where work and life became indistinguishable.
