The Geography of Sobriety

How the moves to Wapping and Canary Wharf changed where London's journalists and City workers drank.

Fleet Street and the City were full of courtyards, narrow passages, basement bars and panelled drinking rooms.

This essay is about one cause: both industries left the old streets.

Wapping took the presses and Canary Wharf took a good share of the people. The buildings stayed where they were.

Fleet Street and the City before the moves

Fleet Street

The papers employed copy boys, court reporters, hot-metal typesetters and tipsters, and pubs and lunch counters around them served the day shifts. The street was long noted for its taverns and coffeehouses, and journalists met in the pubs to collect stories.

Val Guest shot part of The Day the Earth Caught Fire on Fleet Street in 1961, and said he “tried very hard to make it as ‘documentary’ as possible”. His reporters drink at Harry's Bar, which the BFI places in St Bride's Avenue, the side street alongside St Bride's Church, diagonally opposite the Express offices. Whether the bar ever existed, the BFI says, “is open to question”; the spot is now a brick wall covered in tiling.

The Square Mile

Further east, around Bank and Cornhill, the City had its own courtyards, basement wine bars and banking halls.

It employed Stock Exchange blue-button runners, Lloyd's underwriters and brokers on expense accounts.

Deals were discussed over long lunches. Balls Brothers and Davy’s ran wine bars in City basements and cellars.

The companion essay goes through the City’s wine bars in detail. Twenty-eight closed City wine bars from the 1999 Evening Standard London Pub & Bar Guide are pinned on the map.

The moves east

The papers left Fleet Street from 1986, and Canary Wharf was built from 1988 and grew through the 1990s and 2000s.

Wapping, 1986

In January 1986 News International moved production of its papers, as the Marx Memorial Library puts it, “overnight from central London’s Fleet Street to a secretly-equipped and heavily guarded plant at Wapping”, a site The Independent describes as “surrounded by high walls topped with razor wire”.

Journalists at The Sun and The Times moved from a street lined with pubs to a walled plant in Wapping.

The Cheshire Cheese and El Vino, where Fleet Street journalists had drunk, were now a long way off.

The rest of the industry took nearly twenty years to follow. Other publishers moved to Canary Wharf or Southwark, and Reuters, at No. 85, was the last major news outlet to leave, in 2005.

The supporting cast went too, though not in the same way: the copy boys, independent tipsters and pub-borne informants had nothing left to gather round, and the print workers were not taken to Wapping at all. They were dismissed, and picketed it for a year.

Canary Wharf

Construction began in 1988 and the first buildings opened in 1991, on a privately owned estate on the Isle of Dogs.

Banks went east for space the City could not give them. Victoria Barnes, Lucy Newton and Peter Scott, writing in The Conversation, put the move down to space and planning restrictions on expanding City buildings, as electronic trading arrived from the 1980s. Morgan Stanley, Credit Suisse, First Boston and Citigroup were the first tenants. HSBC followed in 2002, and Barclays left Lombard Street in 2005, the last major UK retail bank to go.

How Canary Wharf differed

Nothing was banned. There was simply nowhere to drink.

Canary Wharf and the City compared

A private estate

Unlike the public streets of the City, Canary Wharf is a privately owned estate. It was laid out with wide, straight walkways.

Towers

At Canary Wharf, going for a drink meant taking the lift down a tower and going out through the security barriers.

One that is still shut

The Tipperary at 66 Fleet Street is Grade II listed and claimed, on CAMRA’s WhatPub, as London’s first Irish pub. It reopened after restoration and closed again on 2 December 2024. WhatPub records it as closed long term and future uncertain, being refurbished probably until 2027.

Ghost Hunter

PINtPOINT’s Ghost Hunter layer puts the closed pubs on the radar as purple blips. Tap one and you get a Street View of what stands there now, historical photographs where we have them, the year it closed and its last known tap list.

Walk Fleet Street with the ghost layer switched on and the pubs that served the press city reappear, one purple blip at a time. Walk the Square Mile the same way and the wine bars, merchant cellars and basements come back the same way.

Modern London drinks less at lunchtime.
