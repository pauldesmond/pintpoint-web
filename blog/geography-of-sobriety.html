The Geography of Sobriety

How the moves to Wapping and Canary Wharf changed where London's journalists and City workers drank.

Fleet Street and the City were full of courtyards, narrow passages, basement bars and panelled drinking rooms.

This essay is about one cause: both industries left the old streets.

Fleet Street and the City before the moves

Fleet Street

The papers employed copy boys, court reporters, hot-metal typesetters and tipsters, and pubs and lunch counters around them served the day shifts. Some held special licences for the small hours: the Hoop and Grapes on Farringdon Street, as A London Inheritance records, could open “between two and five in the morning for the convenience of printers who worked in nearby Fleet Street”.

Printers and journalists drank in the same pubs. Peter McKay, quoted by the Guardian from his Fleet Street obituary in the Independent, remembered Fleet Street as “a seething mass of printers, advertisers and journalists, drinking and punching each other every night, all night”. They did not always share a room. Ian Aitken, writing in the London Review of Books, remembered the Kings and Keys standing halfway between the Daily Express and the Daily Telegraph and serving as the office pub for both, with a spit-and-sawdust front bar where “genuine inky printers sank pints of bitter” and only one journalist, with his guests, allowed into it: Arthur Christiansen, the Express editor.

Val Guest shot part of The Day the Earth Caught Fire on Fleet Street in 1961, and said he “tried very hard to make it as ‘documentary’ as possible”. His reporters drink at Harry's Bar, which the BFI places in St Bride's Avenue, the side street alongside St Bride's Church, diagonally opposite the Express offices. Whether the bar ever existed, the BFI says, “is open to question”; the spot is now a brick wall covered in tiling.

The Square Mile

Further east, around Bank and Cornhill, the City had its own courtyards, basement wine bars and banking halls.

It employed Stock Exchange blue-button runners, Lloyd's underwriters and brokers on expense accounts.

Deals were discussed over long lunches. Balls Brothers and Davy’s ran wine bars in City basements and cellars.

The companion essay goes through the City’s wine bars in detail. Old Haunts of the Square Mile collects the closed City wine bars from the 1999 Evening Standard London Pub & Bar Guide.

The moves east

The papers left Fleet Street from 1986, and from 1991, the Guardian’s timeline records, banks including Morgan Stanley and Credit Suisse First Boston moved to Canary Wharf, with Barclays leaving Lombard Street in 2005.

Wapping, 1986

In January 1986 News International moved production of its papers, as the Marx Memorial Library puts it, “overnight from central London’s Fleet Street to a secretly-equipped and heavily guarded plant at Wapping”, a site The Independent describes as “surrounded by high walls topped with razor wire”.

Journalists at The Sun moved from Bouverie Street, off Fleet Street, to the walled plant; The Times, Press Gazette records, came from Gray’s Inn Road.

The Cheshire Cheese and El Vino, where Fleet Street journalists had drunk, were now a long way off.

By April 1989, UPI reported, “a 287-year tradition of journalists packed onto a half-mile stretch of Fleet Street ended with the recent departure of the Daily and Sunday Express”. Other publishers moved to Canary Wharf or Southwark, and Reuters, at No. 85, was the last major news outlet to leave, in 2005.

The print workers were not taken to Wapping at all: as the Marx Memorial Library records, “five and a half thousand striking workers were sacked”, and over the next year the print unions picketed the plant.

Ian Aitken wrote in 1991 that the Fleet Street pubs “now stand relatively empty”, and that many of the new newspaper offices “exist in wastelands where there are few pubs within reasonable walking distance”.

Canary Wharf

Construction began in 1988 and the first buildings opened in 1991, on a privately owned estate on the Isle of Dogs rather than the public streets of the City. It was laid out, its master planners SOM record, with “tree-lined urban boulevards” catering “primarily to pedestrians”.

After the 1986 Big Bang, The Conversation explains, banks wanted large open-plan trading floors that the City’s space and planning restrictions made hard to build, and Canary Wharf’s Enterprise Zone status “removed virtually all planning constraints”.

The Tipperary

The Tipperary at 66 Fleet Street is Grade II listed and claimed, on CAMRA’s WhatPub, as London’s first Irish pub. It reopened after restoration and closed again on 2 December 2024. WhatPub records it as closed long term, future uncertain, and being refurbished, probably for at least two years.

Ghost Hunter

PINtPOINT’s Ghost Hunter layer puts the closed pubs on the radar as purple blips. Tap one and you get a Street View of what stands there now, historical photographs where we have them, the year it closed and its last known tap list.

Walk Fleet Street with the ghost layer switched on and the pubs that served the press city show as purple blips. Walk the Square Mile and the wine bars and merchant cellars do the same.

UK workers, City A.M. reported, “monitor their drinking far more carefully than they did 30 years ago”.
