The Geography of Sobriety

How the moves to Wapping and Canary Wharf changed where London's journalists and City workers drank.

If you wanted to understand the old mechanics of London, you had to forget the elevations of St Paul’s and the balance sheets of the Bank of England, and look at the floors of its back-alley taverns. For centuries, London’s working world ran on an inherited street life: a messy, accidental patchwork of medieval courtyards, narrow passageways, gaslit basement vaults and wood-panelled drinking rooms. It was where information changed hands and how people got by.

The standard narrative tells us that London eventually grew up and sobered up. We are told that corporate compliance, modern HR departments, digital trading and wellness culture slowly squeezed the legendary excess out of the working week. But nobody suddenly decided to prioritise their liver health, and nobody brought in prohibition. The old London drinking culture was made homeless by relocation.

Within a few short decades, London’s two greatest professional tribes, the print journalists of Fleet Street and the financial operators of the Square Mile, were lifted out of their chaotic, organic street habitats and dropped into sterile, controlled corporate spaces.

Fleet Street and the City before the moves

Fleet Street

Fleet Street was a sprawling, ink-stained web of dependency: printer’s devils running through the mud, late-night copy boys, court reporters, hot-metal typesetters, tipsters, basement lunch counters and drinking dens that kept odd hours for the night shifts. Some held special licences for the small hours: the Hoop and Grapes on Farringdon Street, as A London Inheritance records, could open “between two and five in the morning for the convenience of printers who worked in nearby Fleet Street”.

The deadline was the absolute dictator of the clock, and the pub was the decompression chamber.

Printers and journalists drank in the same pubs. Peter McKay, quoted by the Guardian from his Fleet Street obituary in the Independent, remembered Fleet Street as “a seething mass of printers, advertisers and journalists, drinking and punching each other every night, all night”. They did not always share a room. Ian Aitken, writing in the London Review of Books, remembered the Kings and Keys standing halfway between the Daily Express and the Daily Telegraph and serving as the office pub for both, with a spit-and-sawdust front bar where “genuine inky printers sank pints of bitter” and only one journalist, with his guests, allowed into it: Arthur Christiansen, the Express editor. Information in those bars meant gossip, overheard whispers across sticky counters, and late-night scoops bought for the price of a double Scotch.

Val Guest shot part of The Day the Earth Caught Fire on Fleet Street in 1961, and said he “tried very hard to make it as ‘documentary’ as possible”. His reporters drink at Harry's Bar, which the BFI places in St Bride's Avenue, the side street alongside St Bride's Church, diagonally opposite the Express offices. Whether the bar ever existed, the BFI says, “is open to question”, and the spot is now a brick wall covered in tiling. Half a century later, reporters kept looking anyway.

El Vino is a Fleet Street wine bar long patronised by journalists, still pulled toward the legal district, and descended from a wine-merchant culture that also belongs to the City, and it has a bar-length history of who was allowed to stand at the counter and who controlled the ritual. The Punch Tavern, a few doors from Ludgate Circus, is associated with Punch magazine and the vanished comic press world, but sits close enough to the City’s western edge to feel like a hinge rather than a museum piece.

The Square Mile

Further east, around Bank and Cornhill, the Square Mile was a tight labyrinth of hidden courtyards, subterranean wine bars, grand banking halls and ancient alleyways.

It was a world of Stock Exchange blue-button runners, Lloyd’s underwriters, old brokers who still knew their clients’ grandfathers, and rigid expense-account rituals. If Fleet Street ran on the frantic pressure of the deadline, the City ran on the mutual trust of the transaction.

Deals were hammered out on the back of napkins over multi-course liquid lunches. The dark, mahogany basement vaults of Balls Brothers or the candlelit brick arches of Davy’s provided the perfect physical camouflage for a culture that thrived on privacy and unrecorded handshakes.

The companion essay goes through the City’s wine bars in detail. Old Haunts of the Square Mile collects the closed City wine bars from the 1999 Evening Standard London Pub & Bar Guide.

The moves east

The papers left Fleet Street from 1986, and from 1991, the Guardian’s timeline records, banks including Morgan Stanley and Credit Suisse First Boston moved to Canary Wharf, with Barclays leaving Lombard Street in 2005.

Wapping, 1986

The first blow fell with brutal, surgical precision. Rupert Murdoch broke the printing unions and, with them, the physical geography of British journalism.

In January 1986 News International moved production of its papers, as the Marx Memorial Library puts it, “overnight from central London’s Fleet Street to a secretly-equipped and heavily guarded plant at Wapping”, a site The Independent describes as “surrounded by high walls topped with razor wire”.

Journalists at The Sun moved from Bouverie Street, off Fleet Street, to the walled plant, and The Times, Press Gazette records, came from Gray’s Inn Road. A Sun reporter went from a dense, centuries-old urban fabric where ten different pubs sat within a thirty-second sprint of the desk to an isolated industrial site surrounded by bleak docklands.

There were no historic alleyways to duck into. There were no accidental encounters with rival reporters at the bar of the Cheshire Cheese or El Vino. The move to Wapping severed the connection between the writer and the street.

By April 1989, UPI reported, “a 287-year tradition of journalists packed onto a half-mile stretch of Fleet Street ended with the recent departure of the Daily and Sunday Express”. Other publishers moved to Canary Wharf or Southwark, and Reuters, at No. 85, was the last major news outlet to leave, in 2005.

The print workers were not taken to Wapping at all: as the Marx Memorial Library records, “five and a half thousand striking workers were sacked”, and over the next year the print unions picketed the plant.

Ian Aitken wrote in 1991 that the Fleet Street pubs “now stand relatively empty”, and that many of the new newspaper offices “exist in wastelands where there are few pubs within reasonable walking distance”. The old hack engine had been pulled out of its chassis.

Canary Wharf

While Fleet Street was being abruptly decapitated, the City of London was preparing for a slower, more expansive sterilisation: the rise of Canary Wharf.

Construction began in 1988 and the first buildings opened in 1991, on a privately owned estate on the Isle of Dogs rather than the public streets of the City. It was laid out, its master planners SOM record, with “tree-lined urban boulevards” catering “primarily to pedestrians”. It is heavily patrolled and intensely visible. The dark corner where a broker could disappear for a three-hour session was replaced by bright plazas where every movement feels observable.

After the 1986 Big Bang, The Conversation explains, banks wanted large open-plan trading floors that the City’s space and planning restrictions made hard to build, and Canary Wharf’s Enterprise Zone status “removed virtually all planning constraints”. In solving the spatial problem, Canary Wharf designed out the conditions that had made the old corporate drinking culture possible.

In the old Square Mile, you were rarely more than two flights of stairs and a short cobblestone walk from a basement wine bar. In Canary Wharf, the casual pint becomes a tactical expedition through lifts, turnstiles and marble lobbies.

Canary Wharf did not explicitly ban the long lunch, nor did it post signs prohibiting daytime champagne. It did not have to. It simply built a world where those habits became structurally impossible to maintain.

Pubs were collision chambers: dense little rooms where gossip, trust, rumour, rivalry and opportunity smashed into one another until something new appeared. The medieval street grid was a machine for generating accidental encounters. You turned a corner into a blind alley and met a contact, rival or old friend. Canary Wharf has no inherited corners, and nowhere for old, inefficient rituals to hide. It removed the collisions, and London never found its Higgs boson.

The Tipperary

The Tipperary at 66 Fleet Street is Grade II listed and claimed, on CAMRA’s WhatPub, as London’s first Irish pub. It reopened after restoration and closed again on 2 December 2024. WhatPub records it as closed long term, future uncertain, and being refurbished, probably for at least two years.

Ghost Hunter

PINtPOINT’s Ghost Hunter layer puts the closed pubs on the radar as purple blips. Tap one and you get a Street View of what stands there now, historical photographs where we have them, the year it closed and its last known tap list.

Walk Fleet Street with the ghost layer switched on and the pubs that served the press city light up. Walk the Square Mile the same way and another lost city appears beneath the glass towers: wine bars, merchant cellars and basements where the bill mattered less than the relationship it preserved.

UK workers, City A.M. reported, “monitor their drinking far more carefully than they did 30 years ago”.

Modern London is cleaner, healthier and probably less likely to make catastrophic decisions after a bottle of claret. That is not nothing. But the old city lost more than alcohol. It lost the unplanned encounter, the inherited room, the landlord who knew too much, the back table where work and life became indistinguishable.
