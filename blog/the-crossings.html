Crossing the Pond

Five essays on the beer traffic between Britain and America, and the Londoner who moved a good deal of it.

Beer crossed the Atlantic in both directions and the same name keeps appearing on the manifest. Jeffrey House sold Fuller’s ESB to America, watched American brewers take more from it than a good pint, lost the agency in 1990, and started making cider in Sonoma instead. In between, a London yeast went west, and an October 1975 tour of English breweries went home in two of Anchor’s beers. Gordon A. Ponce named the shape of it, the Transatlantic Beer Bridge, writing at The Craft Beer Finder on House’s research.

One · Fuller’s ESB in America

The first pour was at the College Inn in Seattle on 3 November 1987, and the argument that got it there was with the brewery, not the market. Fuller’s head brewer did not want ESB bottled, so America got it on draught. John Keeling says ESB is the beer that started John Hall, who wanted Goose Island to be Chicago’s Fuller’s.

Two · Why Firestone Walker chose Fuller’s yeast

A London strain went into a Californian fermenting tank and stayed there. David Walker says they picked it over the cleaner Chico yeast because it made the beer they liked best, and Chiswick had sent yeast west before, to a Canadian brewery in 1982.

Three · A Cider of His Own

An importer gets about ten years with somebody else’s brand. It happened to House twice, and the second time he stopped importing and built ACE in Sonoma, out of a pack of cards, a building the Duttons put up for him and an order he could not yet fill.

Four · Anchor Brewing’s British heritage

Fritz Maytag came to England in 1975 and went home with British ales in mind, Timothy Taylor’s Landlord among them.

Five · The Return Crossing

Anchor’s Liberty Ale reached a British handpump at the White Horse in 1992. From Pale Rider in Sheffield to Jaipur at Thornbridge, here’s what British brewers made of American hops.
