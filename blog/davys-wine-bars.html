Davy's: a century and a half of sawdust and candles

A family wine merchant that started off the Strand in 1870, opened the Boot & Flogger in a Southwark cellar in 1964 and helped define the modern London wine bar, and still lists fifteen London sites of its own, fourteen of them trading: bars, cellars, wine-shop-tasting-rooms, a bar-and-grill and one specialist tobacconist, plus three El Vino sisters brought in in 2015. In 2007 the chain had 37 sites. More than half of the family-name ones have gone since.

In October 2011, hanging under the BANGERS TOO pub sign at 1 St Mary at Hill, was a smaller sign in gold letters on black: Davys · Fine Wines and Eating. The bar closed the following year, and 1 St Mary at Hill became a Bengali restaurant called Jamdani. The Davy's marque above the door came down with it.

That's the story of a lot of Davy's since 2007. The chain has been quietly editing itself down: closing sites, letting leases run out, folding branches into offices and restaurants. But it isn't gone. Fourteen trading sites are on Davy's own listing, some of them in the same cellars they've been in for forty years, most of them still following the house formula: stone floors, candle stubs, port bottles racked to head height, and, if you know to ask, Old Wallop pulled through a hand pump. This is where that formula came from and what's left of it.

1870: Rising Sun, off the Strand

Francis Edwin Davy opened The Rising Sun just off the Strand in 1870, calling it a "wine house." Davy’s own history puts the Strand and Charing Cross of the day “changing fast”, with pubs, theatres and the wine house springing up to serve customers arriving by the new railway station. In its first decades the business was more merchant than bar. The wine-merchant side grew with sales, the family history says, “particularly strong to upmarket, West End hotels”, and in 1902 FE Davy introduced the first of what the family still call their "own-label" wines. Those own labels remain, per the company's own account, "the cornerstone of our range" today.

Davy's is now on its fifth generation. The second (JHH Davy) took the business through the First World War and the General Strike of 1926, and it was under him, per the same history, that Davy’s “established a reputation as major shippers of Port”. The third (SJE Davy) ran it through the Second, during which, per the company history, "customers had to bring their own glasses" and "a number of premises were lost during the Blitz". There is a photograph on the Davy's site of a lady scrubbing the front step of The Alfred's Head, 140 Newington Causeway, while men repair bomb damage behind her. The family kept trading.

1964: the Boot & Flogger and the London wine-bar template

The fourth-generation head of the firm, John Davy, opened The Boot & Flogger on Redcross Way near London Bridge in 1964. Davy's own copy calls it "the birth of what would become the UK wine bar phenomenon," which oversells it. El Vino on Fleet Street had been going since 1879 and the Ebury Wine Bar in Pimlico since 1959. What Davy did was take a repeatable format, cellar and candles and port bottles racked to head height and house pours in pewter, and set it up as a proper chain. It was less the founding of the movement than its most durable prototype.

Nor is the name a groan-worthy pub pun: a boot and a flogger are the two hand tools a Victorian merchant used to bottle wine by hand. The boot was the shoe that held the empty bottle upright, and the flogger a mallet-like contraption that drove the cork home. Davy's runs on that kind of vintners' lore.

Getting the room open was the wrinkle. As John Davy put it, via James in Lander's interview, "back then there were only three kinds of places to get a drink close to the City: the tea houses, pubs, which were strictly controlled by the brewers, and The Savoy. That was it." The brewery-influenced licensing magistrates weren't going to grant a wine bar. One reportedly told the family the room wasn't needed because "no wine bars existed at the time." Davy sidestepped the whole system by taking a free vintner's licence under his Vintners' Company membership. It was an ancient City freeman right that let him sell wine only, as a sole trader, within the City, without a magistrate's licence at all. The Boot & Flogger opened and poured. The Redcross Way site is still there.

By the 1970s the wine-shop side of the business had been sold off to fund the bar rollout. The chain hit its stride in 1980, opening a site in newly-refurbished Covent Garden with, per the family history, "customers queued out of the door." Late-Thatcher London was a wine-bar city for about a decade, and Davy's had been trading since 1870. By 2007, when Nick Lander wrote up the chain for the FT (later republished on Jancis Robinson's site), the count was 37 wine bars and restaurants from Canary Wharf to White City. The naming style was at peak Davy's whimsy: Bung Hole Cellars, Skinkers, Tappit Hen, the Crusting Pipe. Which is why losing the Crusting Pipe in 2025 felt like more than routine attrition.

The Davy's formula

CAMRA describes Davy's at St James's as stone-flagged floors, dark wood fittings, bottle racks and candlelight, and the description travels to most of the others. Nick Lander, in that 2007 FT piece, called the interiors "the quintessential low-maintenance approach — spit and sawdust floors and empty bottles, of every possible size, on any available ledge and shelf." Sawdust used to be more literal than it is now. The phrase "sort of posh spit-and-sawdust", attributed to James Davy (fifth generation, currently at the helm) via Lander, does the job of two adjectives.

Their house ale is called Old Wallop. It used to be cask. Since the pandemic it's mostly been on keg, though a Londonist visit to Truckles of Pied Bull Yard in 2009 caught the tail-end of the older regime: "imbibe Old Wallop from pewter tankards". The pewter is real. The pots are the tankards Davy's use, and the beer is cheaper by the four-pint jug than by the pint, a survival of vintners' economics from an era when the price of the vessel was part of the pour.

What was actually under the Old Wallop badge is one of those things that everyone-in-the-know knows and nobody-else-quite-does. The old City tankard pour was remembered by long-time regulars (and London CAMRA reporting of the era) as Courage Directors Bitter (4.8% ABV), but PINtPOINT's current profile for Davy's Old Wallop is lighter (4.2%, session-bitter rather than ESB), so today's beer may have drifted, been re-specified, or simply be recorded differently. Spitfire gets guessed at the bar now and then, but that's Shepherd Neame, a different brewery in a different county.

The menu is very Davy's too, and famously has been forever. Four items have been on the card, Lander noted, "since the beginning": chicken liver pâté, a plate of Alderton ham from Suffolk with hot buttered potatoes, a plate of rare beef with the same, and treacle tart with clotted cream. It's not gastropub cooking and it never has been. Lander's own verdict was that the wine, the pricing and the hospitality all outran the kitchen. Walk in for the wine and the room, and treat the food as a bonus.

The bar staff, at St James's and one or two of the others, still work in white shirts and ties. The room fills with what CAMRA politely calls a "largely besuited clientele." The look of the place (sawdust and candles, wood and bottle racks, port glasses on marble) has been so stable for so long that walking into one Davy's for the first time feels like walking back into a Davy's you've been to before, even if you haven't.

The ghosts: Bangers, Boots and the slow thinning of Davy's

The Davy's brand ran two adjacent City sites for years, each named, with a house-style groan, "Bangers." Bangers Too at 1 St Mary at Hill was the Fenchurch Street one. Ewan Munro, whose photograph is above, dates its closure to 2012, and 1 St Mary at Hill became Jamdani after it. Bangers proper, at Eldon House, 2–12 Wilson Street, EC2M, held on longer. It closed for the pandemic on 23 March 2020 and never reopened. The site was granted planning consent for office conversion and the pub was permanently closed in August 2021. A Tripadvisor review posted around then, mourning the closure, called it "one of the last 'real' Davys wine bars and restaurants in the City." That was the sound the brand made losing two of its bigger addresses in under a decade.

The count now sits at fifteen Davy's-operated London sites on their own tally, one of them already closed, with three El Vino sisters alongside, and a ghost list that includes Bangers, the Crusting Pipe and two different lives of the Tappit Hen name. Together they sketch the pattern of a shrinking chain: leases run out, sites redeveloped, revivals opened and let go, one entire Charing Cross classic reborn as a French bistro and then lost to lockdown.

The Crusting Pipe, on the lower ground floor of the Covent Garden market hall, poured its last glass on Sunday 21 December 2025, after 45 years. Opened 1980, it was one of the sites that rode the wine-bar boom in, and, as the Evening Standard recorded, one of the first tenants in the market hall after the 1980s redevelopment: a bright arched cellar under the piazza's pillars, house Port and a menu that leaned toward Iberian cured meats. Davy's gave their own reason on their website: “As trading environments and customer habits have evolved, this chapter has now come to a close.” The Standard set that against what had just opened round the corner, a £73 million Guinness brewery. Mr Fogg's Market Tavern took the room at 27 The Market and opened on 20 July 2026, a fortnight before this went up. Hot Dinners has it pitched as “an ode to a traditional Victorian public house”, making the most of the brickwork and the flagstones, with a Guinness Saloon at the heart of it and a counter showing how many pints have gone out: a free round for every thousandth customer, and a trip to the Dublin brewery for the ten thousandth. One Covent Garden cellar, swapping its house Port for a Guinness Saloon.

The City Boot ran for 41 years off the Barbican Highwalk at 7 Moorfields Highwalk EC2Y, wedged into the elevated pedestrian network above London Wall, before the site was redeveloped in 2015. After a fifteen-month gap Davy's reopened it a short walk west, in a former El Vino bar at 3 Bastion Highwalk, 125 London Wall. The new City Boot was announced by Barbican Life in August 2016 and opened that September and closed three years later, in September 2019. Two goes on the same stretch of Highwalk: the first ended by the developers, the second closed quietly, and CAMRA still lists the unit as "closed long-term, seeking new tenants".

City FOB, on Lower Thames Street EC3R under the Monument end of London Bridge, was the platonic 1980s City Davy's. The 1986 CAMRA guide caught it at full pomp: "exclusive rather pricey cellar bar, sawdust on the floor, wood seating, pints (no halves allowed) in pewter tankards. City dress only." It closed in 2010. The building still stands but the pub is gone.

Skinkers at 42 Tooley Street, just east of London Bridge, was another Lander-era City Davy's, one of the whimsy names on the 2007 list. Closed January 2013 to make way for the Network Rail redevelopment of London Bridge Station (the Shard-era rebuild swallowed a run of Tooley Street trade). CAMRA marked it permanently closed in March 2019, the site now restaurant use. The Ewan Munro Flickr shot of its hanging sign is the frontage image we have on file.

The Tappit Hen (Charing Cross) / Terroirs, at 5 William IV Street WC2N, was the older and better-remembered of the two Tappit Hens Davy's have run. It was a deep-cellar Charing Cross classic, and its vault level was specifically labelled "The Tappit Hen". Davy's closed it as the Tappit Hen by mid-2007. The site reopened in 2008 as Terroirs, a natural-wine French bistro that kept the atmospheric basement. Per CAMRA it closed 15 December 2020 in the second lockdown, "future uncertain", and the permanent-closure notice landed in June 2021.

The Tappit Hen (St Swithin's Lane) was the revival. Davy's took the name back in July 2019, opening a City version at 21–23 St Swithin's Lane just off the Bank end of Cannon Street (PA Life covered the opening). It didn't last long: by the early 2020s the room had passed to Bar 1790, a Portuguese wine bar, which trades there now. For a strange couple of years, the revived Tappit Hen name at St Swithin's Lane overlapped with Terroirs still trading in the old Charing Cross Tappit Hen cellar.

All nine are ghosts in PINtPOINT now, cross-linked back to their sites (and where relevant to their successor tenants: Mr Fogg's for the Crusting Pipe, Bar 1790 for the St Swithin's Tappit Hen) with archive photography where we have it and a "then / now" toggle so you can see them as they were and see what stands there today.

The fifteen Davy's still list

Davy's list fifteen non-El Vino London sites of their own: bars, cellars, wine-shop-tasting-rooms, a bar-and-grill and one specialist tobacconist. One of them, the Crusting Pipe, closed in December 2025 and is still on the page. Not all fifteen fly the family name: the group runs bars under wine-cellar names (Grapeshots, Truckles, the Boot & Flogger), a bar-and-grill (Factory House, in Leadenhall Market), retail shops with tasting bars (Davy's Wine Shop and Davy's Wine House, in Greenwich and Holborn) and even a specialist tobacconist (Segar & Snuff Parlour in Covent Garden). The El Vino sisters

Davy's acquired the El Vino group in 2015: the Fleet Street institution and its sisters, run as sibling operations under the same ownership but with their own signage, menus and identity. They aren't pure Davy's, though they sit in the same portfolio, and they're filed under the same chain in PINtPOINT as the family-name sites, so searching Davy's in the app finds both.

  • El Vino Fleet Street47 Fleet St, EC4A · the flagship, with El Vino founded in 1879

Davy's vs Firkin: two ways to run a chain

The Davy's story sits alongside the Firkin one as two very different pillars of London chain drinking. Both, as plenty of drinkers remember them, had sawdust on the floor, for opposite reasons.

Davy's (from 1870) is a traditional wine and ale house: candle-lit tables, private alcoves, house Port, Old Wallop in pewter tankards. Still family-run, five generations in.

Firkin (1979–2001) was the brewpub revolution: loud, punning, Dogbolter brewed downstairs, and gone within two years of the brewing stopping.

One is still holding its rooms. The other is remembered as much for what it lost as for what it built.

Davy's has never been in a hurry, which is part of why it's still here. A chain that opens a handful of sites a decade and closes a handful more is tending its rooms and passing them on. The stubs of candle in the wax rings on the shelf above the bar are the point. The marketing isn't.

When a Davy's does close, as Skinkers did in 2013, Terroirs (the old Tappit Hen) in 2020, Bangers in 2021, the St Swithin's Tappit Hen revival not long after, and the Crusting Pipe just before Christmas 2025, the room usually goes with it: the sawdust vacuumed up, the tankards packed off to the next site, the sign taken down. Ewan Munro's 2011 shot at St Mary at Hill caught the sign the year before it came down, though nobody knew it yet.

The fourteen still trading are, between them, most of the argument for why the chain outlasted the wine-bar boom. They did change, but inside the room, where you can't see it from the door.
