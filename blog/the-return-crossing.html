The Return Crossing

How American hops came back across the Atlantic.

Some time after 1996, in Mark Tranter’s recollection, one of the owners of the Evening Star in Brighton went to California and came back with Cascade hop pellets. Dark Star, which Fuller’s bought in 2018, was brewing in the pub at the time. Those pellets, with other American hops the merchants Charles Faram could supply in small quantities, became the basis of what the brewery’s founder Rob Jones called “The Hophead Club”, where each meeting tasted a different single-hopped beer. “Cascade was the customers’ and brewers’ favourite, so it was not long until that became the staple,” Tranter said.

Then, in 2002, a British brewer went to the fields to choose for himself.

Gordon A. Ponce’s account of the Transatlantic Beer Bridge, published at The Craft Beer Finder in October 2026 and built on Jeffrey House’s research, follows the traffic west: Fuller’s ESB into America, London Pride into American distribution, Fuller’s yeast into a Californian fermenting tank. We have followed that span four times. This piece follows the traffic coming the other way.

Before the bridge

Michael Jackson’s World Guide to Beer appeared in 1977, and Martyn Cornell called it probably “the single most important event in the history of opening people’s minds in the UK to other types of beer”. The Pitfield Street shop that became the Pitfield Brewery’s own started life as the Two Brewers off-licence around 1980, and was “a vital source for beers from Belgium, especially, but also Germany, the US and elsewhere at a time when such beers were almost impossible to find in the UK”. Jackson’s Beer Hunter followed on Channel 4 in 1990.

House brought Fritz Maytag and Anthony Fuller together at a reception held by his own firm, Thames America Trading Company, at the Mark Hopkins Hotel in San Francisco, he says, then encouraged Fuller’s to import Anchor in their own returnable 50-litre kegs, as our piece on Fuller’s ESB in America sets out. Fuller’s arranged a trial and the beer went into three London pubs in late 1991: the Mawson Arms, the Dove and the Churchill Arms. The 1993 Good Beer Guide also records Fuller’s selling Maytag’s beers in Britain, and Protz, in his chapter there, picked one out: “My favourite is the fruity Liberty Ale, with a massive hop aroma from the use of the home-grown Cascade strain.”

Liberty Ale reached a British handpump on 4 July 1992, at the White Horse at Parsons Green, and Jackson announced it that morning in The Independent. It “appears on draught in Britain today for the first time”, he wrote. He called Cascade America’s traditional “aroma hop” and gave the bouquet as “huge, flowery, piney, faintly citrus”. He ran it on Independence Day, about a beer he noted had first been brewed in 1975, for the anniversary of Paul Revere’s ride.

Fritz Maytag has told House it was John Segal, a Yakima hop grower, who brought Cascade to his attention, and that Segal had learned of the hop through a hop research programme in Oregon. In House’s account Segal also supplied the hops that went into Liberty Ale. Coors had been experimenting with Cascade a couple of years before Liberty Ale, in small quantities, as a participant in the same programme. Maytag’s recollection is that it was never a commercial hop until Anchor used it, in the boil and again to dry-hop. The USDA registered Cascade in 1972, three years before Liberty Ale, and the English Fuggle appears twice in the pedigree the USDA records for it.

The White Horse was not one of those three pubs. Mark Dorber had looked after that cellar since 1981, and in 1990 had convened a seminar there on Burton pale ale which Protz credits with the rebirth of IPA in Britain.

British brewers had gone looking for themselves, years before any of that. Sean Franklin was experimenting with American Cascade “as far back as the early 1980s”. He left brewing, drove a taxi for five years, and came back to it in 1993. Brendan Dobbin established the West Coast Brewery at the Kings Arms in Chorlton-on-Medlock in 1989, and Beer52 say his Yakima Grande pale ale could well be the first example of American Cascade in British brewing.

Three beers arrived in 1993. Franklin’s Rooster’s Yankee was 4.3 per cent, a beer that “fit comfortably into British pub and beer festival culture” while being “a touch more aromatic than most UK drinkers were used to at the time”. Oakham brewed Jeffrey Hudson Bitter commercially for the first time on 13 September, with Mount Hood as the main hop and English Challenger for bittering, Willamette having been trialled first and not chosen: an American hop in the first batch of a beer voted “Supreme Champion Beer of Great Britain” in 2001. Boak and Bailey call JHB “a contemporary product developed quite independently”, inspired by Summer Lightning rather than by anything American. In Sheffield, Dave Wickett’s Kelham Island brewed Pale Rider, and Patrick Morton, who devised it, says that beer was American hopped as well.

In October 1997, writing from the judging at the Great American Beer Festival in Denver, Jackson called the most characterful British IPAs new ones, “such as Fuller’s and Usher’s”, and said both had “clearly been influenced by American examples”, from Bengal IPA, made by Tomcat in Raleigh, North Carolina, to Hophead from the Jones Street brewpub in Eugene, Oregon.

American hops were not new to Britain. Ron Pattinson has British beer production rising by almost half between 1857 and 1871 while the hop acreage stood still, so the hops came in: Bavarian first, then American, dominant among imports by the 1880s. The verdict of the day ran the other way: American hops, in the opinion of “the best judges” quoted by The Farmer’s Magazine in 1863, carried a “disagreeable flavour which renders them quite unsuitable for fine qualities of ales”.

The road

In 2002 someone built a road. Oakham’s own history says John Bryan was “the first UK brewer to travel to the USA hop fields to select hops direct from the farms”, and that he went with Paul Corbett of Charles Faram. He returned for almost every harvest afterwards. Beer Today says those visits “are widely acknowledged as a key factor in the increased availability of US-sourced hop varieties to UK brewers”.

Corbett is what joins the thing together, and he was in it nine years before the road was built. Morton has told House that Corbett at Charles Faram could supply him the American hops he wanted for Pale Rider. Oakham had trialled Willamette for JHB in 1993. Faram were the merchants behind the other American hops in Dark Star’s Hophead Club too, in the small quantities a tasting club needs. Until then the hops came through a merchant.

Sheffield

Wickett was one of the two Sheffield economists in the title of Ponce’s piece: an MA in economics at the university, which he left in 1976, then lecturing in it at the city’s polytechnic, now Sheffield Hallam. The other was House himself. We set out those years in our Anchor piece. Wickett bought a pub called the Alma and reopened it in 1981 as the Fat Cat, added the brewery in 1990, the city’s first new one in over 100 years. That same year he and a partner, John Roman, opened the Old Toad in Rochester, New York, a pub dedicated to American craft beers. Liberty Ale was on sale in Wickett’s city in bottle before Pale Rider existed: the Independence Day column lists Small Beer, at 57 Archer Road, among the shops selling it. Kelham Island’s Pale Rider followed in 1993, and in 2004 it was named Champion Beer of Britain. The brewery’s own bottle label, from around 1998, gives 1992. James O’Hara, who led the group that rescued Kelham Island in 2022, called it “known and revered beyond Sheffield”. Jonny Garrett, writing in Good Beer Hunting, calls it “the first American-hopped beer to win the coveted prize”. Oakham’s JHB, with Mount Hood in the first batch, had taken the title in 2001. Pete Brown put Sheffield’s beer revolution down to “the singular influence of Dave Wickett”.

Protz tells the story Wickett told him. The Fat Cat was refurbished and ready to open with no beer in it, so he rang Timothy Taylor in Keighley, was told Sheffield lay outside the delivery area, and drove over to fetch an eighteen-gallon cask of Landlord in the boot of his car. It went in two days. On three casks, Wickett “helped the brewery turn Landlord into a national brand”. Brown, who was supposed to be ghosting Wickett’s autobiography when he died, tells the same journey and names the beer: Landlord was his favourite.

Brown calls Pale Rider “an early example of the golden ale that has now come to dominate Britain’s cask ale revival”, and Protz, writing Wickett’s obituary in the Guardian, “one of the first of the new breed of golden ales”. Melissa Cole, in Let Me Tell You About Beer, says it was conceived to appeal to women drinkers. Wickett gave the brief himself, in Protz’s 2004 profile. Fat Cat Bitter was built on Landlord, which he loved for its “complexity, full flavour and uncompromising bitterness”, and enough of his customers found that beer “too bitter and hoppy” that he “thought we’d try a beer that is full flavoured, with a good aroma but not too bitter”. Protz calls the hops that did it “varieties famous for their aroma and fruity notes but not aggressively bitter”. Ponce has written the fuller Sheffield account and it is worth your time.

The recipe changed hands several times in eleven years. Morton devised it and handed over to Bob Lawson in early 1996, by his own account, after five years at Kelham Island. Paul Ward brewed the winning batch, “a part-timer and cask washer” when he arrived and head brewer by then, and Pale Rider was 5.2 per cent at that point, brewed from pale malt alone. Ward went on to consult at Thornbridge.

Morton, who devised the beer, has told House that he used whole American Willamette, and that Cascade was not available to him at the time. The brewery named hops for its other beers. The Fat Cat’s beer menu, in the same words from March 2001 to September 2010, gives Kelham Island Bitter “bitterness and flavour from the Mount Hood hops”, Easy Rider “Willamette hops from America’s Yakima Valley”, which is the hop Morton remembers using. Pale Rider gets “a mixture of hops”. Protz names one, in his book 300 Beers to Try Before You Die: Maris Otter pale malt, a touch of wheat malt, and “the only hop is the American Willamette”. That is not what he wrote in 2004, when he gave it as pale malt alone with American and German hops. The notice of the brewery’s closure in May 2022 names Willamette, and has Pale Rider among the first British beers to use American hops. Thornbridge, who have brewed it since the 2022 rescue, list the bottle today at 5.2 per cent on Chinook, Willamette, Crystal and Cascade. That puts Cascade on the record for today’s Pale Rider, but not when it went in. Paul Corbett of the hop merchants Charles Faram, asked by House, recalls Pale Rider as an all-Willamette beer and does not remember Cascade in it. He believes it was still all Willamette when it won in 2004. Faram’s records from that time no longer survive.

Asked by House whether Liberty Ale had influenced Wickett and his use of Cascade, Aaron Gore, an Advanced Cicerone who writes as The Ale Chaser, answered yes, with the reason he would not put it more strongly attached: “that would be overstating it, as I don’t have enough direct contact with Dave to confirm. But that is my understanding, correct.” Put the wider question to Roger Protz, whether Liberty Ale had been influential in Britain’s pale and hoppy movement, and the answer came back in two words: “Very influential.”

Morton left in 1996 to found Abbeydale, where the first Black Mass, brewed that 6 November and developed from a stout he had made at Kelham Island, went out single-hopped with Cascade. Abbeydale’s own account has Moonshine, the first recipe Morton worked on there, originally all Willamette, and names Brendan Dobbin and Sean Franklin as the brewers who made him want that pale, hoppy, American hop flavour. Bob Lawson took Pale Rider over from Morton that year, and House counts him as the Bob Lawson who co-founded Ossett Brewery as a brewpub in 1998 with Bob Hunter and bought his partner out to take it on alone. Ossett’s founder was a “former career brewer at Joshua Tetley in Leeds”. Ossett’s Silver King was on their permanent list at 4.3 per cent by 2009, and was “brewed with generous amounts of American Cascade hops”. When Magic Rock opened in Huddersfield in 2011, its brewer was Stuart Ross, “formerly of Kelham Island, Acorn and Crown Brewery”, and the stated intention was “modern flavour-forward beers inspired in part by US craft breweries”.

Thornbridge to BrewDog

Jim Harrison founded Thornbridge “at the suggestion of his friend David Wickett”, with Wickett promising to help him “get a brewery off the ground in exchange for using the site to contract brew additional batches of Kelham Island beers”. Simon Webster has described how that worked. Thornbridge “started brewing in October 2004”, he said, helping Wickett with “the sudden demand for his beer” after the championship, and “brewing once a week for Kelham Island, which was more often than we were brewing our own”. The brewery at Thornbridge Hall had two young brewers, Stefano Cossi and Martin Dickie, meaning to use American hops at low strengths for a British drinker.

The brief came from Wickett. Harrison recalls him saying they needed “a beer that would turn people’s heads”, and that Wickett took one sip of Jaipur and said, “That’s a world beater”. Webster told House Dickie and Cossi devised the recipe, and that they knew Pale Rider. Ward was Thornbridge’s consultant head brewer in 2004 and a consultant in 2005.

Wickett was not the first British brewer to reach for an American hop. Brown has him hiring Thornbridge’s two young brewers himself. When the brewery was rescued in 2022, Webster said: Kelham Island “were the reason we started the business. Theirs were the first beers we brewed and Dave Wickett was a guiding hand in the early days of Thornbridge.”

Jaipur was first brewed on 7 June 2005, an IPA made with American hops, poured at a garden party at the hall. Garrett has the brewers with “a bold, 6% American IPA in tank, saturated with Cascade, Chinook, Ahtanum, and Simcoe hops” by that summer, and Dickie ties that batch to the party himself: “I think the first batch must have been for that. We dry-hopped a few casks and cracked them on the day of the party.” Brown has the two brewers “excited by new hops from America and New Zealand, which at that point had hardly been seen in Britain”, and Thornbridge “began brewing British cask ales with American hops, used American style”. It is 5.9 per cent today. It was named after the city where Harrison had married. Dickie told Garrett: “Jaipur was the first one where we tried to mimic the American IPA.” Brown says: “For me, Jaipur was the start of the British craft brewing scene.”

Counting the brewers who had learned at Kelham Island and gone on by then, the Brewers Journal listed Abbeydale, Thornbridge, Magic Rock, Bradfield and BrewDog.

In June 2006 the White Horse hosted a celebration of American craft brewing with the Brewers Association and the USDA’s Foreign Agricultural Service. Dorber handled the food pairings; Jackson closed the evening. Almost fourteen years after Liberty Ale’s British draught debut, the same pub was welcoming American brewers with a US government agency as co-host. Doug Odell, one of five visiting brewery representatives, offered the evening’s verdict: “I sense we’re preaching to the choir.”

Dickie left Thornbridge at the end of that year, and BrewDog launched in 2007. Punk IPA arrived at about 6 per cent on a malt and hop bill similar to Jaipur’s. The beer was dialled back to 5.6 per cent within a few years.

Cascade, the hop Protz singled out in Liberty Ale, is in the current listing for Jaipur, where Garrett has it from the start in the beer the brewers had in tank in the summer of 2005, and in BrewDog’s later dry-hopped Punk recipe. The man who brewed Jaipur went and brewed Punk.

Back at Chiswick

Fuller’s is the brewery this story keeps touching: ESB west, Anchor’s beers back east in its own kegs, its yeast in a Californian fermenter. Jackson had heard America in a Fuller’s IPA back in 1997 and named no hop for it. Discovery is the earliest Fuller’s beer in which we can name an American hop. The blonde beer Fuller’s launched in May 2005 was “brewed with barley, wheat, and Liberty and Saaz hops” and aimed at weaning the lager drinker onto ale. It was trailed that April as Fuller’s first blonde, 4.2 per cent in bottle, launched to compete with Belgian brands. Liberty is one of the four West Coast varieties Fuller’s would import for Wild River seven years later. Thornbridge first brewed Jaipur on 7 June 2005, with the same hop country and the opposite intention.

Wild River went on the bar on 15 June 2012, nineteen years after Pale Rider and seven after Jaipur, and was a break with tradition at a brewery that had been “a firm believer in all-English ingredients”: 4.5 per cent, pale malt alone, and four American hops, Cascade, Chinook, Liberty and Willamette. John Keeling, Fuller’s head brewer, had found a precedent in the brewery’s own papers, old recipe books showing that Fuller’s imported Oregon hops and Californian barley in 1891. He used Cascade “sparingly”, for its “extreme bitterness and citrus character”, and leaned on the subtler Liberty, the hop Fuller’s had put in Discovery in 2005. His reason for the beer: “American micros, when they started, were influenced by British beer and now British brewers are being influenced by American craft brewers.”

The report ended on two conditions: the beer would run to the end of August, and if it went well Fuller’s might make it permanent and bottle it, stronger. A bottle duly appeared. The Beer Nut found one in Dublin in November 2014, past its date, and tasted an English beer under the American hops: “Under this there’s a lot of sugary malt, putting the whole thing much more in the English golden ale category than US-style pale ale.” Six years after Wild River, Fuller’s bought Dark Star.

The loop

Wickett’s beer won the championship, and the brief that became Jaipur came from him. House sent Fuller’s west and brought Anchor back east. The route runs out of Yorkshire and back into it: Landlord at Keighley, Maytag in San Francisco, Anchor’s beers to Britain in Fuller’s own kegs, and Liberty Ale on a Sheffield shelf in 1992.

The beer Wickett drove to Keighley to fetch is the beer Maytag has long named behind Liberty Ale. Liberty Ale was first brewed six months before Maytag set foot in Keighley, and the beer went on changing after that: the first brew was not all-malt and the later ones were. If Taylor shaped Liberty Ale, it shaped what the beer became rather than what it was.

At the Fat Cat, Landlord has never been out of stock.

The last word: Jeffrey House

My reading is that Liberty Ale’s 1992 draught debut gave British drinkers and brewers an early taste of what Cascade could do in a pale ale. Roger Protz’s assessment gives Liberty Ale a leading place in the story of the pale and hoppy movement. In Sheffield, Wickett and Morton showed with Pale Rider what the gentler aroma of Willamette could bring to a British golden cask ale, at a time when Morton recalls being unable to get Cascade. Wickett then helped set the course for Jaipur, and several brewers who came through Kelham Island went on to use Cascade in beers of their own.

I introduced Fritz Maytag to Anthony Fuller and encouraged Fuller’s to bring Anchor’s beers to Britain, and Wickett and the brewers around him showed where British brewing could take those American hop aromas. The wider influence comes through even if we leave open whether Liberty Ale directly inspired Pale Rider.
