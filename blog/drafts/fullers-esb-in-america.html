The man who took ESB to America

Jeffrey House spent years persuading Fuller’s to send ESB to America. Brewers found more than a good pint in it. He lost the account two years after the draught launch.

Fuller’s ESB reached a Seattle bar in November 1987. Jeffrey House had been trying to get it there for years. One of the people he had needed to convince was the man who brewed it.

Reg Drury, Fuller’s head brewer, had good reasons to hesitate. ESB was a traditional cask beer. Bottling, pasteurisation and forced carbonation could damage the flavours that made it worth drinking. Getting more beer out of Chiswick was not much of an achievement if it arrived tasting worse.

House wanted to sell it in America. Drury wanted to protect it. When the beer finally arrived, House’s verdict was generous.

“The brew was spot on the money. Reg had got it right!”

Within two years, House would lose the Fuller’s agency. But the beers he had introduced, and some of the ideas American brewers took from them, would last much longer.

A London bitter in Seattle

The bar was the College Inn, beside the University of Washington. The beer arrived through a wholesaler called Alehouse. House dates the launch to November 1987 and identifies it as ESB’s first American draught appearance.

By then he had been importing Fuller’s for eight years. London Pride had come first, in cans, but ESB offered him something he thought would make a stronger impression.

House’s own assessment is candid. Pride was a fine session beer, but perhaps not distinctive enough for the American market, especially at the colder temperatures at which Americans served it. ESB had more to announce itself with.

Even the name helped. House thought American drinkers would take to the initials: three letters were easier to remember and order than an explanation of English bitterness.

House sums up the wait.

“We finally convinced Fullers to ship us ESB in 1987, nearly 10 years after we first started with the London Pride cans.”

For all the work involved in taking beer across an ocean, one of the hardest sales had been to its own brewery.

Selling it before he could pour it

House had arrived in San Francisco in the mid-seventies for an advertising job, only to find that the job came without a green card. Importing beer became another way forward. He chose a brewery that had been local to him in London.

London Pride cans entered the business in 1979 through a three-way partnership, Spectrum Imports. On 1 January 1980, Thames America Trading Company followed, operating from Sutter Street in San Francisco.

Gordon A. Ponce documents those early years in “The Transatlantic Beer Bridge” at The Craft Beer Finder, the foundation for this feature.

The work continued long after a shipment landed. House had to find wholesalers, win accounts and persuade bars that their customers would order the beer. He was giving an unfamiliar product a place behind the counter.

Among the people he met was Jeff Gunn of IDD, an English-born supplier of draught equipment. Gunn introduced him to Fritz Maytag of Anchor Brewing. House would later introduce Maytag to Anthony Fuller.

At the time, putting the two brewery owners together must have looked like a useful thing for an importer to do. House came to remember it rather differently.

What the brewers borrowed

Fuller’s influence in America went beyond the beer House could sell. At Goose Island it helped shape an ambition. At Firestone Walker it entered the fermentation tank.

John Hall, who founded Goose Island in Chicago in 1988, admired the relationship between Fuller’s and London. He wanted a brewery that could occupy a similar place in his own city.

“The dream for me was to be a Fuller’s, to represent Chicago like Fuller’s does in London.”

Hall said that in an interview with The Brewers Journal, in which he also expressed his respect for the brewery and for John Keeling. Fuller’s offered an example of the kind of brewery Hall wanted to build, as well as beer he admired.

Keeling’s own account makes the ESB connection more specific. Writing on Fuller’s blog in April 2018, he identified it as the beer that inspired Hall to start brewing. In the same passage, he recorded House’s introduction of London Pride to America in 1979, with ESB following in 1987. That is Fuller’s account of Hall’s inspiration; Hall’s own words describe the larger ambition.

For Firestone Walker, the connection was a choice between yeast cultures. Developing Double Barrel Ale, the Californian brewery ran trials and preferred a strain identified as Fuller’s ESB, despite the cleaner Chico alternative.

It was a difficult choice to live with. Strong flocculation helped the brewery recover yeast for reuse, but could complicate the beer’s maturation. Co-founder David Walker recalls years of diacetyl problems before they adjusted the culture to suit their needs.

His description of the later house yeast is the lasting connection: still essentially a Fuller’s or London yeast. We have told the trials and their consequences in “Why Firestone Walker chose Fuller’s yeast”.

Hall wanted his brewery to mean something to Chicago. Walker and his colleagues wanted the beer they preferred in the glass. Fuller’s supplied something different to each. Neither story requires a claim that the resulting beers were copies of ESB.

Something in the empties

Beer also travelled back, in containers that had already done one Atlantic crossing.

Fuller’s sold Thames America one-way kegs made by Van Leer. Fuller’s beer went west in them, but the empty barrels did not have to be returned to Chiswick. Anchor bought them, filled them with its own beer and shipped them east.

The distinction is easy to lose in the telling. These were not deposit kegs routinely going home. They were containers bought from Fuller’s, sold on to Anchor and given another cargo.

According to House, the hardware left a surprisingly long legacy: for years, Anchor was not using the American Sankey keg fittings the rest of the industry ran on. The arrangement reached beyond which beer crossed the ocean. It affected the equipment needed to serve it.

House had brought Maytag and Anthony Fuller together at a distributor party at the Mark Hopkins Hotel on Nob Hill. Fuller’s subsequently began importing Anchor. In late 1991, its beers went on trial in three London pubs: the Mawson Arms, the Dove and the Churchill Arms. We have followed what that did to British brewing in “Anchor Brewing’s British heritage”.

For House, the return shipment was a practical extension of a relationship he had helped establish. The American beer could use containers his business had bought for the English beer. The brewery owners knew one another. There were pubs in which to try it.

The Fuller’s agency, however, had already left his hands.

Losing the account

House dates its ending to 1989. His account begins with a final visit to the Fuller’s boardroom that summer.

Anthony Fuller proposed removing the Eastern and Midwest states from Thames America’s territory, with $50,000 in compensation. House refused. The offer was withdrawn. Andrew Clarke, formerly of Watney’s American operation, then told Thames America that Grolsch would handle Fuller’s nationally. Fuller’s, House notes, was itself under contract to sell Grolsch in its pubs.

John Lambright managed the handover. In the end, House says, the $50,000 was paid anyway.

A letter from Fuller’s marketing director, Charles Williams, offered a different kind of recognition: House had started the brand in America, and that achievement could not be taken away from him.

His lesson from the episode was pointed.

“Introducing a brewery owner to another brewery owner is never a good idea.”

Elsewhere in the same book, House offers a broader explanation of an importer’s predicament.

“When you import brands, you usually have about 10 years to market them. The best thing to do is do a moderate job, because if you do a bad job, they fire you. If you do a very good job, they get rid of you because they think somebody else can do better, because you’re a small company.”

It is the frustration of building a market for something you do not own. Success increases the value of the account, without necessarily making the importer more secure in it.

House describes losing Blackthorn cider to Miller Brewing on a similar timetable. Whatever part he believed the introduction had played at Fuller’s, the vulnerability was familiar.

Thames America did not close with the loss of the agency. It continued with Greene King Abbot Ale, Wexford Irish Cream and Old Suffolk Ale. The cider business grew, and House would eventually make a drink of his own.

The name on the tap

In the PINtPOINT venue data used for this feature, on 19 October 2026, Fuller’s ESB appears at 41 venues in Britain and one in the United States. London Pride appears at 202 in Britain and three in America.

Those are sightings within our coverage, not a census of either country. They cannot measure the whole market House helped open. They do show that both beers remain on bars long after his part in selling them ended.

London Pride gave him an importing business. ESB took years of persuasion. The account went elsewhere, while the beer continued to find customers and brewers found uses for what Fuller’s had taught them.

House lost the right to sell it. He kept his place in the story of how it got there.
