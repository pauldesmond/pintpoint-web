The man who took ESB to America

Jeffrey House spent years persuading Fuller’s to send ESB to America. When it got there, brewers found more in it than a good pint.

Fuller’s ESB reached a Seattle bar in 1987. Jeffrey House had been trying to get it there for years. One of the people he had needed to convince was the man who brewed it.

Reg Drury, Fuller’s head brewer, was not keen. ESB was a traditional cask beer, and in House’s account Drury’s objection was specific: bottling, pasteurisation and forced carbonation would damage the delicate flavours that made it worth drinking.

House wanted to sell it in America. Drury wanted to protect it. When the beer finally arrived, House was delighted with the result.

“The brew was spot on the money. Reg had got it right!”

A London bitter in Seattle

The bar was the College Inn, beside the University of Washington. The beer arrived through a wholesaler called Alehouse. House dates the Seattle draught launch to 1987.

By then he had been importing Fuller’s for eight years. London Pride had come first, in cans, but ESB offered him something he thought would make a stronger impression.

Pride was a fine session beer, but perhaps not distinctive enough for the American market, especially at the colder temperatures at which Americans served it. ESB had more to announce itself with.

Even the name helped. House thought American drinkers would take to the initials: three letters were easier to remember and order than an explanation of English bitterness.

House sums up the wait.

“We finally convinced Fullers to ship us ESB in 1987, nearly 10 years after we first started with the London Pride cans.”

For all the work involved in taking beer across an ocean, one of the hardest sales had been to its own brewery.

Selling it before he could pour it

House had arrived in San Francisco in the mid-seventies for an advertising job, only to find that the job came without a green card. Importing beer became another way forward. He chose a brewery that had been local to him in London.

London Pride cans entered the business in 1979 through a three-way partnership, Spectrum Imports. On 1 January 1980, Thames America Trading Company followed, operating from Sutter Street in San Francisco.

Gordon A. Ponce documents those early years in “The Transatlantic Beer Bridge” at The Craft Beer Finder. His account helped inform this feature.

The work continued long after a shipment landed. House had to find wholesalers, win accounts and persuade bars that their customers would order the beer.

Among the people he met was Jeff Gunn of IDD, an English-born supplier of draught equipment. Gunn introduced him to Fritz Maytag of Anchor Brewing. House would later introduce Maytag to Anthony Fuller.

What the brewers borrowed

Fuller’s influence in America went beyond the beer House could sell. At Goose Island it helped shape an ambition. At Firestone Walker it entered the fermentation tank.

John Hall, who founded Goose Island in Chicago in 1988, admired the relationship between Fuller’s and London. He wanted a brewery that could occupy a similar place in his own city.

“The dream for me was to be a Fuller’s, to represent Chicago like Fuller’s does in London.”

Hall said that in an interview with The Brewers Journal, in which he also expressed his respect for the brewery and for John Keeling.

Keeling’s own account makes the ESB connection more specific. Writing on Fuller’s blog in April 2018, he identified it as the beer that inspired Hall to start brewing. In the same passage, he recorded House’s introduction of London Pride to America in 1979, with ESB following in 1987.

For Firestone Walker, the connection was a choice between yeast cultures. Developing Double Barrel Ale, the Californian brewery ran trials and preferred a strain identified as Fuller’s ESB, despite the cleaner Chico alternative.

It was a demanding choice to live with. Strong flocculation helped the brewery recover yeast for reuse, but could complicate the beer’s maturation. Co-founder David Walker recalls years of diacetyl problems before they adjusted the culture to suit their needs.

Walker describes the later house yeast as still essentially a Fuller’s or London yeast. We have told the trials and their consequences in “Why Firestone Walker chose Fuller’s yeast”.

Something in the empties

Beer also travelled back, in containers that had already done one Atlantic crossing.

Fuller’s sold Thames America one-way kegs made by Van Leer. Fuller’s beer went west in them, but the empties never had to go back to Chiswick. Anchor bought them, filled them with its own beer and shipped them east.

According to House, the hardware left a long legacy: for years, Anchor was not using the standard American Sankey keg fittings.

House had brought Maytag and Anthony Fuller together at a distributor party at the Mark Hopkins Hotel on Nob Hill. Fuller’s subsequently began importing Anchor. In late 1991, its beers went on trial in three London pubs: the Mawson Arms, the Dove and the Churchill Arms. We have followed what that did to British brewing in “Anchor Brewing’s British heritage”.

Ten years with Fuller’s

House dates the end of the agency to 1989. His account begins with a final visit to the Fuller’s boardroom that summer.

Anthony Fuller proposed removing the Eastern and Midwest states from Thames America’s territory, with $50,000 in compensation. House refused, and the offer was withdrawn. Andrew Clarke, formerly of Watney’s American operation, then told Thames America that Grolsch would handle Fuller’s nationally. Fuller’s, House notes, was itself under contract to sell Grolsch in its pubs.

John Lambright managed the handover. In the end, House says, the $50,000 was paid anyway.

A letter from Fuller’s marketing director, Charles Williams, offered a different kind of recognition: House had started the brand in America, and that could never be taken away from him.

House draws two lessons from it. One is about the Mark Hopkins evening.

“Introducing a brewery owner to another brewery owner is never a good idea.”

The other is about the trade itself.

“When you import brands, you usually have about 10 years to market them. The best thing to do is do a moderate job, because if you do a bad job, they fire you. If you do a very good job, they get rid of you because they think somebody else can do better, because you’re a small company.”

Blackthorn cider went to Miller Brewing on a similar timetable.

Thames America carried on, with Greene King Abbot Ale, Wexford Irish Cream and Old Suffolk Ale. The cider business grew, and House would go on to make a drink of his own.

The name on the tap

In the PINtPOINT venue data, on 19 October 2026, Fuller’s ESB appears at 41 venues in Britain and one in the United States. London Pride appears at 202 in Britain and three in America.

London Pride gave him an importing business. ESB took years of persuasion. Both are still on the bar, and brewers are still using what Fuller’s taught them.
