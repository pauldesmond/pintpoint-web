The introduction

Fuller’s ESB went on draught in America for the first time in 1987, at a bar beside the University of Washington, and it got there over the objections of the man who brewed it.

The usual version of transatlantic beer history runs one way: American hops, American ideas, arriving in Britain and rearranging it. This one starts in the other direction, with an English bitter that took the best part of a decade to persuade its own brewery to export, and with the man who sold it finding that the hardest part was never the shipping.

The College Inn

The bar was the College Inn, at 45th and University in Seattle, and the beer arrived through a wholesaler called Alehouse. Jeffrey House, who had been importing Fuller’s into the United States since 1979, puts the launch in November 1987. It was the first time Extra Special Bitter had been poured from a tap on American soil.

What makes the scene worth opening on is that the resistance had come from Chiswick. Fuller’s head brewer Reg Drury wanted ESB kept as it was, a traditional cask beer, and did not want it bottled. In House’s account the worry was specific and technical rather than sentimental: bottling, pasteurisation and forced carbonation would, between them, impair some of the delicate flavours that made the beer worth exporting in the first place.

That is a brewer arguing against his own distribution. The beer was shipped, and House recorded the verdict afterwards.

“The brew was spot on the money. Reg had got it right!”

House also claims the name did some of the work. Americans like mnemonics and shortened words, he writes, and three letters that already stood for something made a better handle on a bar than a sentence about bitterness would have done.

How it got there

The setup is shorter than the beer deserves. House arrived in San Francisco in the mid-seventies chasing an advertising job that turned out to come without a green card. The import business began partly as the answer to that problem, which means the first British beer he brought in had a job to do beyond selling. He picked the brewery that had been local to him in London.

London Pride came first, in cans, in 1979, through a three-way partnership called Spectrum Imports. Thames America Trading Company followed on 1 January 1980, run out of Sutter Street in San Francisco. Gordon A. Ponce has written the fullest account of that period at The Craft Beer Finder, and the detail of those first years belongs to him.

He is blunt about the length of it.

“We finally convinced Fullers to ship us ESB in 1987, nearly 10 years after we first started with the London Pride cans.”

Convinced is the word to notice. The obstacle was never an ocean or a customs form. It was persuading a London family brewery that a beer built for its own cellars would survive the crossing and still be worth drinking at the other end.

What is useful here is why it took until 1987 to get the second beer across. House is candid about it in his own book: London Pride is a fine session beer, but it was perhaps never distinctive enough for America, especially given how much colder Americans serve their beer. A pint engineered for a warm cellar and a long evening does not announce itself at American temperatures. ESB did.

Along the way he met Jeff Gunn of IDD, an English-born supplier of draught equipment, and through Gunn he met Fritz Maytag of Anchor. That meeting is the hinge of everything that follows.

What American brewers took from it

The influence is real and it is not one thing. Two American brewers took something lasting from Fuller’s, and what each took was so different that flattening them into a single claim would lose the interesting part.

John Hall founded Goose Island in Chicago in 1988. What he took was an idea of what a brewery could be to a city.

“The dream for me was to be a Fuller’s, to represent Chicago like Fuller’s does in London.”

That is a model, not a recipe. Writing on the Fuller’s blog in April 2018, John Keeling put it the brewery’s way round: ESB was the beer that inspired Hall to start brewing. Keeling recorded in the same paragraph that the first Fuller’s beer in the United States was London Pride, imported by Jeffrey House in 1979, with ESB following in 1987.

Firestone Walker took something else entirely. When the Californian brewery built Double Barrel Ale it ran beer trials, and a Fuller’s ESB strain won them, beating the cleaner and more predictable Chico yeast that most of American craft was built on. Co-founder David Walker says what they use today is still essentially a Fullers/London yeast. We have told that story on its own, in Why Firestone Walker chose Fuller’s yeast, and it does not need repeating here.

So: an idea of a brewery in one case, a living organism in the other. The same source, two completely different kinds of inheritance, and no shared recipe between them.

Neither brewer copied a beer. One borrowed a civic ambition and built something that tasted nothing like its model. The other took a yeast out of an Oregon laboratory and spent years learning to handle it, and that one you can still taste.

The beer that came back

The traffic did not stay one way, and the mechanism is better than the shorthand usually allows.

Fuller’s beer crossed the Atlantic in one-way kegs from a Dutch maker, Van Leer. At the American end they were empties with no journey home. Anchor bought them, refilled them with its own beer, and shipped them east. These were not Fuller’s kegs coming home on a deposit. They were barrels bought secondhand by a San Francisco brewery and sent back full of something else.

House records one lasting consequence: for years Anchor was the only American brewery not using American Sankey keg fittings.

House had introduced Fritz Maytag to Anthony Fuller at a distributor party at the Mark Hopkins Hotel on Nob Hill. Fuller’s began importing Anchor into England, and in late 1991 Anchor’s beers went on trial in three London pubs: the Mawson Arms, the Dove and the Churchill Arms. We have written about what that did to British brewing in Anchor Brewing’s British heritage.

What followed

The Fuller’s agency ended in 1989, and House sets out the sequence himself.

In the summer of that year he made his last visit to the Fuller’s boardroom. Anthony Fuller proposed taking the Eastern and Midwest states away from Thames America, with fifty thousand dollars in compensation. House would not agree to it. The offer was withdrawn. Andrew Clarke, who had come from Watney’s American operation, then told Thames America that Grolsch would be handling Fuller’s nationally. Fuller’s, House notes, was under contract to sell Grolsch in its own pubs. The handover was managed by John Lambright, and the fifty thousand dollars was paid in the end anyway.

What survives of it in his papers is a letter from Fuller’s marketing director, Charles Williams, reminding him that he was the one who had started the brand over there, and that this could never be taken away from him.

House has two explanations for it. The first is personal, and he states it flatly.

“Introducing a brewery owner to another brewery owner is never a good idea.”

The second is structural, and he states it as a rule of the trade.

“When you import brands, you usually have about 10 years to market them. The best thing to do is do a moderate job, because if you do a bad job, they fire you. If you do a very good job, they get rid of you because they think somebody else can do better, because you’re a small company.”

Blackthorn cider had already left him for Miller Brewing on the same timetable.

Losing an agency is not the same as losing a company. Thames America carried on afterwards with Greene King Abbot Ale, Wexford Irish Cream and Old Suffolk Ale, and the cider side of the business grew into something larger than the beer had ever been.

Still pouring

Of the venues we track, Fuller’s ESB is on the bar at forty one in Britain and one in the United States. London Pride, the beer he started with, is at two hundred and two in Britain and three in America.

Forty years after a Londoner with no green card talked a family brewery into putting its best beer on a boat, the beers are still here.
