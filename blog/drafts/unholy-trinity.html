The Unholy Trinity

Three essays on who made the season up.

Three of the autumn’s great nights fall inside a single week: Halloween on the thirty-first, Lewes Bonfire on the fifth, and — on the first Friday of November, at one minute to nine — the evening Denmark declares Christmas open.

Three nights in a week, and not one of them is as old as it says. Each of these essays takes one of them apart, and the beer is the evidence: what a brewery makes when it is answering to something real, and what it makes when it is answering to a date somebody chose.

The question is not whether these traditions were invented. All of them were. The question is who did the inventing, and whether the people who keep them were ever told.

The overture, six weeks earlier
The same audit, run on a festival that is not in this week and not even in the month it is named after: September is Oktoberfest — a royal wedding with an agricultural show bolted on a year later, a beer chosen for having sat still the longest, and four countries that cannot agree what is in the glass. It is also where the green hop that Part One goes looking for is first mentioned, and lost.

Part one · Saturday 31 October

Season of the Witch

England's most famous pagan survival was named in 1939 — by an amateur antiquarian, in a journal, after the pub signs that were already hanging. Then folk horror borrowed him, the comics got hold of him, and nobody checked. Also: why pumpkin ale was a poverty measure, and why the real British autumn beer is over before Halloween.

Invented by an outsider, and the keepers were never told. Part two · Thursday 5 November

Voice of the Fire

Lewes burns a Pope who had nothing to do with the Gunpowder Plot, for reasons invented in the 1850s, in a celebration that had already lapsed once and been rebuilt by young men with tar barrels. It is still the most honest festival in England — and the brewery that makes the beer for it is inside the town.

Invented by the people who keep it, in public, and they are still arguing. Part three · Friday 6 November

The First Friday of Christmas

A Norwegian king once made brewing Yule ale compulsory, on pain of losing your farm. A thousand years later a Danish brewery fixed the start of Christmas at one minute to nine on a Friday in November. In between, an American brewed a Christmas beer in 1975 because he had looked for the tradition and concluded it was gone.

Invented by a marketing department, in 1990, and sold back as ancient.

Lewes is the control. Without it this is three demolitions in a row; with it, the argument is about custody rather than authenticity. A tradition can be made up and still be real, so long as the people doing it are the ones who made it up — and are still prepared to fall out over it.
