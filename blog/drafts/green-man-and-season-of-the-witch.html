Season of the Witch

Britain's most famous pagan survival was invented in 1939, and it was named after a pub.

According to CAMRA’s count, 72 pubs open in the UK are called the Green Man, and a good many of their signs now show a face made of leaves. The foliate head is carved into English churches from the twelfth century onward — Exeter, Norwich, Southwell — peering out of the stonework with oak springing from its mouth. The standard reading is that he is a pagan god who survived the conversion, smuggled into the masonry by masons who had not quite let go of the old religion.

It is a wonderful story. It is also younger than the pubs. He is not even an autumn figure — Raglan tied him to May Day — but he is the purest case of what the last night of October does every year: take something recent and dress it as something ancient. That goes for what is in the glass, too.

Three nights in seven days, and not one of them is as old as it says. This is the first of three.

Invented in 1939, named after a pub

The carvings had no agreed name until Lady Raglan gave them one, in an article called "The Green Man in Church Architecture" in the journal Folklore in 1939. Her interest is thought to have begun with the carvings at St Jerome's in Llangwm, Monmouthshire. She took the name from the pub signs, which were already everywhere, and attached it to the stonework. Then she argued that the foliate heads, the Jack-in-the-Green and Robin Hood were all the same thing: one ancient god of nature and fertility, living on under the noses of the clergy.

Folklorists have spent the years since taking it apart: there is no evidence the motif was pagan, and no continuity anyone can demonstrate. The folklorist Icy Sedgwick puts it plainly: “People insist the Green Man was an ancient nature god, hiding among the carvings as a representation of paganism, but the truth is less exciting.”

Which leaves the pub signs in an odd position. They are older than the myth they are supposed to illustrate. Whatever the landlords thought they were hanging up, it was not a fertility deity, because nobody had thought of that yet. The conventional readings are all workaday: a forester in Lincoln green, the woodwose or wild man of heraldry in his ivy with a club, or the Jack-in-the-Green — the walking arbour the chimney sweeps paraded on May Day. Modest trades and municipal pageant, promoted to an eternal priesthood by an amateur antiquarian with a taste for the romantic.

1968, 1971, 1973

None of which slowed anybody down. British folk horror's three founding films — Witchfinder General in 1968, The Blood on Satan's Claw in 1971, The Wicker Man in 1973 — all turn on the same idea: that the old religion is still down there under the topsoil, and that the countryside is keeping something from you.

And the pub in The Wicker Man is The Green Man. Summerisle's entire pagan apparatus, the thing Sergeant Howie cannot get anyone to explain, is presented under a sign whose meaning was invented thirty-four years before the cameras rolled. The most ancient-seeming object in British folk horror is thirty-four years old and hanging over a bar.

That is the film's own point, arrived at from the outside: Summerisle's traditions are a Victorian landowner's invention, believed in since. The set dressing was more honest than anyone intended.

Then they gave it to the children

The same years handed the identical material to eight-year-olds as comedy. Shiver and Shake arrived in 1973 with Scream Inn, a haunted pub nobody could survive a night in; Monster Fun followed in 1975 with Frankie Stein and Draculass; from 1976 Rentaghost ran a South Ealing agency that hired out the dead by the hour. Anyone raised on that lot got their folklore twice — as terror in the cinema their parents went to, and as a joke in a comic that cost five pence. The pagan and the pantomime arrived in the same childhood, a few years apart.

Even the lantern is a pub story

It is not only the foliage that starts with a round. The usual telling of the jack-o'-lantern has an Irishman variously called Stingy Jack, Jack the Smith or Drunk Jack — "a knave and a trickster with a well-known taste for beer" — meeting the Devil on his way home. Jack asks for one last drink first, and in the pub he suggests a trick: the Devil turns himself into a silver coin to pay the landlord, then vanishes, and the two of them drink for nothing. The Devil obliges. Jack pockets him.

He is eventually condemned to walk the earth with a coal in a hollowed-out vegetable, and the vegetable in question was a turnip or a swede, because that is what nineteenth-century Ireland and Britain had. The pumpkin is what happened when the story emigrated and found something bigger and easier to carve.

Which makes Halloween's most recognisable object a souvenir of a man getting the Devil to buy a round.

So what do you actually drink

Not pumpkin, if you want the honest autumn beer.

Pumpkin ale is American and it began as poverty. The first written records of brewing with pumpkins are seventeenth century, and they describe it as the poor household’s beer, made from whatever could be fermented: molasses, corn, a little malt, pumpkin. The early accounts complain of its twang. In 1771 the American Philosophical Society printed a recipe for “pompion ale” that treats the pumpkin exactly like an apple for cider — beaten in a trough, the juice boiled and skimmed, then hopped and fermented “as Malt Beer”. Cindy Ott, the University of Delaware historian who wrote the book on the pumpkin, puts it flatly: "Pumpkin beer used to be a drink of last resort. It wasn't something that was valorised at all." Pumpkin went in because barley was scarce and expensive.

By the twentieth century the style was forgotten. It came back in 1985, when Bill Owens of Buffalo Bill's Brewery in California read that George Washington had used fruit and vegetables including pumpkins in his beers at Mount Vernon, and thought: why not a pumpkin ale? He roasted the pumpkins in the pizza oven and put them into the house amber, and because pumpkin on its own tastes of very little, the brewery added nutmeg and cloves. The colonial beer was pumpkin standing in for malt. The modern one is pumpkin pie standing in for pumpkin. It is the revival everyone points to, and most of the spiced, whipped-cream-rimmed pumpkin beers on the shelves this month follow where it led.

Asked to confirm the Washington story, Ott couldn't. Possible, she said. So American autumn's favourite beer was revived on an anecdote nobody can source, as the reverse of what the ingredient was for. Lady Raglan would have recognised the method.

Britain's real autumn beer is green hop, and the bad news is that you have very likely missed it. Hops go into the copper within twelve hours of picking, because undried hops rot, so the beer exists only for as long as the harvest does. Gadds’ held their Green Hop Day at the brewery in Broadstairs on 19 September; the last sizeable outing was the Spa Valley Railway festival, more than twenty-five green hop beers over four days, finishing on 18 October. Thirteen days before Halloween. What is left on the last night of October is the final pins and firkins of the September pick, going or gone.

Which is the whole difference, put in a cellar. Pumpkin ale is available for the entire autumn because nothing about it is seasonal; it is a flavour, and flavours keep. Green hop is nearly over by Halloween because it is the actual thing — governed by when a plant is ready, not by a marketing calendar. One of them is autumn. The other is a costume.

If the green hop has gone, the older Halloween is still in season, and it was never orange. It was apples. Ireland called the night Snap Apple Night, after a game in which apples and lit candles were fixed to the arms of a cross hung from the ceiling, the whole thing was set spinning, and you went for an apple with your teeth. Daniel Maclise painted one from a party he went to in Blarney in 1832 and hung it at the Royal Academy the following year: snap apple in the middle, apple bobbing to one side, a couple burning nuts in the grate to see how their courtship would go. That was the old point of the night. Not fright but fortune, and mostly about who would marry whom: the first to bite the apple was next up the aisle, and a girl who peeled one in a single strip and threw it over her shoulder would read her future husband’s initial in the peel on the floor. Toffee apples still belong to the night in Scotland and Northern Ireland, and to Bonfire Night in England. The apple is the one Halloween ingredient that is actually ripe on the thirty-first. Which makes the honest drink for the night a cider, or a beer with the orchard in it: the season in the glass, rather than a flavour of it.

And the witch was a brewer, except she wasn't

You will have met this one, because it is everywhere. Medieval brewing was women's work; alewives wore tall pointed hats to be seen at market, hung a broom outside the door to show ale was for sale, kept a cat for the mice in the grain and brewed in a great pot over a fire. Hat, broom, cat, cauldron. The witch, the story goes, is just a brewster nobody liked.

It is a lovely idea and the History Channel has helped it along. The trouble is the pictures. Dr Christina Wade, who works on the history of women in brewing, has been patiently pointing out for years that when the witch-hunts got going in earnest, alewives in the art of the period are shown bareheaded, or naked, or in a "devil's snare" — a two-pronged headpiece that meant vanity. Not a tall pointed hat. The iconography does not line up, and the claim appears to be a twenty-first-century one that grew in the telling.

What is true is better anyway, and doesn't need the hat. Brewing really was women's work, domestic and everywhere and barely worth remarking on. And then it stopped being, as it got profitable and organised and moved into guilds and capital, and the women who had done it were written out over a couple of centuries. That happened. It just wasn't Halloween that did it.

The signs stay up

Seventeen White Harts in our data. Sixteen Royal Oaks, twelve Moons, ten Dragons, seven Ravens, six Devils, six Cross Keys, one Hobgoblin. A white stag was a messenger from the otherworld long before it was Richard II's badge; the oak was venerated for centuries before Charles II hid in one; the keys are St Peter's and the moon is nobody's in particular.

The pub sign is where England files the things it has stopped being able to explain, and it does not much mind whether they were ever true. Four times over in one evening: a carving named after a signboard, a lantern carved from a drinking story, a seasonal beer revived on an anecdote nobody can source, and a witch built out of a brewster who never wore the hat. The Green Man got his name from a signboard and his religion from a magazine article, and he is still up there, dripping oak leaves over the door, on a building where people have been drinking since before anyone thought to ask what he meant.

Seventy-two Green Man pubs, and the last of the green hop going flat somewhere as you read this. If any tap near you still has it, PINtPOINT will show you where.
