Roast is not smoke

English malting spent two centuries getting the smoke out of beer on purpose. Craft put it back in to sell November.

A trade newsletter went round the pub trade this week with a list of beers for the fifth, and it said something about Harvey’s Bonfire Boy that is worth stopping on. The black malt, it explained, gives the beer a delightfully smoky flavour. It is a nice sentence and it has the technical history precisely upside down. Black malt is the single ingredient in British brewing that exists because somebody was trying to get rid of smoke.

The two-hundred-year flight from smoke

Before clean fuel, all kilned malt tasted of whatever had been burned under it, and nobody was pleased about it. Wood was scarce in England by the sixteenth century, so maltsters burned straw, and coal where they could get it — taxed, filthy, and prone to leaving the grain tasting of the fire. Peat and turf were avoided outright, because the smoke got into the malt and stayed there. The trade had a word for what that did to the beer: the twang. It was not a flavour note. It was a fault.

The fix arrived in stages. Coke — coal cooked clean of its volatiles — was first understood as a product in 1603, two years before the Gunpowder Plot, and was being used to make pale malt by 1642. Indirect kilns followed, keeping the fire’s smoke away from the grain entirely. Anthracite was prized for the same reason: it burns with almost nothing coming off it. Every one of those moves was made for one purpose, which was to stop the beer tasting of the kiln.

So for something close to four hundred years, an English brewer whose beer tasted of smoke was not being characterful. He was selling a defect, and his customers knew it.

1817: colour without fire

Which brings us to the black malt. In 1817, Daniel Wheeler took out British patent number 4112 for a new method of drying malt, and the machine in the patent is a revolving cylindrical iron drum — explicitly described as being like the ones already used for roasting coffee. Malt went in, the drum turned, the temperature went past 400°F, and what came out was nearly black without being charred, and without tasting of the fuel at all.

That is the whole point of it. Before Wheeler, dark beer meant brown malt dried over a hot kiln, and the colour arrived with the taint attached. After Wheeler, a brewer could put a very small quantity of very dark malt into a very large quantity of beer and get colour and roast and nothing else. It worked so completely that by 1828 Guinness had thrown out their brown malt entirely and gone over to it.

Roast and smoke are not the same event. Roasting is what heat does to sugars and proteins inside the grain — the same chemistry as coffee, toast and the outside of a joint. Smoke is particulate: solids from a fire landing on the outside of something and sticking. One is cooking. The other is contamination, and the drum was invented to keep them apart. To taste black malt and report smoke is to taste the pump clip.

What a November ale is actually for

The beers that genuinely belong to this fortnight are not trying to reproduce a bonfire. They are malt-led, ruby to tawny, and strong — Harvey’s Bonfire Boy is 5.8%, and that is about the middle of the range rather than the top of it. The warmth is alcohol and specialty malt: crystal, amber, chocolate, giving toffee and toasted bread and dried fruit.

And the reason is not poetic. It is that someone is going to stand in a wet field in Sussex for four hours in the dark. A strong, sweet, malty ale is insulation. It is a beer built to make being outside survivable, which is a completely different design brief from a beer built to taste like the thing you are standing next to.

The bonfire is already there. It is enormous, and it is on fire. Nobody in the field needs the drink to remind them of it.

The scented-candle school

The modern idea that a bonfire beer ought to taste of bonfire is largely an American craft invention, and it is done sincerely: beechwood-smoked malt, peated malt, chipotle, roasted pumpkin, marshmallow, occasionally liquid smoke. The aim is to put the sensory environment of the fifth of November inside the glass — November as a flavour, the way a candle is a flavour.

It makes good beer sometimes. But it is a pastiche of a tradition that never existed, assembled by people a long way from any bonfire, and it reverses four hundred years of British brewers working very hard in the opposite direction. The smoke in a Rauchbier is Franconian and deliberate and old. The smoke in a bonfire beer is a costume.

Which is the same test as ever. The beer that belongs to a night is the one made for the people standing in it. The beer that tastes like the night is made for people who are not there.
