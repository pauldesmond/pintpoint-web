Roast is not smoke

English malting spent two centuries getting the smoke out of beer on purpose. So why does a November ale still smell of a bonfire?

A trade newsletter went round the pub trade this week with a list of beers for the fifth, and it said something about Harvey’s Bonfire Boy that is worth stopping on. The black malt, it explained, gives the beer a delightfully smoky flavour. Harvey’s say much the same thing themselves: a small quantity of black malt, used to impart the suggestion of smoke, and an aftertaste they call superbly smoky. Which is odd, because black malt is the single ingredient in British brewing that exists because somebody was trying to get rid of smoke. No smoke goes anywhere near it. So where is the smoke coming from?

The two-hundred-year flight from smoke

Before clean fuel, kilned malt tasted of whatever had been burned under it, and the trade spent a long time trying to stop it. Wood was scarce in England by the sixteenth century, so maltsters burned straw, and coal where they could get it: taxed, filthy, and prone to leaving the grain tasting of the fire. Peat and turf were avoided outright, because the smoke got into the malt and stayed there. The trade had a word for what that did to the beer: the twang. It was not a flavour note. It was a fault.

The fix arrived in stages. Coke, which is coal cooked clean of its volatiles, was first understood as a product in 1603, two years before the Gunpowder Plot, and was being used to make pale malt by 1642. Indirect kilns followed, keeping the fire’s smoke away from the grain entirely. Anthracite was prized for the same reason: it burns with almost nothing coming off it. What those moves have in common is the direction of travel: away from the fuel showing up in the glass.

So for a long stretch of English brewing, a beer that tasted of the kiln was not characterful. It was a beer that had gone wrong somewhere, and the word for it was not a compliment.

1817: colour without fire

Which brings us to the black malt. In 1817, Daniel Wheeler took out British patent number 4112 for a new method of drying malt, and the machine in the patent is a revolving cylindrical iron drum, explicitly described as being like the ones already used for roasting coffee. Malt went in, the drum turned, the temperature went past 400°F, and what came out was nearly black without being charred, and without tasting of the fuel at all.

That is the whole point of it. Before Wheeler, dark beer meant brown malt dried over a hot kiln, and the colour arrived with the taint attached. After Wheeler, a brewer could put a very small quantity of very dark malt into a very large quantity of beer and get colour and roast and nothing else. It worked so completely that by 1828 Guinness had thrown out their brown malt entirely and gone over to it.

Roast and smoke are not the same event. Roasting is what heat does to sugars and proteins inside the grain, the same chemistry as coffee, toast and the outside of a joint. Smoke is particulate: solids from a fire landing on the outside of something and sticking. One is roasting. The other is exposure to smoke, and the drum was invented to keep them apart.

Which answers the question. The smoke in Bonfire Boy is an impression rather than an ingredient. Hard roasting drives the same dark, burnt, faintly acrid notes that woodsmoke carries, so a nose reads smoke where the kiln only ever put heat. Crisp, who make the stuff, put liquorice, smokiness and nuttiness in their own tasting notes for black malt, and they are describing a grain that has never been near a fire’s breath. The brewer is not making it up and neither is the drinker. What is worth knowing is that the flavour arrives by roasting rather than by any contact with smoke, which is the distinction two centuries of maltsters worked to win.

What a November ale is actually for

The beers that genuinely belong to this fortnight are not trying to reproduce a bonfire. They are malt-led, ruby to tawny, and strong. Harvey’s Bonfire Boy is 5.8%, which is about the middle of the range rather than the top of it. It was first brewed to mark the firefighters’ work at the brewery fire of July 1996, under the name Firecracker, and became Bonfire Boy later. The warmth is alcohol and specialty malt: crystal, amber, chocolate, giving toffee and toasted bread and dried fruit.

And the reason is not poetic. It is that someone is going to stand in a wet field in Sussex for four hours in the dark. A strong, sweet, malty ale is insulation. It is a beer built to make being outside survivable, which is a completely different design brief from a beer built to taste like the thing you are standing next to.

The bonfire is already there. It is enormous, and it is on fire. Nobody in the field needs the drink to remind them of it.

The other way round

The other route is to put real smoke in, and it is done sincerely: beechwood-smoked malt, peated malt, chipotle, roasted pumpkin, marshmallow, occasionally liquid smoke. The aim is to put the sensory environment of the fifth of November inside the glass, November as a flavour.

It makes good beer, and it is worth saying that it runs against the direction English malting travelled in for centuries, which is interesting rather than wrong. The smoke in a Rauchbier is Franconian and deliberate and old, and it is the point of the beer. The smoke in a bonfire beer is scenery, put there on purpose by someone who wanted it.

Two routes to the same impression, then. One runs through a drum built to keep the fire off the grain, and the smoke turns up anyway, as an echo of the roasting. The other puts real smoke in, on purpose, because somebody wanted it there. Both can stand beside a bonfire. It is worth knowing which one you are holding.

PINtPOINT holds Bonfire Boy, and will say so when it reaches a tap near you.
