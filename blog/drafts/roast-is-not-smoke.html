Roast is not smoke

Harvey’s Bonfire Boy has a smoky aftertaste and no smoked malt. The explanation starts with an invention that took the smoke out of British beer two centuries ago.

Harvey’s describe Bonfire Boy as having a superbly smoky aftertaste, and say a small quantity of black malt is there to impart the suggestion of smoke. There is something peculiar about that. Black malt owes its existence to a nineteenth-century invention designed to give brewers dark colour and roasted flavour without the smoke. The ingredient reached for to suggest a bonfire was developed to keep the fire out of the beer.

So what are we tasting?

1817: colour without fire

Before clean fuel, kilned malt tasted of whatever had been burned under it. Wood was scarce in England by the sixteenth century, so maltsters burned straw, and coal where they could get it. Peat and turf were avoided outright, because the smoke got into the grain and stayed there. The trade had a word for what that did to the beer, the twang, and it was not a flavour note. It was a fault. Coke, indirect kilns and anthracite all moved the same way: away from the fuel turning up in the glass.

Then the drum. In 1817, Daniel Wheeler took out British patent number 4112 for a new way of drying malt, and the machine in it is a revolving cylindrical iron drum, explicitly described as being like the ones already used for roasting coffee. Malt went in, the drum turned, the temperature went past 400°F, and what came out was nearly black without being charred and without tasting of the fuel at all.

That is the whole point of it. Before Wheeler, dark beer meant brown malt dried over a hot kiln, and the colour arrived with the taint attached. After him, a brewer could put a very small quantity of very dark malt into a very large quantity of beer and get concentrated colour and roast with the fire kept off the grain. It worked so completely that by 1828 Guinness had thrown out their brown malt entirely and gone over to it.

Roast and smoke are not the same event. Roasting is what heat does to sugars and proteins inside the grain, the same chemistry as coffee, toast and the outside of a joint. Smoke is what comes off a fire and settles on whatever is in its way. Hard roasting produces dark, burnt, faintly bitter notes that a nose can read as smoke, even though the kiln only ever put heat into the grain. Crisp, who make the stuff, put liquorice, smokiness and nuttiness in their own tasting notes for black malt, and they are describing a grain that has never been near a fire’s breath. The brewer is not making it up and neither is the drinker. The flavour simply arrives by roasting rather than by contact with smoke, which is the distinction two centuries of maltsters worked to win.

What a bonfire beer actually tastes like

The beers that belong to this fortnight are not trying to reproduce a bonfire. They are malt-led, and they run from a 4% red ale to an old ale over six. The warmth is alcohol and specialty malt, crystal and amber and chocolate, giving toffee and toasted bread and dried fruit.

The reason is not poetic. Someone is going to stand in a wet field in Sussex for four hours in the dark, and a sweet, malty ale is the obvious thing to be holding. That is a different design brief from a beer built to taste like the thing you are standing next to.

The bonfire is already there. It is enormous, and it is on fire. Nobody in the field needs the drink to remind them of it.

Line up the British beers called Bonfire and the pattern is not subtle. Harvey’s Bonfire Boy is an ESB, Three Acre’s Bonfire Beer an old ale at 6.7%, Westgate’s Bonfire Toffee a 4% red ale. Not one of them is a smoked beer.

The toffee gives the game away. Bonfire toffee is what the fifth of November actually tastes like in this country: treacle, dark sugar, parkin, the slab that takes a hammer and most of a filling. The brewers are not reaching for the fire at all. They are reaching for the stall standing next to it, and they get there with dark malt, which is roasting again.

When the smoke is real

Smoked beer is not missing from the catalogue: seventy beers in PINtPOINT are filed as Rauchbier, eleven of them Schlenkerla’s. It is not that nobody brews it. It is that none of the bonfire names went looking for it. The other route is to put real smoke in, and it is done sincerely, with beechwood-smoked malt, peated malt, occasionally liquid smoke, the aim being to put the sensory environment of the fifth of November inside the glass.

Schlenkerla’s own history reckons that every beer in central Europe once tasted of smoke, because every kiln was an open fire, though not everyone accepts it was quite that universal. The clean kiln ended it almost everywhere, and by 1900 Germany’s smoke kilns had all but gone. Two Bamberg breweries, Schlenkerla and Spezial, still dry their malt over a beechwood fire, and Schlenkerla’s history claims the pair of them as the only breweries in the world to have kept the fire kiln going without a break. So the smoke in a Rauchbier is Franconian and deliberate and old, and it is the point of the beer. The smoke in a bonfire beer is scenery.

Two routes to the same impression, then. One runs through a drum built to keep the fire off the grain, and the smoke turns up anyway, as an echo of the roasting. The other puts real smoke in, on purpose, because somebody wanted it there. Both can stand beside a bonfire. It is worth knowing which one you are holding.
