These aren't revivals — they're hauntings

"I long for the nostalgia, and the thought I might be drinking something resembling something my Dad drank. But in reality the 70s probably wouldn't be to my taste buds — I imagine the pale ale would be more Nicholson's / Greene King than Sierra Nevada. However I for one fully support breweries attempting it, even if it's just a thinly veiled reason to sell crap beer to mugs like me."

When a beer returns, what exactly has been resurrected — the recipe, the badge, the strength, the package, or just the feeling?

That single question is the spine of every British beer revival story right now. The answer is rarely simple, and the honesty with which brewers answer it varies wildly. Some are doing genuine archaeological work. Others are selling you a cartoon and a duty band.

The 6-tier honesty spectrum

Revivals fall on a gradient. The further from a marketing department, the more credible.

TierAnchorExampleHonesty
1Just the nameWatney's Party Seven (2021)Pure nostalgia branding
2"Updated recipe"Carling Black Label (Mar 2026, +0.7% ABV); Belhaven Twisted Thistle (Jan 2026, –1.6% ABV)Honest about commercial; dishonest about heritage
3Archive ledgerAllsopp's Double Diamond (1922 inspiration + Citra/Columbus/Mosaic)Heritage cosplay
4Surviving bottleTennent's 1868 stout commemorativeArtefact-anchored
5Scholar / homebrewRon Pattinson, Barclay Perkins blog, homebrewtalk.comDIY purity
6aContinuity by peopleBurton Bridge DBA — ex-Ind Coope brewers picking up DBA after Carlsberg killed it (Mar 2015)Humble continuity
6bContinuity by placeJennings (Cockermouth) — Canfield purchase 2024/25, 100 archive recipes, cask-firstPlace-based continuity
6cContinuity by region (corporate-licensed hybrid)Boddingtons (JW Lees brewing under AB InBev licence, late 2025) — Manchester yeast non-negotiableHybrid: mega-corporate IP, regional execution

The Allsopp's vs Burton Bridge contrast

Two beers. Same era. Opposite philosophies.

Allsopp's Double Diamond (Tier 3): Relaunched Oct 2024 as a 3.8% pale ale "inspired by 1922". Hops: Citra, Columbus, Mosaic. Jamie Allsopp's own launch text: "Revived as a 3.8% pale ale that drinks like a lager." The hop list is the 2010s American craft palette. The original Double Diamond was a strong IPA. The duty band did the writing.

Burton Bridge DBA (Tier 6a): Launched March 2015 by ex-Ind Coope brewers who had worked on the original. "It would be impossible to brew an exact replica, but we are sure it is as close as possible." 4.8%. Untappd: 3.51 average from 1,399 ratings. The drinkers who remember it call it "smooth, sweet, a little bitter" and "absolutely gorgeous bitter, rich and almost sticky on the palate."

One is selling you a 1922 ledger and a 2024 hop bill. The other is saying: we worked on this once, we know what it tasted like, and we got as close as we could.

The duty-reform structural argument

Since August 2023, UK alcohol duty is based on strength, not category. Below 3.5% is taxed at a lower rate. The recipe rewrite follows the spreadsheet, not the ledger.

Belhaven Twisted Thistle IPA: 5.6% → 4.0%. "Evolve our range." Carling Black Label: original ~4% → 4.7%. "Designed for today's beer market." Allsopp's Double Diamond: original ~6% → 3.8%. "Drinks like a lager."

The tax band is doing more editorial work than the brewers.

The Carlsberg villain pattern

The same corporate parent that kills heritage brands repeatedly creates the conditions for independents to revive them.

  • Carlsberg discontinued Ind Coope Draught Burton Ale (DBA) → Burton Bridge picked it up (Mar 2015)
  • Carlsberg Marston's closed Jennings Brewery (Cockermouth) Nov 2022 → Canfields bought it back
  • Carlsberg Marston's also recently axed Banks's Mild and Bombardier

Journalism-grade: kill the brand → let it be rescued by enthusiasts who do it better.

The hop list as honesty diagnostic

Within seconds of reading a revival's spec sheet, the hop list tells you almost everything.

Modern American "C-hops" (Citra, Centennial, Columbus, Cascade, Mosaic, Galaxy) are the 2010s craft-IPA palette. Classic English hops (Fuggles, Goldings, Challenger, Bramling Cross, Target, Pilgrim, plus the Central European Celeia / Saaz) are the 19th-and-20th-century British palette.

A heritage revival that uses American C-hops while claiming "as it was in 1922" is either being honest about reinventing the beer (Tier 2) or pretending the historical anchor is real when it isn't (Tier 3).

Jennings core lineup (revival of 1990s recipes): Fuggles, Goldings, Challenger, Celeia. Burton Bridge DBA: traditional Burton-Ale style. Allsopp's Double Diamond: Citra, Columbus, Mosaic. The hop list is the spec-sheet receipt of editorial intent.

The shelf-presence defence

Paul's own words again: "The beer is nothing special, but I do love seeing them in supermarkets with original(ish) branding, with the little cartoon fellow from the adverts. It may not be my choice, but I'd rather see that on the shelves than Fosters and Budweiser."

The piece can't be all cynicism. Cultural-visibility-of-heritage-brands has its own value even when the liquid is mediocre. Better a flawed Double Diamond next to John Bull's cartoon than another row of identical macro-lagers.

Verdict

What you actually want from a revival is honesty about which era is being revived, and which part of the beer is being brought back. The 6-tier spectrum is the map. The hop list is the receipt. The duty band is the invisible editor. And the best revivals — Burton Bridge DBA, Jennings under Canfield, True North's pre-1970s Stones — are the ones that say: we know we can't bring the past back exactly, but we can get close enough that the feeling survives.

The rest are hauntings. Nice packaging. Wrong beer.
