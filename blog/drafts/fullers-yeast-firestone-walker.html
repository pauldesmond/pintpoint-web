The Fuller’s yeast that went to California and never quite came home

Firestone Walker’s Double Barrel Ale took a gold medal at the Great American Beer Festival this month. The strain it was built on came out of Chiswick, it gave the brewery years of trouble, and what replaced it is still, in its co-founder’s words, “essentially a Fullers/London yeast”.

Everyone knows the hops went west. Cascade, Centennial, Citra: the American aroma hop is the most retold story in modern beer, and the British end of it — Fuller’s and Timothy Taylor’s as the beers American brewers were trying to answer — gets retold with it.

The yeast is the part nobody tells. It is also the part that never went home.

Why they chose it

David Walker, the English half of Firestone Walker and the W in its name, told us how the brewery picked the strain for Double Barrel Ale. It was not heritage and it was not homage. It was a tasting.

“The number given to the original DBA yeast (wyeast) we can’t recall at the moment, but it was a Fullers ESB yeast. Why did we use it? Because in beer trials, that’s the one we liked best even though the Chico yeast was cleaner.”

That last clause is the whole thing. Chico — the Sierra Nevada strain, the workhorse of American craft — was the cleaner ferment and they knew it. They took the London one anyway, because the beer was better.

What it cost them

A yeast that drops bright is a gift to a brewery reusing it. The same quality nearly sank the beer.

“The Fullers yeast was a prodigious producer and flocculator, which was beneficial when reharvesting yeast for future brews. But that same flocculation characteristic lead to Diacetyl issues.”

Diacetyl is the butterscotch fault, and the trap Walker describes is a precise one. Their lab could measure diacetyl in the tank and watch it fall during maturation. What it could not measure was the precursor — the compound sitting there invisibly, waiting. It found its moment at packaging, where a little oxygen turned it into the fault they thought they had already cleared.

“It was a great yeast when we could tame it, but a nightmare otherwise and we adjusted within a few years.”

Within a few years, then, the strain was changed. But read what it was changed into:

“We eventually morphed to a derivation of this and more reliable for our needs; now our OG House Ale yeast. Still essentially a Fullers/London yeast.”

Not replaced. Derived from. The house culture of one of California’s most decorated breweries is a London yeast that has been living in San Luis Obispo County for a quarter of a century. On the morning Walker wrote this, Double Barrel Ale took gold at the Great American Beer Festival.

How it got there

Fuller’s was available to an American brewer in the first place because of Jeffrey House, who founded Thames America Trading Company at the turn of 1980 and brought London Pride and ESB to the United States — ESB on draught from 1987. We have written before about the other direction of that trade, when House introduced Anthony Fuller to Fritz Maytag in 1985 and Anchor’s beers came back across the Atlantic in Fuller’s returnable kegs for a London pub trial in 1991.

What Walker adds is the part of the exchange that could not be shipped back. A hop is an ingredient; you buy it, you use it, you buy more. A yeast is a population. It arrives once, it adapts, and it becomes yours.

What this is not

It is not evidence that Fuller’s yeast is loose across American craft brewing generally. It is one brewery, one beer, one account from one of the two people who founded it, given to us with permission to quote. The original Wyeast strain number has not been recalled, so the paper trail stops at the name.

It is also not the Transatlantic Beer Bridge argument, which holds that Liberty Ale’s influence returned to Britain through the pale-and-hoppy movement. That one still rests on recollection and inference. This does not. Walker is describing his own brewhouse.
