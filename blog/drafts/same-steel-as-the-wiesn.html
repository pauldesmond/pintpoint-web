Same steel as the Wiesn. Different beer.

At noon on 19 September the new Lord Mayor of Munich drove a tap into the first barrel of the 191st Wiesn. About two months later Winter Wonderland opens: five steel loops, a £5 stein deposit and a Bavarian hall.

Hyde Park Winter Wonderland runs from 19 November 2026 to 3 January 2027. How much of it is actually Oktoberfest?

The five-loop coaster is the same one.

The rollercoaster

At Winter Wonderland the coaster is called Munich Looping. In Germany it is Olympia Looping, and the billing is the same at both ends of the trip: the ride’s own site calls it the world’s largest transportable coaster with five loops, and Winter Wonderland’s ride page calls it “the world’s largest transportable rollercoaster”. Wikipedia puts the whole structure at nine hundred tonnes.

It has been a fixture of Munich’s Oktoberfest since 1989, owned and operated by the Barth showmen family, and at Winter Wonderland since 2016, which the same history page gives as its first appearance anywhere in Britain. The design is Anton Schwarzkopf’s and Werner Stengel’s. Winter Wonderland markets it as “made famous at Oktoberfest”.

That is the part of Winter Wonderland that is Oktoberfest in the strongest sense: the Schausteller circuit. Olympia Looping’s own history page names the three events it appears at each year: Munich’s Oktoberfest, Hyde Park Winter Wonderland and the Wiener Prater. It is put up again at each by the same showmen who build and tear down the Wiesn’s steel rides, and the firm’s own imprint gives its registered address as Theresienhöhe 6, Munich.

One other ride carries a Wiesn name. Winter Wonderland’s Wilde Maus XXL Coaster stands in the Bavarian Village, and the Oktoberfest has a Wilde Maus of its own: oktoberfest.de, the city of Munich’s official site, describes it as a 1994 steel re-edition of the “Teufelskutsche” of 1934, four to a car, operated by Munich showmen. That is a shared name and a shared kind of ride. Neither operator’s page says they are the same machine, and the Munich one is the small historical mouse rather than an XXL. With the coaster, the showmen are named at both ends. Here the name is all that travels, as far as the pages go.

The XXL is the same kind of near miss. Winter Wonderland’s is a pendulum ride in Thrillville that its page says swings to 47 metres with a claw turning at 15 revolutions a minute. The Oktoberfest’s XXL Höhenrausch, on the city’s site, swings a gondola through 120 degrees at about 45 metres, twenty riders at a time, up to 4G and 120 km/h. Again neither page names an owner.

That is the shape of it across the park. Winter Wonderland’s site carries fifteen rides with a page each; the city’s Oktoberfest site lists about forty. One of the fifteen is documented as the Wiesn’s own machine, operated by the family that operates it in Munich. The rest share a name, a type or a theme.

How it got Bavarian

The Oktoberfest styling is not original to the park. Ride Rater, writing in December 2022, has the Royal Parks commissioning a small funfair in 2005 and 2006 that “generally passed without much attention”, and dates the change to 2007, when AEG Live and PWR were brought on board as organisers and, in its words, “it was the influence of Germany’s mighty Oktoberfest that began to transform Winter Wonderland into one of London’s biggest and most popular annual events”. The same piece has the event originally running from 1 December, with the mid-November start coming later.

The Bavarian Hall

Then there is the Bavarian Village, and inside it the Bavarian Hall. It is one address among many. Winter Wonderland’s site lists twenty-two places to eat and drink, and the Bavarian Village is one of them; the others run to Bar Ice, Bar Narnia, Thor’s Tipi Bar, the Princess Bar, Candy Cane Lane and an Après-Ski Party Resort. Some of them are worth the walk on their own terms. The Carousel Bar in Show Town is a working carousel with a bar on it, and the operator’s whole pitch for it is to spin slowly as you sip. That is a rarer thing to drink on than a long table.

This is the part that most obviously imports the shape of an Oktoberfest night: long tables, steins, hearty German food from Heidi’s Kitchen, and daily entertainment from Frontal and Luigi ‘The Machine’. It is family-friendly in the afternoon and tips toward adults in the evening. The operator’s own word for the chicken on its meal deal is “Oktoberfest-style”.

It is not a Wiesn tent. There is no official outpost status and no Maß of Festbier pulled under Munich rules.

Nor is it one in scale. The city of Munich’s own figures put the 2026 Wiesn on 34.5 hectares of the Theresienwiese’s 42, about 85 acres, with roughly 120,000 seats across its catering businesses and some 13,000 people working it. It counted 7.4 million visitors this September, a record, against 6.5 million the year before. The largest figure we have found for Winter Wonderland is older and smaller: Ride Rater has it over two million in 2010.

Other corners, Almhütte and Café Bavaria, push the Village further into Alpine-party territory, and the park does the same outside it: over in the Arctic Circle, Winter Wonderland’s Après-Ski Party Funhouse promises to transport you to the snowy mountains of the Alps. That is adjacent to Oktoberfest without being the same thing. Oktoberfest begins in September on a field in the middle of Munich, which is some way from a ski slope.

The beer

Munich’s Wiesn will only pour beer from six houses: Augustiner, Hacker-Pschorr, Hofbräu, Löwenbräu, Paulaner and Spaten. The Historisches Lexikon Bayerns has the state-owned Hofbräu added in 1952 and the city concluding supply contracts with the other five since then, and says Munich allows only these breweries to supply the festival. Albert’s Schloss and Kapital Sheffield both advertised all six official Oktoberfest beers this autumn.

At the Bavarian Village the operator promises steins, bratwurst, a German sausage and a hot mulled wine, and offers a meal deal with a choice of an alcoholic or non-alcoholic drink. Winter Wonderland’s 2026 Bavarian Village page names no brewery, beer or strength; the Village’s own FAQ calls the draught Bavarian Gold.

The stein is the tell. The Village’s FAQ says the draught is served in “our one-pint stein (568ml)”, against the Maß of a litre Munich pours, with a £5 deposit refunded when the stein comes back with its token. Other speciality beers and ciders can go in the same stein, the FAQ says, decanted from 500ml bottles.

The beer has a name anyway. Drinkers have been logging it on Untappd as Bavarian Gold, a 4.2% Helles carrying a one-line description: sold at Winter Wonderland, Hyde Park. Untappd files it under the Bavarian Village’s own name and credits the brewing to Camerons Brewery, which it lists in Hartlepool.

That is the limit of what is published. Bavarian Gold is named as the draught, and nothing in the pages we have checked identifies a Big Six Festbier at the Village, on draught or in a bottle. The Untappd entry is a drinkers’ record rather than the operator’s, and its Winter Wonderland check-ins end last New Year’s Eve, which is where the last season finished. This one has not opened. So it records what was poured, not what the taps will carry on 19 November.

What it costs

If the appeal is a taste of the Wiesn without going, the sums are worth setting out, because they do not all point the same way.

Getting in is cheap, and the box below has the detail: arrival slots from a pound, and a free entry ticket for anyone who spends £25 in advance. The rides are extra, and not trivially so. Winter Wonderland prices a standard ride on Munich Looping at £12 in advance. Eating is priced in the same register: a bratwurst meal deal from £12.50, the all-day Almhütte Experience from £42.

The beer is not cheap. The Village’s FAQ says a pint of Bavarian Gold cost £7.95 in 2025, and gives no 2026 price. The city of Munich publishes the Wiesn’s beer prices every year, and for 2026 a litre in the tents runs from €14.80 to €15.90. Scaled to the 568ml the Village pours, that is €8.41 to €9.03. Those are euros against sterling and a year apart, so the two do not line up exactly. What they do show is that the pint is not the cheap part.

What you save is the journey. Lighthouse, the hotel data firm formerly called OTA Insight, put Munich’s advertised room rates across the three festival weeks of 2025 at an average of €357, twenty per cent up on Oktoberfest 2024, with the middle week projected at €415, which it placed 153 per cent above the annual average. Those figures are advertised rates rather than what guests paid, and they ran in Hospitality Net in September 2025. Hyde Park asks a Tube fare and a pound.

None of that is a complaint. The question here is which parts of Winter Wonderland actually come from Munich, and the answer is one ride, a set of borrowed names and a lager whose brewer nobody publishes. Whether it is worth going is a different question, and that one is not ours to answer. A sense of the thing is a fair reason to go, and it does not have to be the same timber.

Nine hundred tonnes of coaster come off the Theresienwiese and onto lorries for Hyde Park. The Helles in the stein has a shorter journey, and Untappd starts it in Hartlepool.

If you’re going

19 November 2026 to 3 January 2027, closed Christmas Day. Green Gate for the Village. The ticket is for Winter Wonderland itself rather than the Village: the Village’s FAQ puts some arrival slots at a pound and others at £5.50 or £8.25 in advance, and that ticket then covers Bavarian Village, the Hall included. Winter Wonderland’s own page adds that an entry ticket does not guarantee entry to Bavarian Hall or the other Village venues.

Winter Wonderland’s page says Bavarian Hall closes at 5pm from Wednesday to Sunday and reopens at 6:15pm to anyone without a table package; the Village’s own site says Thursday to Sunday. The evening session, 6pm to 10pm, is over-18s only, with passport or driving licence checked on a scanner at the door.

The all-day Almhütte Experience gives reserved seating with four drinks and a meal deal per guest; a soft-drinks version is offered too.
