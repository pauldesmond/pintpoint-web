Same steel as the Wiesn. Different beer.

At noon on 19 September the new Lord Mayor of Munich drove a tap into the first barrel of the 191st Wiesn. About two months later a different kind of Munich ritual arrives in Hyde Park: five steel loops, a stein deposit, and a hall that smells of bratwurst and mulled wine.

Hyde Park Winter Wonderland runs from 19 November 2026 to 3 January 2027. Millions will go. A surprising amount of what they queue for is borrowed — sometimes quite literally — from the Theresienwiese. The question worth asking is a simple one: how much of this is actually Oktoberfest?

The honest answer splits three ways. The rides, a lot. The room, quite a bit. The beer, almost none.

Same steel

The giveaway has a name. At Winter Wonderland it is called Munich Looping. In Germany it is Olympia Looping — the world’s largest transportable rollercoaster, five inversions, roughly sixty-five metres of height, and about nine hundred tonnes of kit to move.

It has been a fixture of Munich’s Oktoberfest since 1989, owned and operated by the Barth showmen family of Bonn, and at Winter Wonderland since 2016. Designed by Anton Schwarzkopf and Werner Stengel, it is not a London-themed pastiche. It is the German ride. Winter Wonderland markets it as “made famous at Oktoberfest” because that is where most of Europe first saw it.

London renames it — “Olympia” sits awkwardly after 2012 — but the trains are the same, and in busy seasons it runs longer ones to move the queue. The machine does not care about the weather. It loops.

That is the part of Winter Wonderland that is Oktoberfest in the strongest sense: not a franchise, not a licence, but the Schausteller circuit. In a typical year the coaster works German Volksfeste, peaks on the Theresienwiese, then goes onto lorries for Hyde Park at Christmas — put up again by the same showmen who build and tear down the Wiesn’s steel rides. The five loops that watched the Lord Mayor’s hammer in September are the five loops watching the Serpentine in December.

Same room, roughly

Then there is the Bavarian Village, and inside it the Bavarian Hall.

This is the part that most obviously imports the shape of an Oktoberfest night: long tables, steins, hearty German food from Heidi’s Kitchen, and live music that runs from traditional folk songs to German pop. On the outdoor stages the playlist leans toward pop, Motown and soul. From around 5pm the hall leans into an Oktoberfest-style party with music, visuals and snow effects. It is family-friendly in the afternoon and tips toward adults in the evening — two-year-olds and seventy-two-year-olds on the same dancefloor.

It is not a Wiesn tent. There is no official outpost status, no Augustiner crest on the canvas, no Maß of Festbier pulled under Munich rules. What it imports is long tables, loud entertainment, food that reads as Bavarian, and a crowd of toddlers and grandparents. That is the Volksfest habit, not the Christmas-market habit.

New corners — Almhütte, Café Bavaria, après-ski energy elsewhere on site — push the Village further into Alpine-party territory. That is adjacent to Oktoberfest without being the same thing. Oktoberfest is a beer festival that grew a fair. Winter Wonderland is a Christmas fair that rented a beer hall.

Different beer

Here is where the honesty from the UK Oktoberfest list belongs.

Munich’s Wiesn will only pour Festbier from six houses: Augustiner, Hacker-Pschorr, Hofbräu, Löwenbräu, Paulaner, Spaten. At Albert’s Schloss and Kapital Sheffield, the whole argument of the night is whether those names are actually on the bar.

At the Bavarian Village the house pour is a Helles-style lager at 4.2%, sold in a one-pint stein with the familiar refundable deposit. That is a Christmas operation with a German accent, not a Festbier pilgrimage.

So the DNA is unmistakable — and it is concentrated in the kit and the room, not the liquid. Hyde Park has borrowed Oktoberfest’s travelling steel and Oktoberfest’s evening choreography. It has not borrowed Oktoberfest’s beer list.

How much, then?

It is not an official Oktoberfest outpost. It does not pretend to be one. It is something more interesting for a beer site: proof that what travels most easily from the Theresienwiese to London is not the beer. It is the fair.

This year: 19 November 2026 – 3 January 2027, closed Christmas Day. Green Gate for the Village. Evening sessions are over-18s, and the stein carries a £5 deposit you get back. Bring a coat. Ask what’s in the stein.
