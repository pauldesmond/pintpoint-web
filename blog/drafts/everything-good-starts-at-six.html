Everything good starts at 6%

In 2005 an English beer writer went to Denver, found the Americans crediting England, and found one thing missing: a beer you could drink all evening.

The compliment and the complaint arrived in the same paragraph.

Pete Brown went to the Great American Beer Festival in Denver in the autumn of 2005 and wrote it up for the British Guild of Beer Writers. The compliment was about us. “The English scene was the original inspiration behind this extraordinary celebration of beer,” he wrote, and England was honoured that week when Michael Jackson was given a Lifetime Achievement Award on the Denver stage.

What was missing

Then the complaint, and it was about them. The only thing missing in the United States, Brown found, was a low-ABV session beer, because “everything good starts at 6%”. Charlie Papazian, the festival’s mastermind, had that as the next thing to do: “This is the next challenge, the next big thing.” Brown’s own prediction for the following year’s festival was a run at “the last remaining style unconquered by the Americans”, which is to say real ale.

That is an odd shape for a trade in flavour. By 2005 the hops had gone one way and the strength had stayed at home.

Catty

Why British brewers had not wanted the American hops has a good answer, and it came up in the comments under an argument about what to call the new British pale beers in August 2010. Rob Sterowski, whom Dredge answers in the thread as Barm, made the distinction that matters. “British brewers used US hops in the past, but they didn’t actually want the characteristics of those hops in the beer,” he wrote. “Their flavour was described as ‘catty’ and brewers took care not to let the US hops overpower the English ones. It took the American brewers to teach people to appreciate those piney, resiny or citrussy hops.”

In the same thread he said what had stayed British, and it was not a hop. Oakham JHB, he wrote, “typifies what is uniquely British about pale ‘n’ hoppy: it’s session strength, to fit with local drinking habits.” Mark Dredge, whose post it was, put the style at 3.5 to 5 per cent and drunk “by the bucket load”. Session strength was what Brown had missed in Denver.

In a British pub

Tandleman was at the Port Street Beer House in Manchester in June 2011 when Sierra Nevada Torpedo came out on cask, to “oohs and ahs” from the room. Torpedo is 7.2 per cent, so what it borrows is the dispense and not the strength.

By August 2013 an American brewmaster had made a British cask beer in Suffolk. The Beer Nut found it at the Wetherspoon in Gatwick Airport: “Firestone Walker American Independence on cask. This was put together by Matt Brynildson of California’s Firestone Walker at the Adnams brewery in Suffolk. It’s 5% ABV, so nicely quaffable.” Five per cent sits at the top of the range Dredge gave rather than at the bottom, and a late call for the flight let him have two in quick succession.

And back the other way

Greg Engert, who runs the Washington beer bar Churchkey, told Matthew Curtis in Good Beer Hunting in June 2018 why he pours classic British styles beside the modern ones. “We are awash in hops Stateside, so these styles provide welcome variation.”
