Winter is not Christmas

In 1971 Young’s gave an old beer a new name and a season. Four years later Fritz Maytag visited the brewery while looking for something else.

In October 1975 Fritz Maytag walked round Young’s Ram Brewery in Wandsworth, went home to San Francisco and brewed a Christmas beer because, as far as he could discover, nobody was making one. Young’s had Winter Warmer on at the time. It does not catch him out, because the two are not the same beer, and the difference is worth more than the catch.

Ram Brewery, 1971

Winter Warmer had not been called that for long. It was a Burton ale, the old strong dark English style that all but died through the sixties as drinkers went to bitter and lager, and by the end of that decade Young’s was close to the last brewery in England still making one. In 1971 they renamed it Winter Warmer and gave it a season.

That is what a brewery does to a beer it is trying to save. Put a name on it that tells a drinker when to want it, and a survivor of a dying style becomes a beer with a date. Whether the whole category of winter warmers dates from that one decision is more than the record will carry, but Young’s own does, and it was four years old when Maytag went looking for something else.

Harvest, then cold

Two things made midwinter the moment for the best beer of the year, and neither was sentiment. The first was the grain: by late October the harvest was in, malted and dry. The second was the cold. Summer brewing spoiled before refrigeration, and many regions banned it outright, while crisp autumn air chilled the wort and allowed long, clean fermentations.

The monasteries are the third reason, and the one to handle carefully. The story everybody tells is that Advent meant fasting, but liquidum non frangit ieiunium, liquid does not break the fast, so the brothers drank their dinner; and that the cellar ran three grades, the strongest for the abbot and the thinnest for the pilgrims at the gate. The Latin tag is real, but as a formal ruling it belongs to a seventeenth-century argument about whether chocolate broke a fast, centuries after the monks who are always said to have relied on it. The three grades come from the Plan of St Gall, a ninth-century architectural drawing, and as the beer historian Martyn Cornell spent years pointing out, a plan is not a building.

What survives the scrutiny is narrower. Hospitality in a Benedictine house was a rule, chapter 53 requiring that every guest be received as Christ. What came out of the cellar for a midwinter guest is not recorded.

England’s own winter drink was not a beer so much as an event. The wassail bowl held strong spiced ale with sugar, nutmeg and ginger, and roasted crab apples dropped in hot until they burst, the pale froth giving the drink its name, lamb’s wool. On Twelfth Night it went out to the orchards, where drinkers poured ale over the apple roots and fired shotguns into the branches to frighten off whatever might spoil the crop. It had a commercial life after that: the beer writer Will Hawkes found the Phillips Brewery advertising its Christmas specialities in the Oxford Times in December 1888, and reckons Victorian winter beers often ran past 10%. By the twentieth century it survived mainly as a line in a carol, which is why 1971 is a renaming and not a revival.

Leadenhall, ten o’clock

Winter still begins unofficially, on whichever late-October morning Young’s sends the horses out, this year the twenty-third, carrying the first barrels of Winter Warmer to The Lamb Tavern in Leadenhall Market. The market’s own billing has it arriving at ten, the first pint poured on the Victorian cobbles and a full English laid on for anyone up early enough to drink it. The White Cross in Richmond runs its own from eight, Morris dancers in the road, for the thirty-fifth time this October.

Young’s is not the only old English brewer still keeping a winter beer, only the one that gives it a morning. Fuller’s pours Old Winter Ale at 5.3% and Shepherd Neame keeps its Christmas Ale to draught in December, while Greene King’s Fireside puts grapefruit in a winter beer.

Sussex has the ritual. Harvey’s of Lewes had been brewing Elizabethan Ale since 1953 when head brewer Anthony Jenner adapted it, in 1972, into something darker and dry-hopped for the season, and called the result liquid Christmas Pudding. When the brew is mashed, hops are laid around the mash tun and the beer is blessed. Miles Jenner’s favourite blessing was written by the Rev. Judith Egar. A year after Young’s named a season, Harvey’s named a pudding.

Which is the test. The beer writer Phil Mellows, asked last December to pick ten Christmas beers anyone could find on a supermarket shelf, put the shortest one anybody has offered: “Will it stir into a Christmas pudding?” Strength is not his objection. A cask ale a little over 4% “isn’t a problem as long as they’ve got the flavour”; the danger is “sticking a bit on tinsel on it and calling it Christmas”, and a festive tag on a beer no different from what you would drink all year is “just a marketing thing”. The beer he reaches for as the obvious answer is Harvey’s.

And the winter the beer is named for is getting less reliable. Jeff Alworth, writing on Beervana on 23 October this year, went looking for the cold wet afternoon that makes a drinker want a porter and found the thermometer in the mid-seventies instead. He ordered the porter anyway.

Winter beer follows the harvest, the calendar and the drinker, and the three of them no longer arrive together. Two of them can be relied on.
