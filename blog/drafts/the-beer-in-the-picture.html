The beer in the picture

Behind the sign-painter in Hogarth’s Beer Street there is a lettered board. Four of its letters can still be read on his own copper plate, and they are the start of a London porter brewer’s name.

The thin man on the ladder at the left of Beer Street is the only hungry person in it.

Everyone else is enormous. That is Hogarth’s joke, and it is the one thing everybody takes from the print: beer makes a nation fat and cheerful, gin kills it, and the two sheets were published together on the first of February 1751, in the year of the Gin Act. He is a sign-painter, and he is starving because nobody in a prosperous street needs new signs painted.

Behind his palette, propped against the wall, there is a second board. It has writing on it.

Four letters

The writing is small, and in the printed impressions it does not survive. We looked at three in the Metropolitan Museum, a second state and two thirds, and then at a higher-resolution copy from the Wellcome, and the lettering is plainly there, in capitals, on at least two lines, and will not resolve into words.

What does resolve is the copper plate. The Met has Hogarth’s own engraved plate for Beer Street, and lettering cut into metal photographs better than lettering printed in ink. Mirrored, so that it reads the right way round, the upper line of the board gives up four letters.

C A L V.

Two people read it independently, one of them without being told what to expect, and got the same four. Nothing after the V survives: a scaffold plank crosses the line and takes the rest.

This is not a discovery so much as a confirmation. The British Museum’s catalogue of satirical prints transcribed the board in 1877, and Austin Dobson noticed it again in 1906. What appears to have happened since is that beer writing stopped reading the picture. The argument about what Beer Street is drinking has run for decades on the strength of the figures, the barrels and the mood, and the print has had a brewer’s name painted in it the whole time.

Calvert’s, and what the trade called it

There were two Calverts, second cousins, and in 1748 they were the first and third biggest porter brewers in London. William Calvert at the Hour Glass on Upper Thames Street brewed 55,700 barrels that year. Felix Calvert at the Peacock in White Cross Street brewed 53,600. Alan Pryor sets the rivalry out in his history of the London brewing trade for the Brewery History Society.

William was the better known. He was an alderman, and Lord Mayor in 1748, and the trade called him the Alderman Brewer. Pryor quotes a warning to the public of the period not to trust the signs that were hung out of the Alderman’s Entire Butt Beer. Signs, in the plural, hung out, in his name. That is the world the board in Beer Street belongs to.

And the phrase was already fixed. Pryor found it twice in the Daily Advertiser. On 24 January 1743 a man announces that he has opened the Sun Tavern in the Strand and provided good wines and other liquors with Calvert’s Entire Butt Beer. On 20 October 1744 the Old Swan and Rummer in Finch Lane has its vaults filled with Calvert’s Entire Butt.

Entire butt was the period name for the beer we now call porter. So a board reading Calvert’s in a London street scene of 1751 is not decoration. It is the biggest porter brand in the city, painted where a Londoner would expect to see it.

What the catalogue says, and why it matters

The 1877 British Museum reading, as it has reached us, is Calvert’s Best Butt Beer. Dobson in 1906 has entire butt beer. The newspapers Pryor found say Entire Butt, twice, eight years before the print.

If the catalogue is right and the board says Best where the trade said Entire, then Hogarth changed one word of a slogan every Londoner knew, which is a different and better fact than quotation. If Dobson is right, he copied it.

One word, and the two readings mean opposite things. This piece does not claim to settle it.

What the first reader saw

Someone did write about Beer Street in 1751, in a pamphlet called A Dissertation on Mr. Hogarth’s Six Prints, dedicated to the Lord Mayor. The Library of Congress has a copy. Beer Street gets five pages, and it is pure beer patriotism.

He arrives out of Gin Lane in the best transition any writer has been handed: the fumes of that stupifying liquor are in his head, and for fear of tumbling into some stinking gin shop he takes a walk into Beer Street instead. There he finds the people healthy, the children plump, the men hale, and the women with their true natural complexion.

He reads the signs, and lists them: the Jolly Tinkers, the Jolly Sailors, the Jolly Coopers, the Barley-mow. He watches the workers stop and sends the full joram round, a joram being a large drinking bowl. And then he does the arithmetic that makes the whole print an argument.

If then I can allay my Thirst and refresh my Spirits at the Expence of Three Half-pence only for a Pint of Beer, I shall never be so complaisant to the French, as to give them Eighteen-pence for the same Quantity of their Wine.A Dissertation on Mr. Hogarth’s Six Prints, 1751, p. 33

Three halfpence against eighteen pence. Twelve to one, printed in the same year as the picture, which is as close to a contemporary pub price as this argument ever gets.

What he does not do, in five pages of looking very hard at the street, is mention the board. No Calvert, no butt beer, no sign-painter. The first person known to have written about Beer Street walked straight past the one thing in it that names a beer.

Hogarth had done it before

Or rather, it had been done to him. In 1733 a plagiarised version of A Harlot’s Progress appeared, and whoever made it added words to the sign of the Bell public-house: Parson’s Intire Butt Beer. Humphrey Parsons was the porter brewer Calvert overtook. Pryor reproduces the original and the copy side by side, and makes the point that for the joke to land at all, those words had to be instantly recognisable.

So painting a porter brewer’s slogan into a Hogarth street scene was a thing people already understood, eighteen years before Beer Street. That is the context that turns the board from a curiosity into a deliberate act.

What else is written in the street

Once you start reading the signs rather than the figures, the print fills up.

The sign-painter is finishing a tavern board that reads Health to the Barley Mow, which is not a name but a toast, and the opening of a cumulative drinking song whose earliest relative is in print by 1609. The pamphleteer lists the Barley-mow among the signs, so the sign and the toast were both legible to a reader in 1751.

The tavern with the scaffolding carries a sun in splendour, a face with rays, which makes it the Sun. The pamphleteer does not mention that one either, and it is the largest sign in the street.

And the pawnbroker, over on the right, is the only business in Beer Street that is falling down. In Gin Lane he is the only one thriving. That is the whole diptych in two buildings, and it is made of signage.

Beer Street, 2026

On 19 February this year, at Liverpool Beer Festival, CAMRA named its Champion Winter Beer of Britain. It went to a four per cent bitter from a brewery in north London.

It is called Beer Street. London Brewing have been making it since they founded the brewery in 2011, and their own note on it begins: named after Hogarth’s famous print. CAMRA described it as an amber rye bitter; the brewery calls it a best bitter, brewed with Goldings and Fuggles, with toffee, dried fruit, dark chocolate and a nutty rye finish. Their director Senan Sexton said they were absolutely delighted. The branch magazine’s regional director added that they are the only London brewer ever to have won it, which is his claim rather than CAMRA’s.

Hogarth put a London porter brewer’s name into Beer Street in 1751 and nobody wrote it down for a hundred and twenty-six years, and then stopped again. Two hundred and seventy-five years after he published it, a London brewery put Beer Street on a cask and won the winter championship of Britain with it.

The beer is still in the picture. It is just very small, and nobody looks.
