PINtPRESSEssay19 November 2026 · Henley-on-Thames · Breweries

Established 1779. Brewing at New Street stopped in 2002.

The date on the Brakspear badge is not the date the brewery began. There was already a brewery. 1779 is the year a young man named Robert Brakspear went to work for his uncle in it.

The Brewery History Society has the business founded in 1756 by Richard Hayward at the Cross Keys in Bell Street, Henley-on-Thames, and joined by Robert Brakspear at about 1779. Grace’s Guide names Hayward as his uncle, and has Robert arriving from a different pub of the same name, the Cross Keys at Witney, where he had been landlord since 1769. Hayward died in 1803 and Robert became sole owner.

The other thing on the badge is a bee. The family was distantly related to Nicholas Breakspear, the only Englishman to become pope, and Paul Bailey, writing the firm’s history on his own blog in November 2014, has Adrian IV wearing a bee on his mitre as a play on the B at the front of his name. The bee is still the company’s mark. The managed division it has been buying hotels into is called Honeycomb Houses.

Whitbread

In the 1960s Brakspear went under the Whitbread umbrella, the arrangement by which a national brewer took a minority stake in a regional one to keep a predator off it. Bailey has Whitbread holding about twenty-seven per cent for a decade or so, and is specific about the fear it was bought to answer: an outside speculator who, the company believed, would buy it, close the brewery and sell off the pubs. Whitbread sold the holding when it got out of brewing.

New Street, 1993

In the first week of July 1993, with the town already hung with bunting for the regatta, Michael Jackson walked round the New Street brewery for The Independent.

The brew kettle was copper-domed and riveted rather than welded. The malt went up by the sack on a hoist from a wood-and-glass cabin on the front of the building. The water was chalk-hard, from the Chilterns by way of the brewery’s own well. The hops were Kent Goldings, Herefordshire Fuggles and a dash of Slovenian Styrians, in flower rather than pellet. The barley was Maris Otter, which Jackson noted was already becoming rare and expensive. A bush of holly and mistletoe hung from the eaves all year to ward off misfortune, and nobody knew when the habit had started, only that the bush is in a photograph from 1910.

In the pumphouse an 1890s steam engine had been restored to the last brass bolt and was set running, in his words, “for no purpose other than its own beauty”.

Beer started in circular oak tuns and dropped into square vessels below, the double drop, which leaves the tired yeast and the trub behind. The yeast was a symbiosis of strains whose distant origins were at Mann, Crossman and Paulin of Mile End, in the East End of London. The ordinary bitter was about 3.4 per cent. The special was 4.5.

John Mortimer had written in the New York Times in 1986 that Brakspear’s was the best bitter to be had in England, and thought it tasted of “hop fields in the English summer”. Jackson took him to mean the ordinary rather than the special, and said that if so, he agreed.

Closing the brewery

Brewing in Henley stopped on 17 October 2002, in Bailey’s account, and the site was sold for development. Part of it is a Hotel du Vin now and part of it is flats, and the arch still says Henley Brewery. W.H. Brakspear & Sons kept the beer brands and licensed the brewing of them to Refresh UK, owner of the Wychwood brewery at Witney. What was left operated, in the Morning Advertiser’s account four years later, around a hundred leased and tenanted pubs.

JT Davies

On 21 November 2006 the Brakspear board agreed to sell itself. The Morning Advertiser reported a 915p-a-share offer from JTD Investments, part of the JT Davies Group, valuing the company at around £106m. The offer was declared unconditional on 15 January 2007, and that, rather than the November offer, is the takeover.

JT Davies had been run by the same family since 1911, owned fifty-one tenanted pubs in London and the south-east, and had built a thirty per cent stake in Brakspear over several years. Those fifty-one pubs were rebranded Brakspear, which is why a Henley name ended up over doors in south London.

Davies said at the time that the deal meant Brakspear “can continue under a family ownership structure, helping to preserve the heritage and tradition of the business”.

The beer

Witney was not the first stop. While a site near Henley was looked for, Bailey has much of the beer brewed at Burtonwood in Cheshire. Brewing began at Witney in 2004, and CAMRA records what it took to get it there: Rupert Thompson’s Wychwood spent £300,000 moving six double-drop fermenters from Henley to Oxfordshire.

Witney held it for nineteen years. Since Carlsberg Marston’s closed Wychwood in 2023 the beers have been brewed at Burton upon Trent by Carlsberg Britvic. Since September 2026 they have been made at Hogs Back in Tongham, Surrey, which installed double-drop fermenters for them and uses the original Brakspear yeast strain. Hogs Back is Rupert Thompson’s too, and CAMRA calls the move to Tongham the second time he has taken on the stewardship of Brakspear’s beer.

The flagship is Original Gravity, a 3.4 per cent bitter brewed to the Brakspear Bitter recipe and sold as Gravity from 2019. That is the strength Jackson recorded at New Street, by the same method, on the same yeast. Beside it is Noble Nectar, a 4 per cent pale ale hopped with Farnham White Bine grown on the farm next to the brewery.

The pubs

Brakspear’s own site says it runs 125 pubs. Turnover for the year to 28 December 2025 was £47.9m, up sixteen per cent, with the managed division, Honeycomb Houses, reporting twenty-three per cent sales growth. That division has been buying: three Surrey pubs from Grosvenor Pubs & Inns in March, and the Grade II listed Stroud Hotel, which reopened under the brand on 10 November, taking it to thirteen sites.

It has also put two padel courts in at the Frogmill in Gloucestershire, with planning applications in for two more sites. Chief executive Tom Davies gave his reason: “We want to have as many different income streams as possible because just opening and servicing food and drink is limiting.”

Crazy Bear

On 27 October administrators from BDO were appointed to the two companies behind the Crazy Bear hotels at Stadhampton in Oxfordshire and Beaconsfield in Buckinghamshire. Both closed the same day, and two days later Brakspear bought the freeholds, £2.5m for Beaconsfield and £850,000 for Stadhampton. Of about a hundred and thirty staff, the majority were made redundant. The administrator’s account of the sale was that it had secured the buildings, and “it has not been possible to preserve the hotel operations”.

The Perch and Pike

Forty of those pubs have a page here, and a Brakspear beer has been logged at thirty-five. One is the Perch and Pike at South Stoke. In July 1993, Michael Jackson found Fred Douglas in the brewery’s joiners’ shop, painting three perch and a pike for the pub. The joiners’ shop is gone. The Perch and Pike is still a Brakspear house.

Dates

1756. Richard Hayward founds the business at the Cross Keys, Bell Street, Henley. c.1779. His nephew Robert Brakspear joins him. 1803. Hayward dies; Robert takes it on.

1812. Brewing concentrates on the New Street site by the Thames. 1896. The company is registered.

1960s. Whitbread takes about 27 per cent under the “umbrella”, against a feared bid that would have closed the brewery.

July 1993. Michael Jackson walks the brewery for The Independent. The ordinary bitter is 3.4 per cent.

17 October 2002. Brewing in Henley ends and the site is sold for development. The brands stay with W.H. Brakspear & Sons, who license the brewing to Refresh UK. 2004. Six double-drop fermenters move to Witney.

21 November 2006. The board agrees a 915p-a-share offer from JT Davies, valuing Brakspear at about £106m. 15 January 2007. The offer is declared unconditional. Fifty-one Davies pubs take the name.

2013. Brewing returns to Henley on a small scale at the Bell Street Brewery, in the company’s own Bull.

2023. Wychwood closes and the beer goes to Burton. September 2026. It moves to Hogs Back at Tongham, back on double drop.

27 and 29 October 2026. The Crazy Bear hotels close and their freeholds are sold to Brakspear for £3.35m.
