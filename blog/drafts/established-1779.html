PINtPRESSEssay19 November 2026 · Henley-on-Thames · Breweries

Established 1779. The brewing stopped in 2002.

The date on the Brakspear badge is not the date the brewery began. There was already a brewery. 1779 is the year a young man named Robert Brakspear went to work for his uncle in it.

The Brewery History Society has the business founded in 1756 by Richard Hayward at the Cross Keys in Bell Street, Henley-on-Thames, and joined by Robert Brakspear at about 1779. Grace’s Guide names Hayward as his uncle and has Robert coming from the Cross Keys at Witney, where he had been landlord since 1769. Hayward died in 1803 and Robert became sole owner. The family name went over the door of a going concern.

It has now outlasted the brewing by twenty-four years.

What it was, when a stranger looked at it

In the first week of July 1993, with the town already hung with bunting for the regatta, Michael Jackson walked round the New Street brewery for The Independent. What he wrote down is the best inventory of the place anyone is going to get now.

The brew kettle was copper-domed and riveted rather than welded. The malt went up by the sack on a hoist from a wood-and-glass cabin on the front of the building. The water was chalk-hard, from the Chilterns by way of the brewery’s own well. The hops were Kent Goldings, Herefordshire Fuggles and a dash of Slovenian Styrians, in flower rather than pellet. The barley was Maris Otter, which Jackson noted was already becoming rare and expensive. A bush of holly and mistletoe hung from the eaves all year to ward off misfortune, and nobody knew when the habit had started, only that the bush is in a photograph from 1910.

In the pumphouse an 1890s steam engine had been restored to the last brass bolt and was set running, in his words, “for no purpose other than its own beauty”.

The fermentation was the part that mattered, and still is. Beer started in circular oak tuns and dropped into square vessels below, the double drop, which leaves the tired yeast and the trub behind. The yeast was a symbiosis of strains whose distant origins were at Mann, Crossman and Paulin of Mile End, in the East End of London. The ordinary bitter was about 3.4 per cent. The special was 4.5.

On the beer itself Jackson was careful to give the credit away. John Mortimer had written in the New York Times in 1986 that Brakspear’s was the best bitter to be had in England, and thought it tasted of “hop fields in the English summer”. Jackson took him to mean the ordinary rather than the special, and said that if so, he agreed.

One detail from that morning has outlived everything else in it. Jackson put his head into the brewery’s joiners’ shop and found Fred Douglas, twenty-one years into painting the company’s signs, at work on a board five feet by four for the Perch and Pike at South Stoke. Douglas was an angler, so he had painted three perch and a pike with a rudd in its mouth.

The exit

Brewing in Henley stopped in 2002 and the site was sold for development. Part of it is a Hotel du Vin now and part of it is flats, and the arch still says Henley Brewery. The right to brew the brands went to Refresh UK, owner of the Wychwood brewery at Witney, along with some of the original plant. Brewing started at Witney in 2004.

What was left was a pub company with about a hundred pubs and a name on them.

Twenty years ago this week

On 21 November 2006 the Brakspear board agreed to sell itself. The Morning Advertiser reported a 915p-a-share offer from JTD Investments, part of the JT Davies Group, valuing the company at around £106m. The independent directors intended to recommend it unanimously. The price was a 4.5 per cent premium on the average share price of the previous twelve months.

JT Davies was not a stranger. It had been run by the same family since 1911, owned fifty-one tenanted pubs in London and the south-east, had built a thirty per cent stake in Brakspear over several years, and had approached the directors that September. Its chairman and managing director, Michael Davies, had sat on the Brakspear board as a non-executive since 2003. The fifty-one Davies pubs were rebranded Brakspear, which is why a Henley name ended up over doors in south London.

Davies said at the time that the deal meant Brakspear “can continue under a family ownership structure, helping to preserve the heritage and tradition of the business”.

Twenty years is long enough to see what that sentence bought.

The beer has moved four times

Witney held it for nineteen years. When Carlsberg Marston’s closed Wychwood in 2023 the Brakspear brands went to the former Banks’s brewery in Wolverhampton. CAMRA’s national chairman, Nik Antona, told the Oxford branch’s magazine that it was heartening to know “the iconic Brakspear Double Drop beer will live on at Banks’s”. It lived on there for under two years. Banks’s stopped brewing in 2025, after a hundred and fifty years, and the beer moved again, to Carlsberg’s rebuilt brewery at Burton-upon-Trent. Since September 2026 they have been made at Hogs Back in Tongham, Surrey, which installed double-drop fermenters for them and uses the original Brakspear yeast.

The flagship is Original Gravity, a 3.4 per cent bitter that was called Brakspear Bitter and was sold as Gravity from 2019. That is the same strength Jackson tasted in the oak tuns at New Street, made by the same method with the same yeast, sixty miles away, by a company that does not own the name.

What the name does for a living now

Brakspear runs about a hundred and twenty-five sites. Turnover for the year to 28 December 2025 was £47.9m, up sixteen per cent, with the managed division, Honeycomb Houses, reporting twenty-three per cent sales growth. That division has been buying: three Surrey pubs from Grosvenor Pubs & Inns in March, and the Grade II listed Stroud Hotel, which reopened under the brand on 10 November and took it to thirteen sites.

It has also put two padel courts in at the Frogmill in Gloucestershire, with planning applications in for two more sites. Chief executive Tom Davies explained why in a sentence that would have puzzled the joiners’ shop: “We want to have as many different income streams as possible because just opening and servicing food and drink is limiting.”

And then the bear

On 27 October administrators from BDO were appointed to the two companies behind the Crazy Bear hotels at Stadhampton in Oxfordshire and Beaconsfield in Buckinghamshire. Both closed the same day, every booking was cancelled, and of about a hundred and thirty staff the majority were made redundant. It was the group’s second administration in under two years; its own directors had bought it out of the first in a pre-pack for £6.5m in January 2025.

Two days later the freeholds were Brakspear’s, £2.5m for Beaconsfield and £850,000 for Stadhampton. The administrator’s own account of the sale is the plainest summary of what a pub company is buying when it buys like this: the sale secured the buildings, and “it has not been possible to preserve the hotel operations”.

The two get different futures. Beaconsfield, the dearer one, will be run: redeveloped from the second half of 2027, open in early 2028, a classic Honeycomb House with a possible spa, because a swimming pool came with it. Stadhampton, the cheaper one, is to be let, probably to an events operator, and Davies hopes to have it open by the end of March. The building with the wedding trade is the one being handed to somebody else.

Neither reopens as a Crazy Bear. Stadhampton is ten miles from New Street.

What lasted

Not the brewery, which is flats and a hotel. Not the beer’s address, which has changed four times in twenty-two years and is now in Surrey. Not the share register, which was bought out twenty years ago this Saturday. Not even the ownership of the name on the pump clip, which belongs to the people who brew it rather than the people who run the pubs.

What lasted is the estate. A hundred and twenty-five doors with the word on them, which is more than the hundred the company had when it stopped brewing.

Forty of those pubs have a page here, grouped by area from Henley outwards, and a Brakspear beer has been logged at thirty-five of them. One of the forty is the Perch and Pike at South Stoke, where in July 1993 a sign-painter of twenty-one years’ standing was finishing a board with three perch and a pike on it. That is what 1779 turned out to be worth: not a brewery, and not a beer, but a list of addresses.

The dates

1756. Richard Hayward founds the business at the Cross Keys, Bell Street, Henley. c.1779. His nephew Robert Brakspear joins him. 1803. Hayward dies; Robert takes it on.

1812. Brewing concentrates on the New Street site by the Thames. 1896. The company is registered.

July 1993. Michael Jackson walks the brewery for The Independent. The ordinary bitter is 3.4 per cent.

2002. Brewing in Henley ends and the site is sold for development. The brands are licensed to Refresh UK. 2004. Brewing starts at Witney.

21 November 2006. The board agrees a 915p-a-share offer from JT Davies, valuing Brakspear at about £106m. Fifty-one Davies pubs take the name.

2013. Brewing returns to Henley on a small scale at the Bell Street Brewery, in the company’s own Bull.

2023 to 2026. Wychwood closes, the beer goes to Wolverhampton, then Burton, then Hogs Back at Tongham in Surrey, back on double drop.

27 and 29 October 2026. The Crazy Bear hotels close and their freeholds are sold to Brakspear for £3.35m.
