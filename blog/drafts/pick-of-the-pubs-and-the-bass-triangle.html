The Green Triangle and the Oldest Trademark in British Beer

On 1 January 1876 the United Kingdom opened its first statutory trademark register. A man from Bass Brewery had spent the night on the pavement outside the office in Somerset House to be first in the door. When it opened, he registered a red equilateral triangle. That mark became UK Trade Mark No. 1.

A hundred and fifty years later, PINtPOINT put a triangle on its radar to mark the pub we think you should walk to next. It is not red. It is not accidental.

UK Trade Mark No. 1

The Trade Marks Registration Act of 1875 was the first British statute to create a formal register of commercial marks. Before it, English brand protection was a matter of common law and expensive lawsuits — you owned your mark only to the extent you could afford to defend it in court. The Act changed the game. A registered mark carried statutory protection; the register was public; and priority was, for the first time, a question of who queued.

Bass understood the stakes immediately. Its pale ale was already a global product — shipped to Trieste, Calcutta, New York and the Baltic, and imitated everywhere it went. Getting to No. 1 in the register was not a marketing stunt. It was a legal necessity. So a Bass employee slept on the pavement outside the Registry to make sure that when the door opened, his was the first application handed across the desk. The Bass red equilateral triangle became UK Trade Mark No. 1. A second Bass mark — the diamond, for its stronger export ale — became No. 2, registered the same day.

The story is well-worn now. It survives because it is true, and because it captures what a trademark actually is: not a picture, but a promise about who made the thing and how you can trust it. Bass understood geometry is defensible in a way that words are not. A word can be argued about, translated, mis-set in type. A shape either matches or it doesn't. A red equilateral triangle on a beer bottle either is Bass or is a fraud.

The most famous painting of a beer bottle

Six years later, in 1882, Édouard Manet finished A Bar at the Folies-Bergère. It hangs today in the Courtauld Gallery on the Strand. Behind the barmaid a mirror shows the crowded music-hall. In front of her, on the marble bar, sit two Bass Pale Ale bottles, red triangles facing the viewer.

Nobody had to explain the label. That was the whole point. A French painter, working in Paris, chose to put a British beer bottle on the counter of a Paris cabaret because he knew — and knew that his audience knew — what it was. The triangle had become a language.

Very few beer brands have ever earned that. The green Heineken star has. The Guinness harp has. The Stella chalice has. The Bass triangle got there first, by decades, and it did it with a shape a five-year-old could draw from memory.

Why a shape at all

The reason a well-chosen shape survives is the same reason road signs work. Under pressure — a busy bar, a moving car, a phone at arm's length, tired eyes at 11pm — a shape reads faster than a word. It reads faster than a colour, too, because a shape holds meaning even when the colour drifts. Print it on newsprint, photocopy it, view it through the cataract in your mother's eye. A triangle is still a triangle.

Anyone designing a product that has to be legible at a glance ends up thinking about this. The Bass triangle is the origin story every design school teaches. The lesson isn't "use a triangle." The lesson is: pick geometry that survives the worst-case viewing.

That worst case, for a phone app about pubs, is a pint into the evening, on a screen with a smudge, in a room that is a bit too warm, with someone talking to you.

What PINtPOINT's triangle points at

PINtPOINT's radar shows every pub within your chosen range as a round blip. A dozen or two in a busy area. Some are favourites you've hearted; some are venues at financial risk; some match the beer style you filtered for; most are just there.

One blip on the radar is not round. It is a green downward-pointing triangle. That is our Pick of the Pubs — the single pub, out of everything in range, that we think has the best board of beers for you tonight. It considers the depth of matches on the tap list, the freshness of the data, the pub's rating and, only as a gate, the distance. It is the answer to "where should I walk next." Not a list. One pub, with a shape.

We chose a triangle because it survives everything a round blip doesn't. It survives colour drift. It survives a small screen. It survives the fact that it is sitting on top of a moving radar sweep, at 40% opacity, in a rendering pipeline that has to serve users with a variety of vision, at all hours, in every light. The triangle is doing the same job in 2026 that the Bass red triangle did in 1876: carry a promise in a shape that survives.

Why not paint it red

Because red is already saying something else on our radar.

PINtPOINT flags pubs under real financial pressure — an at-risk status derived from the FPI (Financial Pressure Index) — and those blips flash amber and, at the worst end, purple. Red already means this pub might not be here next year. Putting a red triangle for the Pick of the Pubs would seat two "reds" side by side on the same map, saying two opposite things: this is the pub to walk to, and this pub is fucked. That would be a poor bit of communication, and it wouldn't be more Bass-ish for the trouble.

The homage to Bass is in the geometry, not the pigment. Bass proved that a triangle is enough. That is the lesson we borrowed. Our green stays green because green does the semantic work on our specific canvas: on PINtPOINT's map, green means go here. It is the same principle Bass applied — pick the shape that survives — dressed for a different room.

Copying the colour would be a stunt. Copying the discipline is the compliment.

Trademarks, promises, and the quiet nod

A trademark, at the end of the day, is a promise about origin. The Bass red triangle promised you that the pale ale inside the bottle was brewed in Burton by Bass, not imitated by a wharfside knock-off in Antwerp. Our green triangle promises you that the pub underneath it is one we've actually reasoned about, using real tap-list data and your own taste history, not a paid placement or an algorithmic accident.

Those promises are made in shapes because shapes travel. They travel across languages, across screens, across the sixteen decades between one geometric mark and another. The pub you walk to tonight is a smaller decision than exporting pale ale to Trieste. But the design principle is the same, and it is a good principle, and it deserved the nod.

FAQ

What is the Bass red triangle?

The trademark of Bass Brewery, established in Burton-upon-Trent in 1777. In 1876 it became UK Trade Mark No. 1 — the first mark ever registered under the Trade Marks Registration Act of 1875. Bass Pale Ale bottles carried it around the world, and Édouard Manet painted two of them onto the counter in his 1882 masterpiece A Bar at the Folies-Bergère.

Why is PINtPOINT's Pick of the Pubs a triangle?

The triangle survives on any screen and in any light. It is instantly distinguishable from the round blips that fill the rest of the radar, it works for colour-blind users because the shape carries the meaning as much as the colour does, and it points — literally downward — at the pub PINtPOINT thinks you should walk to next. The Bass triangle proved a hundred and fifty years ago that a geometric mark can carry a whole beer's identity. Ours does the same job at a smaller scale: this pub, tonight, out of everything nearby.

Why isn't PINtPOINT's triangle red like Bass's?

Green does the semantic work green needs to do. On the radar, red already means something else — pubs under financial pressure. A red Pick of the Pubs triangle would sit next to red at-risk blips and say two opposite things at once: this pub is the one to walk to, and this pub is in serious trouble. Green stays green because green means go. The homage to Bass lives in the shape, not the colour.

When did the UK trademark system start?

The Trade Marks Registration Act 1875 created the UK's first statutory register. It opened for business on 1 January 1876. Bass Brewery had a representative queue overnight outside the London registry to be first through the door; the Bass red triangle was assigned Trade Mark No. 1. Bass No. 2 was the Bass diamond, a second mark for its stronger export ales, registered the same day.

Which Manet painting shows Bass?

A Bar at the Folies-Bergère, painted in 1882 and now in the Courtauld Gallery in London. Two Bass Pale Ale bottles stand on the marble counter in front of the barmaid, red triangles facing the viewer. It is the most-cited proof that Bass had become a global product by the 1880s — recognisable enough that a French Impressionist could put the label in a Paris cabaret and expect you to know what you were looking at.

Is Draught Bass still available today?

Yes. Draught Bass is now contract-brewed by Marston's in Burton for AB InBev's Budweiser Brewing Group UK. It survives on hand pump in a network of pubs mapped and defended by the Cask Drinker Network. The pump count has more than tripled in the past year, from around 350 outlets to over a thousand. National Bass Day happens each spring.
