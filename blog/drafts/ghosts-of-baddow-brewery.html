Ghosts of Baddow Brewery

Baddow Brewery closed nearly a century ago. But its pub estate is still scattered across Essex — some still pouring, some converted, some demolished, and two still refusing to give up their secrets.

This is the sort of map PINtPOINT was made for: not just where to drink now, but where Essex used to drink.

At 47 Church Street in Great Baddow stands The Old Brewery: the surviving shell of Baddow Brewery, a local brewing business whose story runs from the late eighteenth century through to the age of tied houses, consolidation and closure.

The headline history is simple enough. Baddow Brewery was founded in 1798. A new brewery was built in 1868. In 1927 it was acquired by Seabrooke, and brewing at Great Baddow came to an end.

But the more interesting story is not just the brewery building. It is the network.

According to the Brewery History Society list, Baddow Brewery was associated with 61 pubs. The question is: how many are still alive?

The answer: 30 still appear to be trading

After working through the estate pub by pub, the current working count is:

30Open*
13Replaced
16Closed
2Unknown

That gives us a beautifully clean, slightly melancholy headline: of Baddow Brewery’s 61 listed pubs, 30 still appear to be trading. Not always under the same name. Not always with the same interior, ownership or beer. But still serving as pubs, bars, inns, hotels or pub-restaurants.

*Method note: “Open” includes renamed pubs, free houses, hotel bars and pub-restaurants such as Harvester — not only traditional wet-led locals.

Twenty-nine appear to have gone, been replaced, been converted, or ceased trading as pubs. Two remain unknown because the evidence is too thin to call honestly.

Accuracy note: this is a draft research classification, not a final legal/historic record. Where the evidence is weak, the status remains cautious. The two unknowns — Gardeners Arms, Kelvedon and New Inn, Pitsea — are deliberately left unknown rather than forced into a tidy answer.

That list is why the story works. Baddow Brewery is dead as a brewery company, but its estate has not vanished. Half of it is still visible in the modern pub map.

Some survivals are stranger than others. The White Hart at Brentwood is now Sugar Hut — Essex’s TOWIE nightclub, a former Baddow tied house reborn as reality-TV shorthand. Five Bells at Vange trades as a Harvester. The Bull at Pitsea gave its name to a housing estate called Bull Mews. This is what estate afterlives actually look like: not always graceful, rarely predictable, occasionally famous.

The two unknowns

The two awkward entries are Gardeners Arms, Kelvedon and New Inn, Pitsea.

Gardeners Arms is troublesome because ClosedPubs’ Kelvedon list and PubWiki both give fairly full Kelvedon lost-pub lists, and neither has a Gardeners Arms. Until a street, licensee or tithe entry turns up, it remains a name on a brewery list rather than a pinned house. New Inn, Pitsea is similarly polluted by other Essex New Inns, and the historic address is not yet nailed down tightly enough. So they stay unknown — not a failure of the map, but the map being honest.

Baddow brews again — but carefully

There is a lovely modern echo to the story.

Chelmsford Brew Co now brews in Great Baddow. Their own account says the site had to be Brewery Fields, the landscape of the original Great Baddow brewery in the 1800s. CAMRA’s visit write-up placed them only a stone’s throw from the old Baddow Brewery, and the early BlueShack story has exactly the right local-brewery texture: family, garden-shed brewing, recipes getting out of hand, beers appearing at The Lion Inn, The Ale House and Chelmsford Beer & Cider Festival.

That does not make Chelmsford Brew Co the same company as Baddow Brewery. It is not a corporate continuation. It is not the same old brewery building. And modern Chelmsford brewing is wider than one brewery, with Radio City Beer Works also part of today’s scene.

But it is still a beautiful ending:

Not the same company. Not the old brewery building. But new beer on old brewing soil.
