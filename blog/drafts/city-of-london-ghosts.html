City of London Ghosts

Turn on Ghost Hunter in PINtPOINT and the City changes.

The glass towers are still there. The office lobbies, coffee chains and polished bars are still there. But beneath them another City starts to appear.

Lost pubs. Vanished wine bars. Old lunchtime haunts. Drinking rooms whose names have been scraped off the glass but not quite out of memory.

Some are properly gone. Some became offices. Some are still bars, just under a different name. Some survive only in old directories, archived websites, Square Meal quotes, ClosedPubs entries and the memories of people who once knew exactly where to go after work.

That is what Ghost Hunter is for.

Not every place on PINtPOINT is there because you can still drink in it. Some are there because London forgot them.

The City had its own drinking geography

The City was never just pubs.

By the late twentieth century it had developed its own after-work and lunchtime drinking map: wine bars in basements, merchant cellars, banker pubs, champagne bars, steak-and-claret rooms and branded post-work chains clustered around stations, alleys and office blocks.

Davy’s had its sawdust, candles and cellars. Corney & Barrow had its City wine-bar circuit. Balls Brothers had addresses scattered across Cheapside, Bishopsgate, Mark Lane, Blomfield Street, Brook Street, Carey Lane, Southwark Street and beyond. El Vino, Bow Wine Vaults, Jamaica Wine House, The Don, La Cave, More Champagne Bar, The Fine Line — names that sound less like individual venues and more like a vanished operating system for City drinking.

A 2010 amenities list from around the Nomura move captures the density of it. All Bar One, Balls Brothers, Bow Wine Vaults, Corney & Barrow, Davy’s, El Vino, La Cave, More Champagne Bar, The Jamaica Wine House, The Vintry, The Rake and The Market Porter all appear inside the same practical map of where office workers might actually go.

It was not nostalgia then. It was infrastructure.

Balls Brothers and the old circuit

The archived Balls Brothers site is a gift from the old internet.

In early-2000s snapshots, the company describes itself as a long-established family-run firm with 19 wine bars and restaurants across the City and West End. The list already reads like a ghost map:

Cheapside. Bishopsgate. Blomfield Street. Mark Lane. King’s Arms Yard. The Hop Cellars. Hay’s Galleria. Cannon Street. Carey Lane. Lime Street. Brook Street. St James’s Street.

Some of those names still have traces. Some addresses point to completely different businesses now. Some seem to have been absorbed into the churn of City redevelopment. But the pattern is the thing: Balls Brothers was not one lost bar. It was a network.

And when a network disappears, the city changes quietly. Not with one dramatic closure, but with a hundred small replacements.

The name above the door changes. The lunch menu changes. The after-work crowd moves somewhere else. Then, after a few years, people forget there was ever anything there.

Not all ghosts are dead

That is the important bit.

A ghost venue does not always mean an empty building or a demolished pub. Some ghosts are still alive as rooms. They are just wearing someone else’s clothes.

A former wine bar becomes a cocktail bar. A City pub becomes a restaurant. A merchant cellar becomes a corporate event space. A familiar name gets folded into a newer brand.

Drake & Morgan, The Cocktail Club, Dirty Martini, All Bar One, Pitcher & Piano — these are not the same thing as Davy’s, Balls Brothers or Corney & Barrow. But they are part of the next layer. They show what the City drinking map became after the older one thinned out.

That makes the ghosts more interesting, not less.

Because sometimes the question is not what closed here?

It is: what did this place used to be?

Ghost categories

  • Gone completely — demolished, converted, redeveloped or erased.
  • Still hospitality — restaurant, event space, hotel bar or members’ room.
  • Still a bar, new name — the old drinking room survives under different signage.
  • Survivor — same name, same lineage or still recognisably part of the old map.
  • Uncertain — found in the archive, waiting for a present-day check.

ClosedPubs and the deeper City

Once you start looking beyond wine bars, the map gets bigger very quickly.

The ClosedPubs archive for EC2 alone is dense with lost names: Arbitrager, Bishop of Norwich, Black Raven, Bow Bell, City Tavern, Dragon, Finch’s, Flying Horse, Hand Pump, Last & First and many more.

Some closed recently. Some vanished decades ago. Some reopened. Some were renamed. Some exist now only as entries on archive pages, old pub guides, planning records and half-remembered directions from stations that themselves have changed.

This is where City of London Ghosts becomes bigger than one article about wine bars.

It becomes a way of reading the City.

Not as a fixed place, but as layers: Roman walls, medieval lanes, Victorian pubs, merchant wine cellars, 1980s wine bars, 2000s chain bars, post-crash closures, glass towers, hybrid-working gaps and new bars moving into old shells.

PINtPOINT can show the current map. Ghost Hunter lets it show the buried one.

Ghost Hunter

Ghost Hunter is a simple toggle in PINtPOINT.

Switch it on, and lost venues appear alongside the living ones: pubs, wine bars and drinking rooms that used to shape an area, even if they no longer trade under that name.

Some entries are closed. Some are re-skinned. Some are survivors. Some are uncertain and need checking.

That uncertainty is part of the point. Cities are messy. Records are incomplete. Websites disappear. Names change. Leases end. People remember different things.

But if we can map the traces — the old addresses, the archived listings, the Square Meal quotes, the ClosedPubs entries, the current occupants — we can make the ghosts visible again.

Not as recommendations.

As memory.

The City changes when you know what used to be there

Walk past a modern bar near Liverpool Street and it might just look like another after-work venue.

But if you know it used to be part of the old wine-bar circuit, it becomes something else. A continuation. A replacement. A disguise.

Walk down Cheapside, Bishopsgate, Mark Lane or Blomfield Street with Ghost Hunter switched on, and the City becomes double-exposed: the current street in front of you, and the drinking map underneath it.

That is the point of City of London Ghosts.

Not to pretend the old places were all better. Some probably were. Some probably weren’t. But they mattered. They formed habits, routes, rituals and memories. They were part of how the City worked.

And now, with a bit of digging, they can be found again.
