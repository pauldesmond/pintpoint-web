The biggest launch in a generation

Fuller’s put a cold, hazy, dry-hopped London Pride on keg, made it the official beer of Pride in London, and then stopped talking about it.

In February 2017 Fuller’s launched London Pride Unfiltered and called it, in its own words, “our biggest beer launch in a generation”. Eighteen months later it was the official beer of the Pride in London parade. Then the brewery stopped mentioning it, and as far as we can find, has never said when or why.

The Truman Brewery, 23 February 2017

The launch was at Craft Beer Rising, in the old Truman Brewery on Brick Lane, which is a pointed address for a brewer from Chiswick to pick. MCA Insight reported the unveiling the next morning and carried the generation line. The Caterer put the claim in its proper frame: Pride had been brewed since 1959, and this was the first time since then that the name and the recipe had been used to widen the range.

Simon Dodd, then managing director of Fuller’s Beer Company, told The Caterer that cask Pride “is a truly great beer and cask ale”, and said the brewery “has relevance to different consumers and different drinking occasions”. That is the sentence of a company that has looked at Frontier, its own craft lager, and liked the numbers.

What unfiltered meant

It was London Pride, with two things done to it and two things not done to it. Brewed to the same recipe, then dry hopped, then spun in a centrifuge and sent out neither filtered nor pasteurised, at 4.1% in thirty-litre kegs, poured at 4 to 6°C rather than at cellar temperature. Head brewer Georgina Young explained the reasoning to the Morning Advertiser: “By only using a centrifuge, we get a hazy beer but retain additional flavour.”

The hops are worth getting right, because the trade press got them wrong. Several outlets, The Caterer and the Morning Advertiser among them, reported the beer as dry hopped with Target. Fuller’s own account is more specific: Pride is brewed with Northdown, Challenger, Target and Goldings, and Unfiltered was then dry hopped with Northdown and Challenger again. Target was in the recipe all along. Somewhere between the press release and the page, a hop list became a dry hop.

John Keeling, whom Fuller’s billed in its own blog as “the undisputed heavyweight champion of craft brewing”, did the explaining, and in a video filmed that September he gave a better account of the beer than any of the coverage. He began with why touching Pride at all was a nervy business: it is the hardest beer Fuller’s has to make, he said, because it has so many fans that the smallest error brings letters and phone calls.

Then the part nobody else reported. The cask and the unfiltered keg were brewed identically, with a little more hop going in during maturation, and they parted company only at the end. Cask Pride goes into the cask with finings, and it is the finings that clear it. Unfiltered used no finings at all, and a centrifuge did that work instead. Every extra step strips something out, Keeling said: more processing gives you a brighter beer with a longer shelf life, which was not what they were after. “It will leave the beer hazy but with more flavour.”

His reason for doing it at all was plainer than anything in the marketing. The market, he said, was moving much more towards keg, and he wanted a great beer in keg as well as a great beer in cask. That is a brewer answering the question. Everything else was an answer to a different one.

Fuller’s wrote the whole thing up in a house style you would not get from a brewery now. London Pride is an iconic pint, the blog says, and if it ain’t broke, don’t fix it, they say. “But what do they know?” It promised a beer for “drinkers who are interested in trying craft beers”, in “a slightly different style of pub and bar” from the one where you would find the cask. And in the middle of reassuring everybody that the recipe had not changed, it typed London Filtered is not to be confused with London Pride Original, which is the most honest sentence on the page.

Soho, 7 July 2018

The high point came the following summer. Unfiltered was the official beer partner of the Pride in London parade on 7 July 2018, under a Pride Loves Pride campaign built by the agency Recipe. Fuller’s houses along the route, as the trade coverage recorded, got the glassware, the keg lens, the beer mats and the staff T-shirts: the Iron Duke, the Jack Horner, the Ship on Wardour Street, the Red Lion in Mayfair, the Sun & 13 Cantons, the Lamb and Flag, the Roundhouse and the Admiralty.

The good bit was the voucher. Ambassadors handed out vouchers worth two pints, in two halves, redeemable only when both halves turned up together. You could not drink it on your own. For a beer at a Pride parade that is not a promotion, it is a small piece of design, and whoever thought of it deserved a better year than the beer went on to have.

And then nothing

It never quite caught, which is not the same thing as nobody liking it. Untappd has it at 3.33 from 3,584 ratings, which for a beer from a brewery that size is a shrug rather than a verdict, and the page now carries the line it carries for the dead: no longer being produced by the brewery.

Some people were paying attention. By May 2018 it was in 330ml cans as well as on keg, and the Real Ale Craft Beer channel reviewed one of those cans outdoors on a slipway, with a drone, having very nearly put both the drone and the can into the sea. The verdict was nine out of ten: more depth than the original, better balanced, the hops further forward, and better than cask Pride “without a shadow of a doubt”. One reviewer is one reviewer. But the beer Fuller’s quietly stopped making was not a beer nobody liked.

When it died is the part nobody will tell you. There is no announcement, no trade report, no farewell post. The last coverage of it we can find is from July 2018, three weeks after the parade. Asahi took the beer business, the Griffin Brewery with it, in a £250 million deal the Morning Advertiser reported as completed at the end of April 2019. So there is an obvious story available here, and we are not going to tell it, because nobody has confirmed a word of it.

A beer that a brewery calls its biggest launch in a generation usually gets an obituary. This one got a sentence on a check-in app.
