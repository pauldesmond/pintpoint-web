Draft 2 — Archive17 August 2026 · By Sophie Ro

These aren't revivals — they're hauntings

"I long for the nostalgia, and the thought I might be drinking something resembling something my Dad drank. But in reality the 70s probably wouldn't be to my taste buds — I imagine the pale ale would be more Nicholson's / Greene King than Sierra Nevada. However I for one fully support breweries attempting it, even if it's just a thinly veiled reason to sell crap beer to mugs like me."

When a beer returns, what exactly has been resurrected — the recipe, the badge, the strength, the package, or just the feeling?

That single question is the spine of every British beer revival story right now. The answer is rarely simple, and the honesty with which brewers answer it varies wildly. Some are doing genuine archaeological work. Others are selling you a cartoon and a duty band.

And there is a necessary objection before the nostalgia gets too comfortable: who says the 1970s beer was worth reviving in the first place? David Behrens, writing about Stones Bitter, called that era the age of the beerage — the six-brewery cartel that owned the route from brewery to beer mat. Roger Protz was making the same structural argument in 1978 and, forty years later, still had to write: plus ça change, plus c'est la même chose.

The 6-tier honesty spectrum

Revivals fall on a gradient. The further from a marketing department, the more credible.

TierAnchorExampleHonesty
1Just the nameWatney's Party Seven (2021); Hofmeister's bear, openly attached to a completely new beerPure nostalgia branding — defensible only when admitted
2"Updated recipe"Carling Black Label (Mar 2026, +0.7% ABV); Belhaven Twisted Thistle (Jan 2026, –1.6% ABV)Honest about commercial; dishonest about heritage
3Archive ledger / technical fragmentAllsopp's Double Diamond (1922 inspiration + Citra/Columbus/Mosaic); Truman's with NCYC yeast but mostly new recipesHeritage cosplay unless the modernisation is named
4Surviving bottleTennent's 1868 stout commemorativeArtefact-anchored

6bContinuity by placeJennings (Cockermouth) — Canfield purchase 2024/25, 100 archive recipes, cask-firstPlace-based continuity

The Allsopp's vs Burton Bridge contrast

Two beers. Same era. Opposite philosophies.

Allsopp's Double Diamond (Tier 3): Relaunched Oct 2024 as a 3.8% pale ale "inspired by 1922". Hops: Citra, Columbus, Mosaic. Jamie Allsopp's own launch text: "Revived as a 3.8% pale ale that drinks like a lager." The hop list is the 2010s American craft palette. The original Double Diamond was a strong IPA. The duty band did the writing.

And the older Double Diamond was not automatically beloved. The useful corrective in the Drinks Business comments is brutal: "universally loathed", "sickly sweet", "full of caramel and sticky". That matters. The badge may be iconic; the liquid may still have been bad.

Burton Bridge DBA (Tier 6a): Launched March 2015 by ex-Ind Coope brewers who had worked on the original. "It would be impossible to brew an exact replica, but we are sure it is as close as possible." 4.8%. Untappd currently needs a fresh timestamp before publication, but the notebook figure was 3.51 average from 1,399 ratings. The drinkers who remember it call it "smooth, sweet, a little bitter" and "absolutely gorgeous bitter, rich and almost sticky on the palate."

One is selling you a 1922 ledger and a 2024 hop bill. The other is saying: we worked on this once, we know what it tasted like, and we got as close as we could.

Hofmeister is the cleaner benchmark because it refuses the false claim. Its own revival story says the permission to use the name and bear was "about all they wanted" of the old brand: "the beer itself was to be completely new." That is Tier 1 honesty done properly. Allsopp's problem is not that it made a modern beer. It is that it asks the archive to do more rhetorical work than the recipe can support.

The duty-reform structural argument

Since August 2023, UK alcohol duty is based on strength, not category. Below 3.5% is taxed at a lower rate. The recipe rewrite follows the spreadsheet, not the ledger.

Belhaven Twisted Thistle IPA: 5.6% → 4.0%. "Evolve our range." Carling Black Label: original ~4% → 4.7%. "Designed for today's beer market." Allsopp's Double Diamond: original ~6% → 3.8%. "Drinks like a lager."

The tax band is doing more editorial work than the brewers.

The corporate continuity problem

Protz's forty-year loop matters because most revivals do not happen outside corporate brewing. They happen inside its paperwork. Heineken holds Watney's and Truman's. Carlsberg-Marston's has the Ind Coope / Jennings / Banks's / Bombardier trail. AB InBev holds Boddingtons. Molson Coors holds Carling and Stones.

The aggregators are the gatekeepers, not usually the executors. The interesting work happens when the IP is licensed or abandoned into the hands of people with a regional or technical reason to care.

The Carlsberg villain pattern

The same corporate parent that kills heritage brands repeatedly creates the conditions for independents to revive them.

  • Carlsberg discontinued Ind Coope Draught Burton Ale (DBA) → Burton Bridge picked it up (Mar 2015)
  • Carlsberg Marston's closed Jennings Brewery (Cockermouth) Nov 2022 → Canfields bought it back
  • Carlsberg Marston's also recently axed Banks's Mild and Bombardier

Journalism-grade: kill the brand → let it be rescued by enthusiasts who do it better.

Which era are you reviving?

This is the axis that stops the piece becoming soft-focus nostalgia. A 1970s brand can revive the 1970s recipe — often cold, carbonated, industrial keg — or it can leapfrog the bad years and revive the pre-1970s beer that corporate brewing interrupted.

Behrens makes that move with Stones Bitter. He says there is little about the 1970s worth inflicting on the present generation, and remembers Stones as "ghastly" and "gassy". But he also gives True North's Sheffield revival its blessing because it is not trying to recreate the keg-era Stones. It is returning to cask: beer drawn from wood for Sheffield steelworkers, with the recipe adjusted to replenish the salt lost sweating in the foundries.

That is the full-circle argument. Not: bring back the beer your dad drank in 1976. Rather: bring back the beer before 1976 ruined it.

The hop list as honesty diagnostic

Within seconds of reading a revival's spec sheet, the hop list tells you almost everything.

Modern American "C-hops" (Citra, Centennial, Columbus, Cascade, Mosaic, Galaxy) are the 2010s craft-IPA palette. Classic English hops (Fuggles, Goldings, Challenger, Bramling Cross, Target, Pilgrim, plus the Central European Celeia / Saaz) are the 19th-and-20th-century British palette.

A heritage revival that uses American C-hops while claiming "as it was in 1922" is either being honest about reinventing the beer (Tier 2) or pretending the historical anchor is real when it isn't (Tier 3).

Jennings core lineup (revival of 1990s recipes): Maris Otter, brewery water, Fuggles, Goldings, Challenger, Celeia. Sneck Lifter brings dried fruit and spice; Cumberland Ale carries the North West bestseller memory; Cocker Hoop keeps the spigot joke in the name; Back Yam is explicitly presented as new, not smuggled in as heritage. Burton Bridge DBA: traditional Burton-Ale style. Allsopp's Double Diamond: Citra, Columbus, Mosaic. The hop list is the spec-sheet receipt of editorial intent.

Truman's complicates this nicely. It is not personnel-continuity like Burton Bridge, but Des de Moor records that the revival retrieved Truman yeast from the National Collection of Yeast Cultures. That makes it technically more serious than a badge-only reboot: yeast-authentic, recipe-modern, ownership awkward.

Boddingtons and the gateway-drug case

Boddingtons is the corporate-licensed hybrid in its cleanest form: AB InBev owns the brand; JW Lees brews the cask revival in Manchester; the yeast clause is the editorial jewel. Michael Lees-Jones called JW Lees' house yeast a "non-negotiable" part of the licence agreement.

The CAMRA argument is generous but not naive. Boddingtons is not reinventing the wheel; there are more interesting pints available. But a familiar name can lower the barrier for younger drinkers who find a row of unknown handpulls intimidating. Bass Pale Ale's reported 19% growth and early Boddingtons demand suggest heritage can act as a gateway drug for cask.

The cost is real too: the world's largest brewer is muscling back onto bar space that might otherwise go to small independents. Two true things at once: heritage names can bring new drinkers to cask, and they can crowd out the brewers who kept cask interesting while the big brands were away.

The shelf-presence defence

Paul's own words again: "The beer is nothing special, but I do love seeing them in supermarkets with original(ish) branding, with the little cartoon fellow from the adverts. It may not be my choice, but I'd rather see that on the shelves than Fosters and Budweiser."

The piece can't be all cynicism. Cultural-visibility-of-heritage-brands has its own value even when the liquid is mediocre. Better a flawed Double Diamond next to John Bull's cartoon than another row of identical macro-lagers.

Verdict

What you actually want from a revival is honesty about which era is being revived, and which part of the beer is being brought back. The 6-tier spectrum is the map. The hop list is the receipt. The duty band is the invisible editor. The corporate owner is usually the gatekeeper. And Behrens's Stones test is the hardest question: are you reviving the beer people remember, or the better beer that existed before the memory got industrialised?

The best revivals — Burton Bridge DBA, Jennings under Canfield, True North's pre-1970s Stones, maybe Boddingtons if the Manchester yeast does enough work — are the ones that say: we know we can't bring the past back exactly, but we can get close enough that the feeling survives.

The rest are hauntings. Nice packaging. Wrong beer.
