The one that isn't pretending

Lewes burns a Pope who had nothing to do with it, for reasons invented in the 1850s, and it is still the most honest festival in England.

Tonight in Lewes, seven bonfire societies put six separate processions through a town of seventeen thousand people. Twenty-five to thirty more societies come in from across Sussex. Up to five thousand march. The shops board their windows, the pubs make their own arrangements, and the railway station has been known to shut. It is the largest Bonfire Night in England by some distance, and it is not a fireworks display. It is a procession of flaming crosses through a county town.

After two essays about traditions that turned out to be younger than the pub signs advertising them, Lewes looks like the exception. Seventeen Protestants were burned here between 1555 and 1557, under Mary I. They died in the High Street, in front of the Star Inn — and ten of them were held before their execution in the inn's undercroft. The Star is Lewes Town Hall now. The seventeen burning crosses carried through the streets tonight are for them, and they go past the spot.

That is not a folklorist's invention. Those are parish records, and a cellar.

And yet.

Three nights in seven days, and not one of them is as old as it says. This is the second of three.

It lapsed, and they made it up again

Start with the Pope, because it is the part everyone photographs. Lewes has been burning an effigy of Paul V since the middle of the nineteenth century, and the reason has nothing to do with 1605. It began as a response to the Oxford Movement, and to prominent Englishmen like Newman and Manning going over to Rome — a Victorian quarrel, fought with a seventeenth-century costume. The historian Jeremy Goring's verdict on the man Lewes has been burning for a hundred and seventy years is worth quoting whole: Paul V "was a peaceable man who happened to be Pope at the time of the Gunpowder Plot in 1605 and who cannot be held responsible for the Gunpowder Plot."

The celebrations themselves faded out towards the end of the eighteenth century. What brought them back was not continuity but revival: from the 1820s, groups calling themselves the Bonfire Boys started up again with tar barrels and fireworks, and got rowdier every year until 1847, when police were brought down from London and there were riots in the streets. Restrictions followed. The bonfires were pushed out to fields at Wallands Park. The modern societies grew out of that settlement, which is to say out of a fight.

So the tradition lapsed, was rebuilt from scratch by young men with tar barrels, and acquired its most famous ritual eighty years later in response to an argument nobody was having in 1605.

Which makes it the most honest of the three

Here is the distinction worth holding on to. The Green Man was named by an outsider and handed a religion he never had. Pumpkin ale was revived on an anecdote about George Washington that nobody can source. J-dag was launched by a marketing department in 1990. In each case the invention was done to the thing, by people at a distance from it, and the people who inherited it were not told.

Lewes invented Lewes. The societies made their own traditions, in public, for reasons they would defend to your face, and have gone on arguing about them ever since. In the 1930s the mayor asked for the "no popery" banners to come down and the Pope-burning to stop; it didn't. In the 1950s the Cliffe society was barred from the United Grand Procession for refusing to fall into line; it is still out on its own. The Cliffe smugglers wear black and white striped jumpers, drink at the Dorset Arms, and answer to nobody's idea of what the evening ought to be.

That is a different species of invented. A tradition can be made up and still be real, so long as the people doing it are the ones who made it up and are still prepared to fall out over it. What Lewes has is not antiquity. It is ownership.

And the brewery is in the town

Harvey's have brewed on the Ouse in Lewes since 1790, in a Victorian tower brewery a few minutes' walk from where the processions pass. Every November they make Bonfire Boy, 5.8%, named for the men who restarted the whole thing in the 1820s.

It matters that the brewery is there. A seasonal beer brewed in Sussex, for one night, by a company whose draymen know which streets will be closed, is a different object from a pumpkin ale brewed to a marketing calendar in a state that has no bonfire. Nobody at Harvey's had to be told when the season was. The season walks past the gates.

Which is the same test that separated green hop from pumpkin last week: is the beer answering to something real, or to a date someone chose? Bonfire Boy answers to a town that will be full of smoke tonight.

Everyone else just uses the name

Fawkes himself is from York, and there is a pub on High Petergate that trades on standing by his birthplace, pouring a house bitter called Guy Fawkes at a very English 3.6% — brewed to be drunk in quantity while the rain comes off the Minster. That is the other honest version: a city keeping its most notorious son in the trade, quietly, at session strength.

Past that, the name has gone travelling and left the meaning at home. The Black Abbey Brewing Company of Nashville makes a Guy Fawkes brown ale at 5%. Black Raven in Washington State brews a Gunpowder Plot — a nitro porter at 6.9%, with locally roasted coffee soaked in bourbon and a conditioning on cocoa nibs. A plot to blow up the House of Lords has become a flavour profile in the Pacific Northwest, which is roughly what happened to the turnip.

The tell is in how they describe themselves. Bourbon-soaked coffee, cocoa nibs, nitrogen: every word is about the inside of the glass. Change the name on a Gunpowder Plot porter and nothing else has to change — not the recipe, not the release date, not a syllable of the copy. Bonfire Boy cannot be moved. Take it out of Lewes and it is a 5.8% Sussex ale with an odd name, because the thing it refers to is happening two streets away, once, tonight.

We can be precise about this, and it is not flattering. Our catalogue holds the Nashville brown ale. It does not hold the pub in York. The novelty travels; the place stays put and waits to be noticed.

What the trilogy adds up to

Three autumn nights in one week. On the thirty-first, a festival that went to America as a turnip and came back a pumpkin, wearing a hat a historian never found any evidence for. Tomorrow, a Danish brewery fires a starting pistol at 20:59 and a country calls it Christmas. And tonight, in the middle, a Sussex town burns a seventeenth-century Pope for nineteenth-century reasons and would not dream of asking anyone's permission.

Only one of the three is honest about what it is, and it is the one doing the most obviously ridiculous thing. The pub signs were right all along: it does not much matter whether a tradition is old. It matters whether anybody still means it.
