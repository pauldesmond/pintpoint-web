# What makes a red ale Irish?

## Spine

The five sources contradict each other, and that is the piece. More usefully,
**each source that claims deep antiquity concedes the modern genealogy somewhere
inside its own text.** Nobody has to be caught out, because they all already
said it.

- **Ron Pattinson** (Shut up about Barclay Perkins, 13 June 2011) says the label
  came last: "It's funny how it seems to have been forgotten that classic Irish
  Red Ale Smithwicks started its life as a Pale Ale," and "Many years later,
  looking for Irish styles, someone decided Red Ales were a particularly Irish
  style." His comparison is Watney's Red, which he reckons style hunters would
  have claimed as English Red Ale if it had survived.
- **Em Sauter** (Alcohol Professor, 19 October 2022) writes that "Irish red is
  as Irish as Guinness" and, in the same piece, that "the modern Irish red was
  invented by Coors Brewing Company in Golden, CO". She also notes that George
  Killian's, the beer that made the name in America, is a lager.
- **Birrificio Otus** (10 May 2021) runs the long Celtic lineage, an 8th or 9th
  century poem about a red beer brewed with heather and bog-myrtle, Franciscans
  at Kilkenny from 1226, John Smithwick in 1710. Then it says modern Irish red
  ales descend from English Bitter and Pale Ale, and admits "We do not know
  exactly which type of beer was produced in Kilkenny."

So the three accounts of origin disagree about almost everything except the one
thing that matters: where today's beer came from.

The second half turns the same question on a style that never pretended. The
**BJCP** entry for Red IPA (21B, 2015) calls it "A modern American craft beer
style, based on American IPA but with the malt flavors of an American Amber
Ale", treats the colour as a consequence of dark crystal malts rather than a
basis for identity, and does not mention Irish Red at all. **Sauter again**
(20 September 2023) is blunter: "There is no info about where this style came to
be."

Two colours, then. One was given a nationality in retrospect. The other was
never given anything but a malt bill, and its own style guide says the colour is
a result, not a category.

**The question the piece asks:** what is a style's nationality actually made of?
The colour, the badge, the brewery's address, or the recipe's genealogy? That is
the same question [revivals.html](revivals.html) asks about beers that come back,
and the answer here lands in its badge tier.

**What the piece must not be.** Not a debunking. Pattinson is not catching
anyone out and neither are we. Several things can be true at once: Irish
breweries brewed ale for centuries, the colour is real, the beer people drink
today descends from English pale ale, and the name arrived late. The dishonest
move would be treating the late name as a fraud rather than as what it is, which
is marketing that outlived its market.

## Structure

### Lede
The most famous Irish Red in America is a lager. George Killian's is a Coors
beer by way of a brand that also passed through Pelforth, owned by Heineken, and
Molson Coors. Open there and the question asks itself.

### 1 — What everyone agrees on
Smithwick's, Kilkenny, the long brewing history on the abbey site. Give the
Otus timeline its due rather than skipping to the puncture: Franciscans from
1226, abbey founded between 1231 and 1234, brewing by the 14th century, John
Smithwick building on the ruins in 1710. State plainly that this is the
brewery's own account and that Otus concede they do not know what was brewed
there.

### 2 — The beer that was a pale ale
Pattinson, quoted properly. Pale ale was the English beer that sold in Ireland,
it was expensive, so Irish brewers made their own. The 1853 source he uses is
John Francis Maguire, who names Beamish and Crawford, Lane, Guinness and Abbott.
The label came later.

### 3 — The colour question
Where the red comes from is itself contested, inside Pattinson's own comments.
Thomas Barnes argues a distinct style with a little roasted barley and its own
yeast; Pattinson doubts the roasted barley and suspects sugar colouring; Gary
Gillman argues it is a kind of mild whose colour came from kilning. The Beer Nut
reports that in blind tasting, keg bitter and Smithwick's were hard to tell
apart. Attribute each of these to the person, in the line, as a comment thread
and not as settled research.

### 4 — The American career
Killian's, Coors, and the style becoming a St Patrick's Day fixture in American
breweries. Sauter's own two sentences side by side without comment: as Irish as
Guinness, invented by Coors. Let the reader do the arithmetic.

### 5 — The honest red
BJCP 21B and Sauter on Red IPA. A colour name that claims nothing: modern,
American, built from American Amber's malt bill, and openly undocumented as to
who first brewed one. Sauter's note that it has faded because crystal malt has
fallen out of fashion, and that crystal oxidises faster and goes stale-sweet, is
the practical reason a colour category dies. Sierra Nevada Flipside and Russian
River's Janet's Brown as the survivors.

### 6 — Close
Nationality is not a colour. What makes a beer Irish is the same thing that
makes a revival honest: whether anyone is straight about what they are selling.
Otus and Sauter both are, in the end, in the paragraph after the romantic one.

## Still to source before drafting

- **Lett's of Enniscorthy.** Otus gives Ruby Ale as produced until 1956 and dates
  the start as 1864 in the body and 1964 in a heading. One of those is wrong.
  Worth chasing, because Lett's is the one strand that might make the Irish red
  claim older than the marketing, and the Lett family's yeast later went to
  Australia, which is a connection to check rather than assert.
- **Randy Mosher, Tasting Beer**, for the "red beers since the Middle Ages"
  claim that Sauter cites. Read what Mosher actually says before repeating it.
- **Smithwick's dates.** Guinness bought the brewery in the 1960s, Sauter says;
  Otus says 1965 specifically. Production moved to Dublin in 2013. Confirm both
  against a primary or at least a non-brewery source.
- **Killian's chain of title**: Pelforth, Heineken, Coors, Molson Coors. Otus
  gives it; it needs independent confirmation before we print a chain of
  ownership.
- **O'Hara's Irish Red**, Carlow Brewing, 4.3%, brand from 1996. A live Irish
  example brewed by an Irish brewery, which the piece needs so it is not all
  multinationals.
- **The Pattinson comment thread** runs to about 27 comments across 2011 and
  2022. Read it properly rather than from a summary before quoting any
  commenter.
