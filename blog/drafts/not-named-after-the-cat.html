Not named after the cat

A Stockport barley wine and two modern gins. Each has a story about where it came from, and each story is younger than it looks.

The strongest beer Robinsons of Stockport make is called Old Tom, and they will tell you why.

It was born in 1899, their tasting note says, when Tom, the old brewery cat, was sketched into immortality by the head brewer. The drawing is still on the bottle. The beer is a barley wine at eight and a half per cent, dark, with molasses and vinous fruit on the nose, and, the brewery says, it has won World’s Best Ale and Champion Beer of Britain. You can look it up on PINtPOINT and find somewhere pouring it.

By 1899, Old Tom had been the name of a drink for ninety years.

The gin got there first

Old Tom is a style of sweetened gin, and nobody can tell you where the name came from, which has not stopped anybody trying.

What can be dated is when it appears. David Wondrich, in the Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails, has it first in print in 1810, mentioned in passing in the sporting pages of a newspaper as a superior grade of gin, and appearing in vintners’ advertisements from 1812. The drinks historian David T Smith gives 1812, in the Northampton Mercury, as the earliest he has found. Two historians, two dates, two years apart.

And by 1823 a slang dictionary of the turf has Old Tom as a cask or barrel of strong gin, and then, by a natural transition from the thing containing to that contained, the liquor itself.

So the name was a trade word for strong gin before anyone put a cat on it. Which raises a question: when a Stockport brewery put Old Tom on a strong ale in 1899, was it the cat, or was it a drink name every drinker in England already knew, with the cat making it stick?

Both can be true at once. The brewery has a cat and the trade had a name, and the name is older.

The cat that is not the reason

The story everybody tells about the gin involves a different cat, and it is worth getting right because the version in circulation is not what the source says.

In 1755 Captain Dudley Bradstreet published his memoirs, and described how he had beaten the Gin Act. He got an acquaintance to take a house in Blue Anchor Alley, in St Luke’s parish, so that no informer could name the tenant. He bought the sign of a cat in Moorfields and nailed it to a street window. He fixed a leaden pipe under the cat’s paw, with a funnel on the inside end. Word went round that gin would be sold by the cat, provided the money went in its mouth, where a hole conveyed it to him.

at last I heard the Chink of Money, and a comfortable Voice say, “Puss, give me two Pennyworth of Gin.”Dudley Bradstreet, The Life and Uncommon Adventures, 1755, p. 86

He took six shillings the first day, then above thirty shillings, then three or four pounds a day. He ran it for a month and cleared upwards of twenty-two pounds, and gave it up when the scheme became common and everybody was at it.

That is a true story, in the sense that it is his own account of his own life and nobody has shown it false. What it is not is the origin of the name. Bradstreet does not use the words Old Tom. He does not say the cat was black, or wooden. He does not say Mew. He gives no year. The slot that appears in every retelling is a hole, in the cat’s mouth.

And the name turns up in print fifty-five years later.

The explanation the evidence best supports

Twice in the nineteenth century a reader wrote to Notes and Queries to ask where Old Tom came from, and twice the editors answered.

In July 1860 the answer was that Messrs Hodges, the distillers at Millbank, had a partner named Thomas Chamberlain who made the gin, and that when Thomas Norris left their service and opened a place of his own, he christened the cordial Old Tom out of respect to his former master.

In March 1868 a Mrs Julia Ramsbottom wrote from Paris to ask who Old Tom was, adding that she and her mother liked the spirit very much. The editors replied that they could solve the query on the very highest authority. Old Tom Chamberlain, a relative and partner of Hodges, superintended the distillery and kept a small laboratory at the back of the premises with a supply of superior gin flavoured in a peculiar way. A favoured customer would be invited into Old Tom’s sanctum and treated to a glass of his particular. A glass of Old Tom became such a regular request that the firm decided to make it for the trade and name it after the man.

Wondrich thinks this story has not received the credence it deserves and that it checks out in almost every respect: the Hodges family rectified gin in London from 1780 to 1870, Chamberlain was a partner from the late 1790s until his death in the mid 1810s, and Norris really did sell Hodges’s gin at the Antelope tavern in White Hart Yard, off Drury Lane. Smith is cooler, pointing out that there is no evidence Norris ever worked with Chamberlain, and that the story does not amount to proof.

So the best-evidenced explanation of Old Tom is a man called Tom.

Where the cat actually comes from

Boord and Son adopted a cat sitting on a barrel as their trade device around 1849, and were mocked at the time for it. That is the cat on the label, and it is a gin brand’s mark from the middle of the nineteenth century, not a relic of the Gin Craze.

Beyond Bradstreet’s single painted sign, which he never calls black, wooden or Old Tom, the cat plaques supposed to have hung outside gin shops have no primary source. No original survives that anyone can verify. Cruikshank’s and Dickens’s gin shops show none.

George Cruikshank drew The Gin Shop in 1829 with the spirits in coffin-shaped casks, labelled Old Tom and Blue Ruin. Six years later Dickens walked into one near Drury Lane and wrote down what was painted on them.

there are two side-aisles of great casks, painted green and gold, enclosed within a light brass rail, and bearing such inscriptions, as ‘Old Tom, 549;’ ‘Young Tom, 360;’ ‘Samson, 1421.’Charles Dickens, “Gin Shops”, Evening Chronicle, 7 February 1835

Green and gold, with numbers on. That is what Old Tom looked like in London before anyone drew a cat on it.

Nobody can tell you what a real Old Tom is

It is not a legal category. In Great Britain the spirit drinks rules are the retained Regulation 110/2008, whose Annex II knows gin, distilled gin and London gin and nothing else. Northern Ireland and the EU run on the 2019 regulation. Neither has Old Tom in it.

London gin may not carry more than a tenth of a gram of sugar a litre, so a sweetened Old Tom cannot be sold as London gin, though it can be sold as gin. The Gin Guild says plainly that there are no strict regulations defining Old Tom.

Which means every modern Old Tom is making its own claim about what the style was. Two of them are worth looking at.

The recipe book nobody else has seen

Jensen’s of Bermondsey make an Old Tom with no sugar in it, and the reason they make it that way is one afternoon in a records office.

The story, as Difford’s Guide tells it, is that Christian Jensen, working in Tokyo in 2001, fell for a Martini made with an old London gin whose label had worn down to the letters don Gin. Back in London he tracked the distillery’s papers to a public records office, worked through them five books at a time, found a recipe book, and photographed it. Among the recipes was one from the late 1840s for Old Tom.

Simon Difford looked at it and saw there was no sugar in it, only sweet botanicals, and reasoned on the page that sugar was too expensive at that date for a mass-market gin. He asked Jensen to make it. That reading has travelled.

Two things sit under that. The first is that Difford is in his own story: he lives in Bermondsey, drank in the bar where Jensen placed his first cases, tasted the gin, and introduced him to the distributor who took the brand on. The second is that the documents are unnamed. Not the brand on the Tokyo bottle, not the records office, not the distillery. The conclusion was drawn once, from photographs nobody else has examined.

It also runs against the strongest account of what Old Tom was. Wondrich has it as a retailer’s category rather than a distiller’s, publicans sweetening bought-in spirit, with Old Tom the strongest and least sweetened of them at what he says seem to have been around thirty-five grams of sugar a litre. Not very sweet. Not nothing.

The quest that was already a supply chain

The other one is about water, and it is the cleanest of the three because one of the men involved has said so himself.

Martin Miller’s Gin tells its founding story on its own site: a bar in Notting Hill in the spring of 1998, three friends served three bad gin and tonics, and a challenge to fix gin. The distillation is split, earthy botanicals in one run and citrus peel in another, and married afterwards. Then the water. Gin is mostly water, so the page has Miller searching for the best there is, and the quest takes him to Borgarnes on the west coast of Iceland, where, according to the brand, the spring water began as snow that fell more than eight hundred years ago. The spirit is distilled in England, taken to Iceland, cut there, and brought back.

The page does not name the other two men. One of them is David Bromige.

It would be nice to say we searched the world for the perfect water source, but it was pure luck that we used Icelandic water and it was perfect for the taste we wanted.David Bromige, co-founder, to Travel Distilled

And the technical case is thinner than the label suggests. Every distiller cuts spirit with water, and whatever is dissolved in that water ends up in the bottle, so distillers want water with as little in it as possible. Difford’s Guide, setting out how spirits are cut to strength, notes that much is made by marketing departments about the purity of the water used, and then describes what the trade actually does: demineralise it by reverse osmosis, producing pure tasteless water that will not affect the flavour.

Brewers met the same problem from the other end and solved it long ago. They wanted the hard sulphate water of Burton upon Trent, and rather than move or ship, they worked out what was in it and added the same salts to their own. PINtPRESS has covered what that did to Bass. The trade still calls it Burtonisation, and it worked.

The name is always older

Three drinks, three stories about where a name came from.

Robinsons say the cat, and the cat is probably real, and the name was a gin word before the cat was born. Jensen’s say the recipe book, and the book may be exactly as described, and nobody outside the company and Difford has examined it. Miller’s still tells the quest for eight hundred years of snow; one of its three founders says it was luck.

None of that makes any of them bad drinks. Old Tom is one of the best beers in the country and has the trophies to prove it, which is a stronger fact than any origin story.

But the pattern is worth knowing when you are reading a label. A drink gets a name, the name outlives whatever produced it, and the trade keeps supplying fresh reasons for it. The cat, the book, the snow.
