The country that lost hot beer

Britain had the purl boats, the Early Purl House and a trade licensed by Waterman’s Hall. Germany still pokes its beer, Poland still warms it, and the only two purls in the world were both brewed this year.

Somebody in Austin is putting a red hot poker into a pumpkin porter.

They are not being silly, and they say so carefully. Beer poking is a real tradition, and Strange Land Brewery, who hold an annual event for it, told Food & Wine that “rather than duplicate the original, we adapted the tradition to our own context”. The original runs in spring when the bock arrives. Texas has no winter worth the name, so they hold theirs in autumn, and instead of a bock they poke an imperial bourbon pumpkin porter. The iron caramelises the malt, they say, and leaves a smoky note and a creamy body behind it.

A brewery in a state with no winter, keeping alive something Britain had more of than anyone, and let go of entirely.

Two purls

There are two beers called purl in the world at the moment and both of them were brewed this year. Howl Brewing’s Merry Windsor Purl Ale is six per cent and Untappd files it under Historical Beer. Rushford & Sons Brewhouse of Upton, Massachusetts, went back further: theirs is 3.1 per cent, listed as an English pale mild, and described by the brewery as “an historic style dating back as far as Shakespearean times, bittered with wormwood and orange peel rather than modern hops”.

Historical Beer is the correct filing, and that is the problem with it.

A trade, not a curiosity

In 1851 Henry Mayhew counted the men who sold purl for a living. His chapter on them, Of the River Beer-Sellers, or Purl-Men, is a survey of a trade rather than a note on a drink: how much a boat cost, what a licence cost, who bought. The purl-men worked the Pool of London in short, broad skiffs with a charcoal fire burning in the bottom of the boat, rowing out to the ships before dawn and ringing a heavy bell as they came. Coal-whippers, watermen, lightermen and coal-porters hailed them with a shout of “Purl ahoy”. They were licensed, like the watermen themselves, from Waterman’s Hall.

In winter fog, when the regular watermen lost their bearings and shouting got them nowhere, they steered home by the purl-man’s bell. The hot beer seller was the fog signal. Boak & Bailey went through the same chapter in 2018 and followed the boats further than we are going to here.

What was in it had already changed by the time Mayhew got there. Purl began as ale with wormwood in it, bitter and medicinal, and the name came from the herb. By 1851, he writes, “the wormwood is unknown; and what is sold under the name of purl is beer warmed nearly to boiling heat, and flavoured with gin, sugar, and ginger”. Not one of the men selling it could tell him what the word meant.

The Early Purl House

Indoors it had its own equipment and its own premises. Dickens put both into Our Mutual Friend, in the tap and parlour of the Six Jolly Fellowship Porters, which “gave upon the river, and had red curtains matching the noses of the regular customers, and were provided with comfortable fireside tin utensils, like models of sugar loaf hats, made in that shape that they might, with their pointed ends, seek out for themselves glowing nooks in the depths, when they mulled your ale, or heated for you those delectable drinks, Purls, Flip, and Dog’s Nose”. The pub advertised itself on its own door-posts as “The Early Purl House”. W. T. Marchant quotes the passage in In Praise of Ale of 1888, in a chapter he simply calls Warm Ale.

The fire was the equipment. The vessels were made pointed so they could be driven into the hot part of it.

Purl was not alone either. Marchant has buttered ale, which a seventeenth century French visitor describes as cinnamon, sugar, butter and beer brewed without hops, and which Pepys drinks in March 1669 before taking a coach. There is egg flip and egg hot and posset and dog’s nose. There is lamb’s wool, the spiced ale with roasted crab apples burst into it, which we have written about before. Marchant gives the reason for all of it in one line: when beer was the staple drink morning, noon and night, people naturally wanted their breakfast beer warm.

What happened to it

Two things, and neither of them is a change of taste.

The drink itself was absorbed. The Gin Is In traces purl through the cocktail books and finds that by William Terrington’s manual of 1869, often called the first British cocktail book, the purl recipe is warmed beer with eggs beaten into it and a glass of spirit that need not be gin. Which is to say it had become a flip. The name survived on a drink that was no longer the thing it named.

And the reason for warming beer at all went away. Michael Carver, who interprets colonial brewing for a living, puts it plainly: before refrigeration beer was served at cellar temperature, and “as the 19th century progressed, mulled beers faded from view. With the introduction of lager beer and artificial refrigeration, cold beer could be made available year-round.” Hot beer did not lose an argument. It was replaced by a machine.

Everywhere else

The same machine arrived everywhere, and everywhere else kept the drink anyway.

In Poland grzane piwo is ordinary enough that an estate agent will take a man who has spent a cold day flat hunting to the Stary Rynek for one, which is how one American first met it, and it is on the Christmas market stalls in December as a matter of course. In Bohemia there is a recipe for teplé pivo in Karolina Vávrová’s Prague cookbook of 1873, and an emperor drinking Glühbier cooked with ginger and cinnamon in a novel of 1871, both of which Beer Culture turned up. In Hungary it is growing rather than surviving: several small breweries are at it, the Advent fairs in Debrecen, Eger, Győr, Pécs and Szeged sell it, and a brewery manager quoted by Hello Hungary gives the rule that matters, which is never to boil it: 60 to 65 degrees and no more.

The poker survived too, as Bierstacheln, in Germany and Austria. A hot iron goes into a malty beer, the residual sugars caramelise on it, and four or five seconds is the limit before the gas goes and the beer flattens. Hop Culture has it crossing the Atlantic to a Minnesota brewery with an annual bock festival, another in Florida, East Brother in California, and from there to the pumpkin porter in Austin.

Where it has come back, it has come back twice over and by two different routes. In Portland, Threshold Brewing serve grzaniec because the owner is Polish and wanted his own winter on the bar; Jeff Alworth found them doing it and noted that they build it on a five per cent grisette, and that in Poland an ordinary lager is the usual base, which disposes of the idea that mulled beer wants something big and dark. That is a tradition carried. Austin is a tradition adopted by people with no claim on it at all.

What we have instead

Britain has a recipe. BBC Good Food will tell you to warm a golden ale with apple juice, brandy and spices and not let it boil, which is a perfectly good drink and a magazine page rather than a trade.

And we have the two purls, both of them a year old at most.

One person has reviewed the American one. He drank it on draught in April and wrote down fresh whole grain bread, toasted biscuit, caramel, toffee nuts, and then “mild bitter earthy hop, drier finish”. He finished: “different than I’m used to for an English mild, but decent beer regardless.”

There are no hops in it. The brewery says so on the label. What he tasted was wormwood, and the only word he had for a bitterness in beer was the one he already had.

A hundred and seventy-five years earlier, Mayhew stood on the Thames among men who sold purl every morning of their lives, and found that not one of them knew what they were selling. The drink is in better shape now than the knowledge of it ever was.
