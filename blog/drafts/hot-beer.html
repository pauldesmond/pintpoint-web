The country that lost hot beer

Mayhew counted the men who sold hot beer off boats in the Pool of London. Britain still has recipes for it. What is harder to find is anything like the trade he describes.

Before dawn, in the Pool of London, a man rowed out to the ships with a fire burning in the bottom of his boat.

He was selling hot beer. In 1851 Henry Mayhew counted men like him and wrote the chapter up as a trade rather than a curiosity, Of the River Beer-Sellers, or Purl-Men: what a boat cost, what a licence cost, who bought. They worked in short, broad skiffs, rang a heavy bell as they came, and were hailed by the coal-whippers and lightermen with a shout of “Purl ahoy”. They were licensed from Waterman’s Hall, like the watermen themselves.

In winter fog, when the watermen lost their bearings and shouting got them nowhere, they steered home by the purl-man’s bell. Boak & Bailey went through the same chapter in 2018 and followed the boats further than we are going to here.

What was in the cans had already changed by the time Mayhew got there. Purl had been ale with wormwood in it, bitter and medicinal, although Mayhew could not say why that was called purl: he notes only that the philologist Nathan Bailey used the word that way. By 1851, he writes, “the wormwood is unknown; and what is sold under the name of purl is beer warmed nearly to boiling heat, and flavoured with gin, sugar, and ginger”. Not one of the men selling it could tell him what the word meant.

The Early Purl House

Indoors it had its own equipment and its own premises. Dickens gave the Six Jolly Fellowship Porters, in Our Mutual Friend, “comfortable fireside tin utensils, like models of sugar loaf hats”, pointed so that they could be driven into the glowing part of the fire “when they mulled your ale, or heated for you those delectable drinks, Purls, Flip, and Dog’s Nose”. The pub advertised itself on its own door-posts as “The Early Purl House”. W. T. Marchant quotes the passage in In Praise of Ale of 1888, in a chapter he simply calls Warm Ale.

Purl was not alone. Marchant has buttered ale, which a seventeenth century French visitor describes as cinnamon, sugar, butter and beer brewed without hops, and which Pepys drinks in March 1669 before taking a coach. There is egg flip and egg hot and posset and dog’s nose. There is lamb’s wool, the spiced ale with roasted crab apples burst into it, which we have written about before. Marchant gives the reason for all of it in one line: when beer was the staple drink morning, noon and night, people naturally wanted their breakfast beer warm.

Decline

Two things happened, and neither of them is a change of taste.

The name moved. The Gin Is In traces purl through the cocktail books and finds that by William Terrington’s manual of 1869, often called the first British cocktail book, a purl is warmed beer with eggs beaten into it and a glass of spirit that need not be gin. Which is to say it had become a flip. The word survived on a drink that was no longer the thing it named.

And the reason for warming beer at all went away. Michael Carver, who interprets colonial brewing for a living, puts it plainly: before refrigeration beer was served at cellar temperature, and “as the 19th century progressed, mulled beers faded from view. With the introduction of lager beer and artificial refrigeration, cold beer could be made available year-round.”

Elsewhere

Refrigeration reached everywhere, and in some places the warm drink stayed anyway.

In Poland grzane piwo is ordinary enough that an estate agent will take a man who has spent a cold day flat hunting to the Stary Rynek for one, which is how one American first met it, and it is on the Christmas market stalls in December as a matter of course. In Bohemia there is a recipe for teplé pivo in Karolina Vávrová’s Prague cookbook of 1873, and an emperor drinking Glühbier cooked with ginger and cinnamon in a novel of 1871, both of which Beer Culture turned up. In Hungary small breweries are taking it up now, and a brewery manager quoted by Hello Hungary gives the rule that matters, which is never to boil it: 60 to 65 degrees and no more.

That is warming the beer. There is also a separate technique for it, and the German-speaking countries kept that too. Bierstacheln puts a hot iron into a malty beer so the residual sugars caramelise on it, and four or five seconds is the limit before the gas goes and the beer flattens. Hop Culture has it crossing the Atlantic to a Minnesota brewery with an annual bock festival, another in Florida, and East Brother in California.

And to Texas. Strange Land Brewery in Austin hold an annual poking event, and told Food & Wine that “rather than duplicate the original, we adapted the tradition to our own context”. The original runs in spring when the bock arrives. Texas has no winter worth the name, so theirs is in autumn, and instead of a bock they poke an imperial bourbon pumpkin porter.

Portland has the other kind. Threshold Brewing serve grzaniec because the owner is Polish and wanted his own winter on the bar; Jeff Alworth found them doing it and noted that they build it on a five per cent grisette, and that in Poland an ordinary lager is the usual base, which disposes of the idea that mulled beer wants something big and dark.

Two purls

Britain still has recipes for hot beer. BBC Good Food will tell you to warm a golden ale with apple juice, brandy and spices and not let it boil, which is a perfectly good drink. What is harder to find is anything like the trade Mayhew describes.

The beer itself has come back twice, and not here. Howl Brewing’s Merry Windsor Purl Ale is six per cent, from a nano-brewery on Mills Road in North Saanich, British Columbia. Howl’s brewer, Dan Van Netten, told the Eagle Valley News in 2021 that he builds his from wormwood, horseradish and black pepper, that he takes the name from The Merry Wives of Windsor, and that purl was the most unpopular beer he ever made, although he liked it himself.

Rushford & Sons Brewhouse of Upton, Massachusetts, make the other, 3.1 per cent, listed as an English pale mild and described by the brewery as “an historic style dating back as far as Shakespearean times, bittered with wormwood and orange peel rather than modern hops”.

Rushford’s purl has one review on BeerAdvocate. The reviewer drank it on draught in April and wrote down fresh whole grain bread, toasted biscuit, caramel, toffee nuts, and then “mild bitter earthy hop, drier finish”. He finished: “different than I’m used to for an English mild, but decent beer regardless.”

The brewery says there are no hops in it.

A hundred and seventy-five years earlier, Mayhew stood on the Thames among men who sold purl every morning of their lives and found that not one of them knew what the word meant.
