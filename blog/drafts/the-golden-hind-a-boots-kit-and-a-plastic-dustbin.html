The Golden Hind, a Boots kit and a plastic dustbin

New Albion of Sonoma took its name from an Englishman’s claim on California, and its founder learned to brew from a kit bought in a Glasgow chemist.

Draft note, not for publication Dates here are page-sourced rather than inferred. The Navy years and the Boots purchase are Tom Acitelli’s The Audacity of Hops, read off the Internet Archive lending copy. The British home-brewing law is Boak and Bailey’s 2019 history, first published in Hop & Barley in 2018, which predates everything of ours it touches. Open before this goes live:
  • The Dave Line purchase is a memory, not a fact, and the piece says so. Acitelli has McAuliffe buying The Big Book of Brewing in that Boots in 1966 and then concedes the dating in his own footnote. Note that his footnote argues it from the American publication date, which is no use to us: the purchase was in Glasgow, so the British imprint is the only date that bears on it. Amateur Winemaker, Andover, 1974. Do not let a later edit quietly promote the memory to a fact, and do not let the American date back in.
  • Scotland, not California, is now sourced three ways and one of them is the family. Acitelli dates the Boots purchase to late 1966 at page 15, Holl has the kit and the sailors tasting it, and the death notice the family placed through Backstrom-Pyeatte of Siloam Springs in July 2025 says he began brewing in Scotland and shared it with Naval personnel and Scottish locals. The accounts that put the first brewing in California, BYO and CraftBeer.com among them, are describing the later serious phase or are simply wrong. Do not let a later edit soften this back to disputed.
  • The Great Beer Trek is an eyewitness and needs dating anyway. The Stern detail is from page 180, and the 1990 preface says the original trip text is virtually unchanged from 1984, so it is a first-hand visit to the brewery while it ran. What is not given anywhere we have read is the date of the visit. Two further traps in the same book: the quiz on page 142 says New Albion opened in 1978, which is wrong, and anything written for the 1990 revision, including the line about the brewery having apparently disappeared off the face of the earth, has to be dated 1990 and not 1984. Morris also said in 2025 that New Albion was still a gleam in the eye when the Trek passed through, which cannot be squared with page 180 and is presumably forty years of hindsight. Date the journey, never the imprint.
  • What Stern and Zimmerman were is genuinely contested, and the sum is too. Alworth, Holl and CraftBeer.com call them founders or partners; Acitelli calls them New Albion’s original outside investors at page 42 and keeps founder for McAuliffe; Morris in 1984 calls Stern the assistant and speaks of two partners. On the money, Tim Carman’s obituary in the Washington Post says fifteen hundred dollars each and Acitelli says twelve hundred each against five thousand raised in all. Morris also spells her Suzie; Holl, Alworth and the Times all have Suzy, which is what the piece uses, so do not let the 1984 spelling be read as a correction. The piece therefore gives no figure and no title, only that they put up money and did the work, which every account supports. Do not let an edit pick a side or print a number.
  • The first claim is attributed and narrow, and must stay that way. Three sizes are in circulation: the Washington Post’s first modern microbrewery built from the ground up, Holl’s first new craft brewery in the United States since Prohibition, and the illustrated histories’ California’s first new brewery since Prohibition. Only the first is careful, because Anchor was older and was not new, and the clause doing the work is built from the ground up. The piece gives it to the Post rather than saying it in our voice, and the standfirst makes no first claim at all. Do not let an edit put a bare one back.
  • How close we can get to what Boots actually sold, which is not close enough to say it. Boots had its name on The Boots Book of Home Wine Making and Brewing by B. C. A. Turner, published by Wolfe for the Boots Co. Ltd, first edition 1970 and a second in 1975, with a plate at page 173 captioned a wide selection of beer kits and equipment. A Boots Best Buys advertisement datable from its own small print to July 1981 offers the Boots Beer-Making Beginners Kit at £6.95, full instructions and re-usable equipment, illustrated with a bucket, bottles, sugar and a siphon. The Science Museum Group holds an accessioned Boots-made brown ale kit of 1986, tub and lid, yeast, concentrated wort and grain. So Boots own-brand beer kits are documented from 1970 to 1986, named, priced and accessioned. None of that reaches 1966, which is why the piece says only that he came out with a pale ale kit and a plastic dustbin and leaves the contents alone.
  • Bravery is image-checked and 1965 is safe. The dustbin line is page 31 and the licence passage page 28, both read off the page rather than off a summary. The copy is the Gramercy printing in New York by arrangement with Arco, copyright 1965 to Bravery, Library of Congress 66-19302, so it was catalogued in America in 1966 and the 1965 date the piece gives is the book’s own. The American edition keeps the word dustbin.
  • The 1966 date is McAuliffe talking in 2011, and the piece says by his own account. Acitelli’s notes for that chapter cite an interview with McAuliffe of 30 September 2011 and Gary Flynn, who served with him at Holy Loch. No contemporaneous source names Boots, Glasgow or 1966. It is the same memory that has him buying a book published eight years later, so it gets the same hedge. The Scotland of it is solid on three independent sources; the month and the shop are his.
  • New Albion did not brew with Cascade and the ending does not need it to. Acitelli has the 1978 hops as mostly Northern Brewer and Cluster from Signorotti Farm at Sloughhouse, image-checked at page 64, with Cascade only as an intention after the Anchor tour at pages 40 to 41. BYO and CraftBeer.com both imply the original was a Cascade beer and both are describing, or sliding into, the 2012 Boston Beer recreation. The hop ending stands on Line’s own page and on Anchor, not on New Albion.
  • Point Reyes is in Marin. Sonoma is the next county. Drake’s landing and the brewery are near each other and not in the same place, and a sentence that lets them touch will be wrong. BYO has already made this mistake, putting Nova Albion in San Francisco Bay.
  • The Albion Brewery founding date is unsettled, 1870 on the San Francisco landmark report and 1875 in the Brewery History Society journal. The piece avoids giving one.
  • Anchor is our inference. Line does not name the brewery. The piece says so in its own voice rather than pretending he did.
  • Cascade in Line’s 1980 chapter is written for Americans, in a British book. He tells British readers minor alterations may be needed and says corn sugar is not generally available here. That almost certainly went for the hop too, so this is not evidence that British brewers could get Cascade in 1980, and the piece does not say it is. A Boots home-brewing catalogue page, undated in the copy we have seen but of the same era, sells Fuggles and Goldings by name with style notes, Goldings for pale ale and Fuggles for brown ales and stouts, alongside crystal malt, liquid and dried yeast and isinglass. No American variety on the page. One retailer and one spread, so that is consistent with Cascade being a name a British reader met in a book rather than a hop he could buy, and it is not proof of it.
  • Liberty Ale 1975 had no Cascade in it. See our own Anchor piece, sourced to Burkhart. What stands is Maytag’s recollection, through Jeffrey House, that Cascade was not a commercial hop until Anchor used it.
  • Still wanted: the exact first edition date of Brewing Beers Like Those You Buy. Ben Bennetts calls it much sought after in March 1980, so Argus 1984 is a reissue, but we have no imprint for the first.

One of Read the sign, five stories about what a name can tell you. The run starts with A cask called Father Christmas.

There is a ship on the label. It is sailing through hills, which no ship can do, and it is the Golden Hind.

The label belonged to the New Albion Brewing Company of Sonoma, California, which the Washington Post calls the country’s first modern microbrewery built from the ground up, and the man who commissioned it was perfectly frank about what he was doing. History matters in brewing, Jack McAuliffe told the editor John Holl in 2011, and if you have not got one you can invent one. He had made English ale, porter and stout. He had taken the name of a brewery that closed with Prohibition. Name, logo and history, he said, and that was that.

Except that he invented none of it.

What the name already meant

On 17 June 1579 Francis Drake brought the Golden Hind into a cove on the northern California coast to careen her, having failed to find a short way home. He stayed five weeks, left on 23 July, and established no settlement. The site is now identified as Drake’s Cove at Point Reyes, a National Historic Landmark since 2012, in the next county along from Sonoma.

Before he left he claimed the whole of it for Elizabeth and gave it a name. Two reasons are recorded in his own voyage narrative, and both of them are England: the white banks and cliffs along that shore looked like the ones at home, and Albion was the archaic name for Britain. Nova Albion. New Britain.

So a brewery in Sonoma making English ale took its name from an Englishman’s claim on California, and carried an English ship on its label. That is not an invented history. That is the actual one, picked up whole.

And the brewery he borrowed was English too

There really had been an Albion in San Francisco. John Hamlin Burnell built it at Hunters Point over a set of natural springs, had the plant shipped out from England, and brewed ale and porter, bottling it in stoneware on site. By about 1908 the firm was trading as the Albion Ale and Porter Brewery. It closed around 1919 with Prohibition.

Its founding date is disputed, 1870 on the San Francisco landmark report and 1875 in the Brewery History Society journal. The Norman tower Burnell put up over the springs is still standing at 881 Innes Avenue as San Francisco Landmark 60.

An English ale and porter brewery, with English kit, making the three beers McAuliffe went on to make. He thought he was borrowing a name. He was borrowing the lineage as well.

The chemist’s counter

John Robert McAuliffe was born in Caracas on 11 May 1945. He enrolled at Michigan Technological University, left it for the Navy in 1964, trained for thirty-eight weeks on Treasure Island and was posted to the submarine tender USS Simon Lake at Holy Loch, where he repaired submarine antennas. Tom Acitelli heads the chapter Dunoon, Scotland 1964 to 1968.

He drank in the pubs. Everybody writing about him says so, and what they agree he drank was British ale, porters and stouts above all. That part is never in doubt.

What happened next is the part the summaries keep getting wrong. By his own account, in late 1966, running errands, he went into a Boots in Glasgow and came out with a pale ale kit and a plastic dustbin.

He took them back to a grey stone cottage in Dunoon and brewed over a coal fire. When it was ready he gave it to the other sailors, who told him what they thought, and he made another one. The Scots around him drank it too, and thought well of it.

That is the whole apparatus. A kit off a shelf, a bin, a coal fire and a tasting panel of sailors.

Not on his return to America, which is where several accounts put it, including the illustrated histories. In Scotland, in 1966, two years into a four year posting, out of a shop on a Scottish high street. His family’s own death notice says the same.

And the reason that kit was on a Scottish shelf at all is a British budget three years earlier.

Why the kit was there to buy

Gladstone replaced the malt tax with a duty on finished beer in 1880, and the side effect was that a household brewing for itself needed a licence, with registration and inspection attached. It did not stop anybody. It simply drove it indoors. By 1961 and 1962, as Boak and Bailey set out, there were 250 licences to brew beer at home in the entire country.

On 3 April 1963 the Chancellor, Reginald Maudling, abolished the requirement in his budget speech.

H. E. Bravery, writing two years after, called it an absolute boon and expected home brewing to become as popular as home wine making, which by his count already had more than half a million practitioners in Britain. He also passed on a complaint. Some who had been brewing without a licence for as long as they could remember told him that now they were not breaking the law, “half the fun has been knocked out of it”.

What followed is the reason there was anything on that shelf in Glasgow. Ingredients and equipment that had been sold furtively came out from under the counter, were advertised openly and were made easier to use. Edme sold 300 tons of malt extract to British home brewers across 1966 and 1967, and launched pre-hopped extract in 1969, by which point a beginner with a bucket could make forty pints in a quarter of an hour.

The dustbin is the detail that gives him away. H. E. Bravery’s pocket guide of 1965 tells the beginner that for fermenting, a polythene dustbin “bought especially for the purpose” is ideal. Not the one from under the sink. A new one, off a shelf, for brewing in.

So McAuliffe did not improvise a vessel in a foreign country. He followed the instruction of the moment, in the country that had issued it, in the year everyone else was following it.

His own country took another fifteen years. Jimmy Carter signed the bill on 14 October 1978 and it took effect on 1 February 1979, exempting from tax a household making 200 gallons a year where two or more adults lived there, and 100 where there was one. New Albion was founded in 1976, was brewing by 1977 and closed in 1982.

The book he remembered buying

Acitelli has him picking up Dave Line’s The Big Book of Brewing in that same Boots, and concedes the problem in his own footnote. Amateur Winemaker published it at Andover in 1974. McAuliffe left Scotland in 1968.

So he remembered buying it, and the print record holds no copy before 1974.

The hop goes home

New Albion opened in a rented warehouse on the edge of Sonoma with a brewhouse McAuliffe welded together from dairy equipment and Coca-Cola syrup drums, three storeys high so it could run on gravity. He was not alone in it. Suzy Stern, later Suzy Denison, and Jane Zimmerman put up money and worked the hours, and Stephen Morris, writing it up in The Great Beer Trek, has Stern in hip boots starting up the steam hose and observing drily that this was not quite what she had pictured herself doing when she grew up. Four barrels of ale and stout a week between the three of them and some part-time help. The accounts that make McAuliffe the solitary founder are leaving two people out, and the earliest one we have does not. Its best year made 450 barrels.

It made an ale, a porter and a stout. Not, whatever gets repeated, on Cascade: Acitelli has New Albion buying its hops in 1978 from a family farm at Sloughhouse, and they were mostly Northern Brewer and Cluster. McAuliffe came away from a tour of Anchor meaning to use the new hop. The Cascade people taste in New Albion Ale is in the bottle Boston Beer made in 2012.

Which brings the traffic round. By 1980 Dave Line had written three books for Amateur Winemaker at Andover, and the last of them, Beer Kits and Brewing, has a chapter called North American Home Brew Practice. He finished it and died before it was bound; a note in the front, signed in March 1980, says he died suddenly while the book was at proof stage. He thanked his wife Sheila for typing it and his five year old son for not helping too much with the experiments.

The chapter is written to Americans, and it is written down to them a little. Britain had been free to brew at home since 1963 and America since 1978, and Line notes the signature and sets the two dates against each other. His British methods, he tells his new readers, hold good. The differences he allows are ingredients and measures: corn sugar, US gallons, local malts, American hops. British readers get a line saying they may need minor alterations, and a warning that corn sugar is not generally available here, which almost certainly went for the hops as well.

The one thing he grants the Americans as their own is a style. Steam beer, he writes, is about the only traditional American beer still brewed commercially. He does not say by whom. In 1980 he can only mean Anchor.

And the hop running through that chapter, in the house recipe and most of the others, is Cascade.

Anchor is the brewery Fritz Maytag remembers as the first to put Cascade to commercial use, a recollection he gave to Jeffrey House and one we have set out at length along with what complicates it. So the last book of the man who taught Britain how to clone its own beers is teaching America to brew, with that hop, recommended back across the Atlantic to the country it came from, in a chapter that treats the Americans as pupils.

They learned it. Within a decade the hop came back the other way and changed what British beer tastes like, which is a story we have told. Line did not live to see any of it.

Read the sign

A name can tell you less than the sign claims, which is what the Nag’s Head and the Red Lion and Old Tom are for. This one is the other way about.

McAuliffe said that if a brewery hadn’t got a history, it could invent one. He brewed the kinds of beer he’d learned to make in Scotland, took the name of an old California brewery that had made English ale and porter, and put Drake’s ship on the label.
