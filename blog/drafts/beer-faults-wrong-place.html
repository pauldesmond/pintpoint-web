A fault is only a fault in the wrong place

Four lists of what goes wrong in beer, written for four different people. The drinker is the one who has to taste the result, and the only one who cannot tell whose fault it was.

There is butter in your pint. Not much, but it is there, somewhere behind the hops, and once you have noticed it you cannot stop noticing it.

That is diacetyl, and it is the most useful fault in beer, because it can arrive by two unrelated routes. It can come from the brewery, where the yeast was taken off the beer before it had finished clearing up after itself. Or it can come from the pub, out of a draught line that has not been cleaned properly, where bacteria make the same compound from scratch. Jess Keller Poole set both causes side by side writing for Hop Culture in 2020.

Same taste. Same word. One of them may have happened three months ago and four hundred miles away, and the other last Tuesday, nine feet from where you are standing. Nothing in the glass tells you which.

Everybody has a list, and they are not the same list

There is no single catalogue of beer faults. There are several, and what separates them is less the contents than the reader each one had in mind.

The Brewers Association keeps sixteen, with a fact sheet apiece, and it reads like a production problem: things to be managed on the way to the package. Dimethyl sulphide, the cooked sweetcorn note, comes with the instruction that a vigorous boil prevents it. Acetaldehyde, green apple, is described as something always present that only becomes a fault above a threshold.

Thomas Barnes keeps 109. The Complete Beer Fault Guide, version 1.4, written between 2011 and 2013 and hosted by London Amateur Brewers, is built on George Fix’s Principles of Brewing Science and the Meilgaard flavour wheel. Its style names have aged since, which is worth knowing before quoting it. Every entry carries a perception threshold and a concentration range, not so that anyone can measure a sample, but so a taster knows how little of something it takes to be noticed.

Then there is the cellar, which has a literature of its own. The Brewers Association publishes a Draught Beer Quality Manual, free to download, covering line cleaning, system design, gas dispense and balance, pouring and sanitation. Avani Solutions, a Norfolk company that sells line cleaning to pubs, keeps a shorter list of four written for licensees.

The question worth asking of any of them

Barnes ends almost every entry with the same heading. When are these notes appropriate? He asks it sixty-two times, and the answer is frequently not never.

That is the subject in one question. Lactic acid is spoilage in a bitter and the entire point of a Berliner weisse. The Brewers Association files onion and garlic as an off-flavour and then notes that it comes off the hops and is not always unwelcome, depending on the style. Ethyl acetate is pear drops low down and nail polish remover higher up, and the line between them is degree rather than kind.

Even diacetyl has a right place. Barnes lists low levels as acceptable in Bohemian pilsner, English pale ales, Scottish ales, brown porters, sweet and oatmeal stouts and English IPA, which is a long list of exceptions for something a brewer otherwise spends his time removing.

So the catalogue is not really of chemicals. It is of chemicals in the wrong beer, or in the right beer at the wrong strength, which is a much harder thing to put on a chart.

Before it leaves

We have written before about what diacetyl did to Firestone Walker in their early years, when the Fuller’s yeast they had chosen dropped out of suspension so hard that it was at the bottom of the tank before it had finished taking the diacetyl back up. David Walker described a laboratory that could measure diacetyl but not its precursor, so the fault appeared at packaging, after everyone had stopped looking.

Not every brewery fault works like that. Contamination and oxygen picked up at packaging are different problems with different causes. But it is a good illustration of the ones that are hardest to catch, where something is doing exactly what it was chosen to do, slightly too well.

After it leaves

These are the ones a drinker is most likely to meet.

Avani describe TCP as an antiseptic, hospital note, and give the cause as chlorine from cleaning products meeting phenols already present in the beer, with chlorinated tap water and hose that is not food grade as contributors. Worth saying where that comes from: they sell the cleaning service. It is a trade supplier’s account, and it is the only one we found describing the mechanism for a British cellar.

Oxidation is the other. Cardboard, wet paper, envelopes, and leather or sherry when it has gone far enough, although those later stages involve chemistry beyond the single compound. The papery note itself is trans-2-nonenal, and Barnes puts its detection threshold between 0.00001 and 0.002 milligrams per litre, among the lowest of anything in his guide.

Time on sale matters here, and it matters differently depending on the container. A cask takes in air once it is broached, which is why it has days rather than weeks. A keg dispensed properly under gas does not, so a long time on does not spoil it the same way, even though beer does not improve by waiting.

They also name TCA, the musty, damp-building note, and blame fungi in lines left standing in chlorinated water and in pythons with failed insulation.

The one you can act on

Most of this is out of a drinker’s hands. One thing is not.

The skunk note is not a hygiene failure or a chemical accident. It is light. Barnes sets out the reaction: wavelengths below 520 nanometres break down hop-derived compounds in the beer and liberate a mercaptan chemically close to the stuff a skunk uses.

Hop Culture gives the half you can use. Clear glass and green glass offer very little protection, brown glass offers some, and a can blocks it entirely. That is one of the few facts in this whole subject that is any use while standing in a shop.

Barnes also corrects something almost everyone says about it. People describe the smell as natural gas. Natural gas has no smell at all. Mercaptans are added to it, deliberately, so that a leak is noticeable, and the resemblance runs the opposite way round from the way it is usually told.

What to do about the butter

Butter in a Czech lager may belong there, at a low level, and Barnes would tell you the same about a sweet stout or an English IPA.

Butter in several unrelated beers in the same place is a reason to ask the pub to look at it. You do not have to name the compound, work out whether it came from a fermenting vessel or a length of tubing, or settle an argument between a brewer and a publican, before asking somebody to check your pint.

PINtPOINT records when a pub’s tap list was last confirmed, which is a dated sighting and not a measure of how the beer has been kept. Nobody publishes when a line was last cleaned.
