A fault is only a fault in the wrong place

Four lists of what goes wrong in beer, written for four different people. The drinker is the one who has to taste the result, and the only one who cannot tell whose fault it was.

There is butter in your pint. Not much, but it is there, somewhere behind the hops, and once you have noticed it you cannot stop noticing it.

That is diacetyl, and it is the most useful fault in beer, because it can arrive by two completely unrelated routes. It can come from the brewery, where the yeast was taken off the beer before it had finished clearing up after itself. Or it can come from the pub, out of a draught line that has not been cleaned properly, where bacteria make the same compound from scratch. Jess Keller Poole set both causes side by side writing for Hop Culture in 2020.

Same taste. Same word. One of them happened three months ago and four hundred miles away, and the other happened last Tuesday, nine feet from where you are standing. Nothing in the glass tells you which.

Everybody has a list, and they are not the same list

There is no single catalogue of beer faults. There are several, and the interesting thing about them is not what they contain but who each one was written for.

The Brewers Association keeps sixteen, with a fact sheet apiece. It is a brewer's list, and it reads like a production problem: things to be managed on the way to the package. Dimethyl sulphide, the cooked sweetcorn note, is on it with the instruction that a vigorous boil prevents it. Acetaldehyde, green apple, is described as something that is always there and only becomes a fault above a threshold.

Thomas Barnes keeps 109. The Complete Beer Fault Guide, hosted by London Amateur Brewers and built on George Fix's Principles of Brewing Science, is a judge's list. Every entry gives a perception threshold, a concentration range and a number from the Meilgaard flavour wheel, because a judge has to say how much of something is present and not merely that it is.

Avani Solutions, a Norfolk company, keeps four. That is a cellar list, and it is worth saying plainly that they sell line cleaning, so every fault they name has a dirty line somewhere in the cause and a service of theirs in the cure. It is still the most useful of the four, because it is the only one about what happens to beer after it leaves the brewery.

And Hop Culture keeps seven, which is a drinker's list: the ones you are actually likely to meet.

The question the judge's list asks and the others do not

Barnes ends almost every entry with the same heading. When are these notes appropriate? He asks it forty-four times, and the answer is not always never.

That is the whole subject in one question. Lactic acid is spoilage in a bitter and the entire point of a Berliner weisse. The Brewers Association files onion and garlic as an off-flavour and then notes that it comes off the hops and is not always unwelcome, depending on the style. Ethyl acetate is pear drops at low concentrations and nail polish remover higher up, and the line between the two is a matter of degree rather than kind.

Even diacetyl has a right place. Barnes lists low levels as acceptable in Bohemian pilsner, English pale ales, Scottish ales, brown porters, sweet and oatmeal stouts and English IPA, which is a long list of exceptions for something a brewer otherwise spends his time removing.

So the list is not of chemicals. It is of chemicals in the wrong beer, or in the right beer at the wrong concentration, which is a far harder thing to put on a chart.

The brewery's faults

The ones a brewer spends time on are mostly fermentation and packaging. We have written before about what diacetyl did to Firestone Walker in their early years, when the Fuller's yeast they had chosen dropped out of suspension so hard that it was at the bottom of the tank before it had finished taking the diacetyl back up. David Walker described a laboratory that could measure diacetyl but not its precursor, so the fault appeared at packaging, after everyone had stopped looking.

That is the shape of most brewery faults. Something is doing exactly what it is supposed to, slightly too well or slightly too early.

The cellar's faults

These are the ones a drinker meets, and almost nobody writes about them.

TCP is the clearest example. Avani describe it as an antiseptic, hospital note, and the cause they give is chlorine from cleaning products meeting phenols that are already in the beer, with chlorinated tap water and non food grade hose as contributors. The beer left the brewery correct. It acquired a hospital smell on the way to the glass, from the act of cleaning the line it came through.

Oxidation is the other one, and it is the most ordinary fault in British beer. Cardboard, wet paper, envelopes, and leather or sherry when it has gone far enough. The compound is trans-2-nonenal, and Barnes gives its detection threshold as between 0.00001 and 0.002 milligrams per litre, which is among the lowest of anything in his guide. A tiny quantity of it is enough. Avani's stated cause is the obvious one: kegs left on sale too long after they were broached.

They also name TCA, the musty, damp-building note, and blame fungi in lines left standing in chlorinated water and in pythons with failed insulation. It is the one fault here we could only find in a cellar-services company's own material, which is not the same as finding it nowhere, but it is worth you knowing where it came from.

The one you can act on

Most of this is out of a drinker's hands. One thing is not.

Lightstruck beer, the skunk note, is not a chemical accident or a hygiene failure. It is light. Barnes sets out the mechanism: wavelengths below 520 nanometres make riboflavin in the beer react with hop-derived isohumulones and liberate a mercaptan, detectable at a few parts per billion, chemically close to the stuff a skunk uses. Perception threshold four nanograms per litre.

Hop Culture gives the practical half. Clear glass and green glass offer very little protection, brown glass offers some, and a can blocks it entirely. That is one of the few facts in this whole subject you can use while standing in a shop.

Barnes also corrects something almost everyone says about it. People describe the smell as natural gas. Natural gas has no smell at all. Mercaptans are added to it, deliberately, so that a leak is noticeable, and the resemblance runs the other way round from the way it is usually told.

What we can see, and what we cannot

PINtPOINT knows what is on in a pub and when that was last confirmed. It does not know when the lines were last cleaned, and no app does, because nobody publishes it.

Which means the one cellar variable we can see is freshness, and freshness is the cause of exactly one fault on this page. If a keg has been on for a fortnight the beer in it is a different beer from the one that was broached. Everything else in a cellar happens out of sight of anybody except the person holding the cleaning bottle.

The butter, then. If it is a Czech lager it may be meant. If it is an IPA that tasted fine here last month, look at the line rather than the brewery. And if it is the third beer in a row with the same note, it is not the beer.
