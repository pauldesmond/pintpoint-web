Voice of the Fire

Lewes burns this Pope, who had nothing to do with the Gunpowder Plot, for reasons rooted in the 1850s.

On the fifth in Lewes, seven bonfire societies put their processions through a town of nearly seventeen thousand people, and dozens more societies come in from across Sussex to march with them. The shops board their windows, the pubs make their own arrangements, and the railway station has been known to shut, and Lewes District Council asks people not to travel in for it. It is the largest Bonfire Night in England by some distance, and it is not a fireworks display. It is a procession of flaming crosses through a county town.

After an essay about a tradition that turned out to be younger than the pub signs advertising it, Lewes looks like the exception. Seventeen Protestants were burned here between 1555 and 1557, under Mary I. They died in the High Street, in front of the Star Inn, and ten of them were held before their execution in the inn's undercroft. Lewes Town Hall, built in 1893, stands on the Star’s site, and the undercroft is still down there. The seventeen burning crosses carried through the streets on 5 November are for them, and they go past the spot.

That is not a folklorist's invention. That is Foxe, and a cellar.

And yet.

Three nights in seven days, and not one of them is as old as it says. This is the second of three.

It lapsed, and they made it up again

Start with the Pope, because it is the part everyone photographs. Lewes has been burning an effigy of Paul V since the middle of the nineteenth century, and the reason has nothing to do with 1605. It began as a response to the Oxford Movement, and to prominent Englishmen like Newman and Manning going over to Rome. It was a Victorian quarrel, fought with a seventeenth-century costume. The historian Jeremy Goring, in Burn, Holy Fire!, his history of religion in Lewes since the Reformation, gives a verdict on the man Lewes has been burning for a hundred and seventy years that is worth quoting: Paul V "was a peaceable man who happened to be Pope at the time of the Gunpowder Plot in 1605 and who cannot be held responsible for the Gunpowder Plot."

The celebrations themselves faded out towards the end of the eighteenth century. What brought them back was not continuity but revival: from the 1820s, groups calling themselves the Bonfire Boys started up again with tar barrels and fireworks, and got rowdier every year until 1847. Lewes Borough’s own history of that night has the Bonfire Boys going for the special constables in the High Street, the Metropolitan Police sent down from London, and Lord Chichester reading the Riot Act from the steps of County Hall. Restrictions followed. The bonfires were pushed out to fields at Wallands Park, and were not allowed back onto the High Street until 1850, the year Pius IX restored the Catholic hierarchy in England and the country worked itself into a panic about it. The modern societies grew out of that settlement, which is to say out of a fight.

So the tradition lapsed, was rebuilt from scratch by young men with tar barrels in the 1820s, and acquired its most famous ritual thirty years after that, in response to an argument nobody was having in 1605.

Which makes it the most honest of the three

Here is the distinction worth holding on to. The Green Man was named by an outsider and handed a religion he never had. Pumpkin ale was revived on an anecdote about George Washington that nobody can source. J-dag was launched by a marketing department in 1990. In each case the invention was done to the thing, by people at a distance from it, and the people who inherited it were not told.

Lewes invented Lewes. The societies made their own traditions, in public, for reasons they would defend to your face, and have gone on arguing about them ever since. In 1933 the mayor, J. C. Kenward, wrote to the Cliffe society asking it to give up the Pope; Cliffe declined, and was ostracised by the other societies for it. In the 1950s the Bonfire Council made the same trade more formally: Cliffe could march in the United Grand Procession, provided the "No Popery" banner stayed at home. Cliffe turned the offer down. It is the only society still parading that banner and still burning Paul V, and seventy years on it is still out of the United procession by its own choice rather than anybody else’s. The Cliffe smugglers wear black and white striped jumpers, drink at the Dorset, a Harvey’s house and their headquarters for a hundred and sixty years, and answer to nobody's idea of what the evening ought to be.

That is a different species of invented. A tradition can be made up and still be real, so long as the people doing it are the ones who made it up and are still prepared to fall out over it. What Lewes has is not antiquity. It is ownership.

And the brewery is in the town

The Harveys were selling wine into Lewes in the 1790s, began brewing around 1820 in Bear Yard, and bought the Bridge Wharf site on the Ouse in 1838. They have been there ever since, in a Victorian tower brewery a few minutes' walk from where the processions pass. Every November they make Bonfire Boy, 5.8%, and its name is younger than almost anything else in this essay. It was first brewed as Firecracker, to thank the fire brigade after a fire at the brewery in July 1996, and only later made the beer Harvey’s brew each year for the bonfire boys and belles of Lewes. Even the local beer is a revival with its story bolted on afterwards. The name is not even exclusive. Seven beers in our catalogue carry Bonfire in the title, and two of them are Bonfire Boy: this one, and an India export porter from a Polish brewery called Browar Monsters. And the bonfire you can smell in the Sussex one is an impression rather than an ingredient, which is a piece of its own.

The yeast is a Yorkshire import, and it arrived at this time of year. By Harvey’s own account, their supplier the British Pure Yeast Company went out of business in the mid fifties, and an analytical chemist pointed the head brewer, Anthony Jenner, at the Tadcaster Brewery Company instead. In the last week of October 1957 the 9.56 passenger train from York came in with a consignment of yeast, which went into that day’s brew. Jenner’s verdict was that it had settled down to the Sussex air. It is still the house strain, so the beer poured on the fifth is fermented by something that came south by steam train seventy years ago next autumn.

The town will defend a beer the way it defends a banner. In December 2006 Greene King took Harvey’s Best off at the Lewes Arms. The regulars picketed their own pub, twelve hundred people signed a petition, the mayor and the town’s MP objected, and trade was reported down by about ninety per cent. Greene King put the beer back in April 2007, its chief executive Rooney Anand saying the company had underestimated the depth of feeling. It sold the pub to Fuller’s the following year. Nobody in Lewes had to be told what the argument was about.

It is not a souvenir beer either. We have seen Harvey’s on in eleven of the sixteen Lewes pubs we hold: Sussex Best in nine of them, Old Ale in seven, and Bonfire Boy in two, the John Harvey Tavern, which is the brewery’s own house, and the Black Horse. The Dorset, where the Cliffe smugglers drink, has six different Harvey’s in its tap history. Tap lists move, so on the fifth itself the only honest answer is to open the app and look.

Which is the same test that separated green hop from pumpkin at Halloween: is the beer answering to something real, or to a date someone chose? Bonfire Boy answers to a town that will be full of smoke on the fifth.

It matters that the brewery is there. A seasonal beer brewed in Sussex for one night, which Harvey’s list on cask for November and sell in 500ml bottles from the brewery shop all year, by a company whose draymen know which streets will be closed, is a different object from a pumpkin ale brewed to a marketing calendar in a state that has no bonfire. Nobody at Harvey's had to be told when the season was. The season walks past the gates.

Everyone else just uses the name

Fawkes himself is from York, and there is a pub on High Petergate that trades on standing by his birthplace, pouring a Tetley-brewed house bitter called Guy Fawkes at a very English 3.7%, as CAMRA has it, brewed to be drunk in quantity while the rain comes off the Minster. That is the other honest version: a city keeping its most notorious son in the trade, quietly, at session strength.

Past that, the name travels and the descriptions stay at home. The Black Abbey Brewing Company of Nashville makes a Guy Fawkes brown ale at 5%. Black Raven in Washington State brews a Gunpowder Plot, a 7.2% nitro porter aged in bourbon barrels with chocolate and coffee. In the copy for both, the plot is the name and the rest is the liquid, which is roughly what happened to the turnip.

Black Abbey went further once. They put that Guy Fawkes brown ale into barrels that had held Corsair’s Triple Smoke whiskey, which is the one time we found actual smoke anywhere near a beer named for the fifth of November. It came out of a Tennessee barrel rather than an English kiln, and they do not make it any more.

The tell is in how they describe themselves. Bourbon barrels, chocolate, coffee, nitrogen: every word is about the inside of the glass. “In order to unify the flavors and give it that classic British feel we conditioned it on Nitrogen,” says brewmaster Beaux Bowman. In Bowman’s account, the classic British feel came from nitrogen. Change the name on a Gunpowder Plot porter and nothing else has to change: not the recipe, not the release date, not a syllable of the copy. Bonfire Boy cannot be moved. Take it out of Lewes and it is a 5.8% Sussex ale with an odd name, because the thing it refers to is happening two streets away, once, on the fifth.

We can be precise about this, and it is not flattering. Our catalogue holds both of the American ones. It does not hold the pub in York. The novelty travels; the place stays put and waits to be noticed.

The newest thing in the story is a face

There is one more invention in this, and it is the youngest and the largest. The most recognisable English face on earth belongs to Guy Fawkes, and it was drawn in 1982 by David Lloyd, for V for Vendetta, written by Alan Moore. Lloyd reached back to the Gunpowder Plot for a mask to put on an anti-fascist anti-hero; forty years on it is worn by Anonymous, by Occupy, and at protests in countries that have never heard of Lewes. A seventeenth-century conspirator, redrawn by a comics artist in the nineteen-eighties, is now the default global costume for opposing a government.

Which puts Lewes in good company rather than bad. The town invented its Pope in the 1850s and has been sincere about him ever since; a comics artist invented that face in 1982 and the world adopted it within a generation. Neither is ancient. Both are real. The difference is that Lewes will tell you exactly when it started and why, and then carry the banner down the high street anyway.

What the trilogy adds up to

Three autumn nights in one week. On the thirty-first, a festival that went to America as a turnip and came back a pumpkin, wearing a hat a historian never found any evidence for. On the sixth, a Danish brewery fires a starting pistol at 20:59 and a country calls it Christmas. And on the fifth, in the middle, a Sussex town burns a seventeenth-century Pope for nineteenth-century reasons and would not dream of asking anyone's permission.

The pub signs were right all along. It does not much matter whether a tradition is old. It matters whether anybody still means it.

If you are going

Not on the fifth

The town is not yours that evening, and half the pubs will be ticket-only or shut. For any other night of the year we have put the nine we would walk on their own page, in the order the ground suggests, from the station to the Greenwich meridian.
