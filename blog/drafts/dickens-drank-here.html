Dickens drank here

Five famous Dickens pubs on one London crawl. Even the two best cases are thin, and the first stop opens on weekday lunchtimes only.

One of Read the sign, five stories about what a name can tell you. The run starts with A cask called Father Christmas.

On the front of the Red Lion in Parliament Street there is a bust of Charles Dickens, standing in a niche.

It commemorates a visit to a different building. The pub Dickens went into was pulled down in 1899, and B. W. Matz, who wrote the standard book on the subject, is careful about it: there is a Red Lion there today, he says, “not the same one Dickens visited”, but on the same spot. It is more pretentious than the old one. It keeps the lion rampant for a sign. And it has the bust.

The man who checked

Matz opened his chapter on the George Inn in 1921 with a sentence that has aged extremely well.

Certain traditional legends naturally grow round our old London landmarks and, when once started, no matter how conjectural, they are hard to overtake or suppress.B. W. Matz, The Inns and Taverns of “Pickwick”, 1921

He was not a debunker. He loved these houses, wrote two books about them, and spent his life on Dickens. That is exactly what makes him useful. When a man who wants the stories to be true goes looking for the evidence and reports that it is not there, you can take it.

So here is the usual London Dickens crawl, five houses, run past the two books he wrote about them.

The George and Vulture

Mr Pickwick’s headquarters, and Matz treats it as exactly that: a house in a novel. Across four period books we have not found a claim that Dickens himself drank in it.

What there is instead is a great deal of hedging about everything else. The floor shown to visitors is “the floor tradition has decided was Mr. Pickwick’s bedroom”. On Chaucer, Matz allows that there is no record to substantiate a claim that he used the house, though his father was a licensed victualler in the district and probably knew it. Addison, Steele, Swift, Defoe and Wilkes are named as frequenters with nothing offered. The first Beefsteak Club was formed there, “so it is recorded”.

It is a beautiful pub in an alley off Cornhill, and the understood pattern is weekday lunchtimes only, which is a better reason to plan around it than anything in the paragraph above. Ring before you go.

The George Inn, Southwark

This is where Matz loses patience.

The legend is that the George is the original of the White Hart in Pickwick. Matz goes through it and rejects it. Then he turns to a writer who had described the George as a favourite of Dickens as a boy in Lant Street, and pointed out the seat he used to sit in.

All of which is sheer nonsense.Matz, 1921

Having said so, he is scrupulous about what remains. Dickens may have known the house as a boy but was unlikely to have frequented it. In later years he may well have visited. Indeed, Matz writes, Miss Murray, the hostess at the time, told him so. Her authority was Abraham Dawson, a carman and carrier, nephew of the man who owned the inn, who assured her he had frequently chatted with Dickens in the coffee room.

That is the strongest claim anybody has for Dickens drinking at the George Inn: the landlady repeating what an old carrier told her. Matz writes it down, attributes it, and does not pretend it is more than it is.

And the novel? One sentence. Little Dorrit, Book One, Chapter Twenty-two, where Maggy says of Tip, “If he goes into the ‘George’ and writes a letter.”

Then Matz closes the chapter with the line this whole piece is built on.

As such Dickensians have a great affection for it, and there is no need to invent stories about it to justify their reverence.Matz, 1921

Ye Olde Cheshire Cheese

The most famous Dickens pub in London, and the thinnest case of the five.

Matz, in 1922, could not find it in the books at all. The Cheese, he writes, was never, he believes, mentioned in any one of Dickens’s books by name, nor can he discover that it was alluded to or described even under an assumed name.

He adds that it is known Dickens visited it. He offers no letter, no diary, no Forster. The evidence he actually produces is a menu card showing Dickens and Thackeray at a table in Dr Johnson’s room, which is a souvenir, not a source.

As for A Tale of Two Cities, where Darnay and Carton dine after the trial, Matz is precise about the standing of that identification: Dickens students are of opinion that the Cheshire Cheese is the tavern. Opinion. The novel describes a walk down Ludgate Hill and up a covered way into a tavern, and never names it.

The house itself makes a stronger claim than the specialist who went looking for the evidence.

The Lamb and Flag

Nothing. It appears in neither of Matz’s books, nor in Hughes in 1891 or Miltoun in 1903.

It is a lovely pub, and that is reason enough to drink in it.

The Red Lion, Parliament Street

In David Copperfield a small boy puts his money on the counter and asks for a glass of the Genuine Stunning, with a good head to it. The landlord calls his wife before they serve him, and she opens the half-door of the bar, bends down, gives the boy his money back and kisses him.

Matz: this incident actually occurred to Dickens himself when a lad in the blacking factory, because he admitted it, in his own words, recorded in Forster’s Life, Book One, Chapter Eleven. Dickens says he went into a public-house in Parliament Street “which is still there, though altered, at the corner of the short street leading into Cannon Row”.

Dickens does not name the pub. He describes it, and Matz identifies the Red Lion from that description. What is documented, in his own words through his own biographer, is the visit, and it is the only one on the crawl.

It is also a child buying one glass of ale, once, in a building that no longer exists.

The other best case, and it is also thin

Jack Straw’s Castle on Hampstead Heath is better documented than anything on the crawl, because the invitations survive. Dickens to Forster: “I know a good ’ous where we can have a red-hot chop for dinner, and a glass of good wine.” Forster called it their first experience of Jack Straw’s Castle, memorable for many happy meetings in coming years. A later note has dinner on the table at Jack Straw’s at four.

Then Matz, in the same chapter, trims it back. Visitors are shown the chair he sat on and the bedroom he slept in. There is no record, he says, that Dickens ever stayed the night there, or any reason for believing he did, given how easy it was to get home.

The chair and the bedroom grew after the fact, on a house that genuinely had the man to dinner.

What he actually drank

Remarkably little is recorded, and almost none of it in a pub.

His fiction is better supplied. Wardle’s Christmas Eve at Dingley Dell in Pickwick sits down to “a mighty bowl of wassail”, on the night Wrexham usually tapped its Christmas ale.

A red-hot chop and a glass of good wine on the Heath. A glass of ale as a boy in the blacking factory. At Gad’s Hill, Hughes and his party take a glass from the celebrated deep well and prefer to drink it, as he puts it, as Dickens himself drank it, pure. And in a story told by a Mrs Masters, a poor woman brought in out of a storm, for whom Dickens told the landlady to draw some brandy, and said, drink that up, and gave her a shilling. That last one, which is Hughes in 1891 rather than Matz, is the only drink in these books that he unambiguously bought, and he bought it for somebody else.

The one place where the drinks are vivid is a pub that does not exist. At the Six Jolly Fellowship-Porters, Matz writes, the modern drinker gets the substitutes for “those delectable drinks” of Miss Abbey Potterson’s day: Purl, Flip, and Dog’s Nose. Watermen reached the house by wooden steps at low tide, and when the water was high and the place was all but afloat they came by boat and moored outside like gondoliers.

Which pub that was is itself contested. Matz says the Grapes at Limehouse and says a reading of chapter six against the building leaves no doubt. Miltoun, twenty years earlier, named a different house entirely, the Two Brewers, and hedged it: supposed to be the original. We have written about the Grapes, and the safe form of words is the one we use there: it is the pub traditionally taken for the Fellowship Porters, and Dickens never names it.

Go anyway

None of this is an argument against the crawl. These are five good pubs and two of them are among the best buildings left in London.

It is an argument against the brass plate. The George Inn is a galleried coaching inn with its outside wooden galleries still standing, which is a far better fact than a carrier’s recollection. The Cheshire Cheese is a warren of dark rooms off Fleet Street. The Red Lion stands where a frightened boy once spent his own money on a glass of ale and got it back with a kiss.

Matz had it in 1921. The plaques never caught up. There is no need to invent stories about these places to justify the reverence.

The beer in the picture
Dickens did write about one great drinking picture. In a letter from Broadstairs in 1848, which John Forster printed in his Life, he said that Hogarth, after one immortal journey down Gin-lane, “turned away in pity and sorrow … and went back no more”. Gin Lane had a companion print, and there is a brewer’s name painted in it.
