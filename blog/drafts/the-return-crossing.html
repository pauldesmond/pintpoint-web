The Return Crossing

How American hops came back across the Atlantic.

Some time after 1996, in Mark Tranter’s recollection, one of the owners of the Evening Star in Brighton went to California and came back with Cascade hop pellets. Dark Star, which Fuller’s bought in 2018, was brewing in the pub at the time. Those pellets, with other American hops the merchants Charles Faram could supply in small quantities, became the basis of what the brewery’s founder Rob Jones called “The Hophead Club”, where each meeting tasted a different single-hopped beer. “Cascade was the customers’ and brewers’ favourite, so it was not long until that became the staple,” Tranter said.

That is how one brewery got its Cascade. Then, in 2002, a British brewer went to the fields to choose for himself.

Gordon A. Ponce’s account of the Transatlantic Beer Bridge, published at The Craft Beer Finder in October 2026 and built on Jeffrey House’s research, follows the traffic west: Fuller’s ESB into America, London Pride into American distribution, Fuller’s yeast into a Californian fermenting tank. We have followed that span four times. This is the other carriageway.

Before the bridge

Michael Jackson’s World Guide to Beer appeared in 1977, and commenting under a 2012 timeline Martyn Cornell called it probably “the single most important event in the history of opening people’s minds in the UK to other types of beer”. Somewhere to buy the stuff came soon after. The Pitfield Street shop that became the Pitfield Brewery’s own “started life as the Two Brewers off-licence around 1980”, Cornell wrote in the same thread, “and was a vital source for beers from Belgium, especially, but also Germany, the US and elsewhere at a time when such beers were almost impossible to find in the UK”. Jackson’s Beer Hunter followed on Channel 4 in 1990.

Then the brewers went looking. Sean Franklin was experimenting with American Cascade “as far back as the early 1980s”. He left brewing, drove a taxi for five years, and came back to it in 1993. Brendan Dobbin established the West Coast Brewery at the Kings Arms in Chorlton-on-Medlock in 1989, the Brewery History Society records, and Beer52 say his Yakima Grande pale ale could well be the first example of American Cascade in British brewing. The beer was not always called that. Dobbin sold it as Sierra Nevada Pale Ale, a name Tandleman says was changed under threat of legal action, and Gazza Prescott drank it under the old one at the Beer House in 1991, his first taste of “the lemony, bitter and zesty character of the Cascade hop”. Dobbin moved the brewery to the King William IV in 1993 and it closed in August 1994.

Three beers arrived in 1993. Franklin’s Rooster’s Yankee was 4.3 per cent, a beer that “fit comfortably into British pub and beer festival culture” while being “a touch more aromatic than most UK drinkers were used to at the time”. Oakham brewed Jeffrey Hudson Bitter commercially for the first time on 13 September, and the brewery gives Mount Hood as the main hop with English Challenger for bittering, Willamette having been trialled first and not chosen: an American hop in the first batch of a beer voted “Supreme Champion Beer of Great Britain” in 2001. Boak and Bailey call JHB “a contemporary product developed quite independently”, inspired by Summer Lightning rather than by anything American. In Sheffield, Dave Wickett’s Kelham Island brewed Pale Rider, and Patrick Morton, who devised it, says that beer was American hopped as well. Three beers, three American hops, and two of the three went on to take the Champion Beer of Britain title.

The beers themselves were arriving too. House brought Fritz Maytag and Anthony Fuller together at a distributor party at the Mark Hopkins Hotel in San Francisco, he says, then encouraged Fuller’s to import Anchor in their own returnable 50-litre kegs, as our piece on Fuller’s ESB in America sets out. Fuller’s arranged a trial and the beer went into three London pubs in late 1991: the Mawson Arms, the Dove and the Churchill Arms. The 1993 Good Beer Guide independently records Fuller’s selling Maytag’s beers in Britain. Roger Protz, in his chapter there, “The Beer Invasion” (p.19), picked one out: “My favourite is the fruity Liberty Ale, with a massive hop aroma from the use of the home-grown Cascade strain.”

Liberty Ale reached a British handpump on 4 July 1992, at the White Horse at Parsons Green, and Jackson announced it that morning in The Independent under the headline “Take a long, serious draught of sweet Liberty”. It “appears on draught in Britain today for the first time”, he wrote. The hop is most of the column. Jackson called Cascade America’s traditional “aroma hop”, described the further charge added during cellaring as a final flourish “known as ‘dry-hopping’”, and gave the bouquet as “huge, flowery, piney, faintly citrus”. He ran it on Independence Day, about a beer he noted had first been brewed in 1975, for the anniversary of Paul Revere’s ride.

Fritz Maytag has told House it was John Segal, a Yakima hop grower, who brought Cascade to his attention, and that Segal had learned of the hop through a hop research programme in Oregon. In House’s account Segal also supplied the hops that went into Liberty Ale. Coors had been experimenting with Cascade a couple of years before Liberty Ale, in small quantities, as a participant in the same programme. Maytag’s recollection is that it was never a commercial hop until Anchor used it, in the boil and again to dry-hop. The USDA registered Cascade in 1972, three years before Liberty Ale.

The White Horse was not one of the trial houses. Mark Dorber had looked after that cellar since 1981, and in 1990 had convened a seminar there on Burton pale ale which Protz credits with the rebirth of IPA in Britain. The American beer landed in a bar already thinking hard about hops.

In October 1997, writing from the judging at the Great American Beer Festival in Denver, Jackson called the most characterful British IPAs new ones, “such as Fuller’s and Usher’s”, and said both had “clearly been influenced by American examples”, from Bengal IPA, made by Tomcat in Raleigh, North Carolina, to Hophead from the Jones Street brewpub in Eugene, Oregon.

American hops were not new to Britain. Ron Pattinson has British beer production rising by almost half between 1857 and 1871 while the hop acreage stood still, so the hops came in: Bavarian first, then American, dominant among imports by the 1880s. Writing in BeerAdvocate in August 2012, he has the verdict of the day running the other way: American hops, in the opinion of “the best judges” quoted by The Farmer’s Magazine in 1863, carried a “disagreeable flavour which renders them quite unsuitable for fine qualities of ales”.

The road

In 2002 someone built a road. Oakham’s own history says John Bryan was “the first UK brewer to travel to the USA hop fields to select hops direct from the farms”, and that he went with Paul Corbett of Charles Faram. He returned for almost every harvest afterwards. Beer Today, writing when he retired in May 2022, says those visits “are widely acknowledged as a key factor in the increased availability of US-sourced hop varieties to UK brewers”.

Corbett is what joins the thing together, and he was in it nine years before the road was built. Morton has told House that he used whole American Willamette, that Corbett at Charles Faram could supply it, and that Cascade was not available to him at the time. Months after Morton left Kelham Island, Abbeydale’s first Black Mass, brewed on 6 November 1996 and developed from a stout he had made there, went out single-hopped with Cascade. Oakham had trialled Willamette for JHB in 1993. Faram were the merchants behind the other American hops in Dark Star’s Hophead Club too, in the small quantities a tasting club needs. What 2002 changed was not whether a British brewer could buy American hops. It was whether he could stand in the field and choose them.

Sheffield

Wickett was one of the two Sheffield economists in the title of Ponce’s piece: an MA in economics at the university, which he left in 1976, then lecturing in it at the city’s polytechnic, now Sheffield Hallam. The other was House himself. We set out those years in our Anchor piece. Wickett bought a pub called the Alma and reopened it in 1981 as the Fat Cat, added the brewery in 1990, and the University of Sheffield’s obituary of him has that brewery as the city’s first new one in over 100 years. That same year he and a partner, John Roman, opened the Old Toad in Rochester, New York, a pub Protz describes as dedicated to American craft beers. Liberty Ale was in Wickett’s city before Pale Rider was. Jackson’s Independence Day column has it going on draught in Britain for the first time at the White Horse in Parson’s Green, and names two shops selling it in bottle, one of them Small Beer at 57 Archer Road, Sheffield; the rest of the trade he gave only as “other northern towns and cities”. Kelham Island’s Pale Rider followed in 1993, and in 2004 it was named Champion Beer of Britain. The brewery’s own bottle label, from around 1998, gives 1992. James O’Hara, who led the group that rescued Kelham Island in 2022, called it “known and revered beyond Sheffield”. Jonny Garrett, writing in Good Beer Hunting, calls it “the first American-hopped beer to win the coveted prize”. Oakham’s JHB, with Mount Hood in the first batch, had taken the title in 2001. Pete Brown, talking to the BBC in July 2012, put Sheffield’s beer revolution down to “the singular influence of Dave Wickett”.

Kelham Island’s alumni turn up elsewhere. When Mark Dredge announced Magic Rock in Huddersfield in 2011, its brewer was Stuart Ross, “formerly of Kelham Island, Acorn and Crown Brewery”, and its stated intention was “modern flavour-forward beers inspired in part by US craft breweries”.

The recipe changed hands several times in eleven years. Morton devised it and handed over to Bob Lawson in early 1996, by his own account, and Sheffield CAMRA’s own history has him leaving Kelham Island after five years to found Abbeydale that year. Protz, writing the brewery up on 1 August 2004, names Paul Ward as the man who brewed the winning batch, “a part-timer and cask washer” when he arrived and head brewer by then, and gives Pale Rider at that point as 5.2 per cent, brewed from pale malt alone, with “American and German hops”. Which American hops he does not say.

The brewery named hops for its other beers. The Fat Cat’s beer menu, in the same words from March 2001 to September 2010, gives Kelham Island Bitter “bitterness and flavour from the Mount Hood hops”, Easy Rider “Willamette hops from America’s Yakima Valley”, which is the hop Morton remembers using, and Sheffield Best Bitter the English dwarf hop First Gold. Pale Rider gets “a mixture of hops”. The brewery’s own page, across the same years, has it “flavoured with North American hops” and stops there. The bottles say it the same way. Three Kelham Island labels survive on the Brewery History Society’s wiki: Sheffield Cathedral Ale, best before February 2001, brewed with “the choicest hand-picked Worcestershire Fuggles hops”; Pride of Sheffield, best before June 2001, with “hand picked English Challenger and Fuggles Hops”; and Pale Rider, in the same house formula, with “hand-picked American hops”. None of them names a variety for it. Roger Protz does. His entry on the beer in 300 Beers to Try Before You Die, second edition, 2010, describing it just after its championship, says “the only hop is the American Willamette”, with Maris Otter pale malt and a touch of wheat malt. The notice of the brewery’s closure in May 2022 names the same hop, and has Pale Rider among the first British beers to use American hops. Thornbridge, who have brewed it since the 2022 rescue, list the bottle today at 5.2 per cent on Chinook, Willamette, Crystal and Cascade. That is where Cascade is on the record: in the beer now, under different owners.

Several things are true about that beer at once. Pete Brown calls it “an early example of the golden ale that has now come to dominate Britain’s cask ale revival”, and Protz, writing Wickett’s obituary in the Guardian, “one of the first of the new breed of golden ales”. Melissa Cole, in Let Me Tell You About Beer, says it was conceived to appeal to women drinkers. Protz’s 2004 profile has the brewery listening to its customers and making Pale Rider the less bitter for it. Ponce has written the fuller Sheffield account and it is worth your time.

Protz tells the story Wickett told him. The Fat Cat was refurbished and ready to open with no beer in it, so he rang Timothy Taylor in Keighley, was told Sheffield lay outside the delivery area, and drove over to fetch an eighteen-gallon cask of Landlord in the boot of his car. It went in two days. On three casks, Wickett “helped the brewery turn Landlord into a national brand”. Brown, who was supposed to be ghosting Wickett’s autobiography when he died, tells the same journey and names the beer: Landlord was his favourite, and it has never been out of stock in the Fat Cat. Maytag has long named the same beer behind Liberty Ale, though the dates complicate it: Liberty Ale was first brewed six months before he set foot in Keighley.

Asked by House whether Liberty Ale had influenced Wickett and his use of Cascade, Aaron Gore, an Advanced Cicerone who writes as The Ale Chaser, answered yes, with the reason he would not put it more strongly attached: “that would be overstating it, as I don’t have enough direct contact with Dave to confirm. But that is my understanding, correct.” Put the wider question to Roger Protz, whether Liberty Ale had been influential in Britain’s pale and hoppy movement, and the answer came back in two words: “Very influential.” Our Anchor piece sets out both exchanges. That makes a loop rather than a line: a Yorkshire beer in Maytag’s account of Liberty Ale, and Liberty Ale on sale in Wickett’s own city before Wickett’s own pale ale existed.

Thornbridge to BrewDog

Jim Harrison founded Thornbridge “at the suggestion of his friend David Wickett”, with Wickett promising to help him “get a brewery off the ground in exchange for using the site to contract brew additional batches of Kelham Island beers”. Simon Webster has described how that worked. Thornbridge “started brewing in October 2004”, he said, helping Wickett with “the sudden demand for his beer” after the championship, and “brewing once a week for Kelham Island, which was more often than we were brewing our own”. The brewery at Thornbridge Hall had two young brewers, Stefano Cossi and Martin Dickie, meaning to use American hops at low strengths for a British drinker.

The brief came from Wickett, in Harrison’s own recollection, published by Protz in BEER magazine in the autumn of 2021: “Dave Wickett said we needed a beer that would turn people’s heads.” In the same recollection, Wickett took one sip of Jaipur and said, “That’s a world beater”. Webster told House Dickie and Cossi devised the recipe, and that they knew Pale Rider. Thornbridge had the man as well. Boak and Bailey’s brewer family tree for Kelham Island has Paul Ward, who brewed the winning batch, as Thornbridge’s consultant head brewer in 2004 and a consultant in 2005.

Wickett was not the first British brewer to reach for an American hop. Franklin was working with Cascade in the early 1980s, and Dobbin was selling it over a Manchester bar by 1989. What Wickett did came later. Pale Rider came from his brewery and became a beer the whole country knew. Thornbridge existed at his suggestion, and Brown has him hiring the two young brewers himself. Jaipur came from a sentence he said to Harrison.

The low strengths lasted until the summer. Jaipur was first brewed on 7 June 2005, by Thornbridge’s own account, an IPA made with American hops, and poured at a garden party at the hall. Garrett has the brewers with “a bold, 6% American IPA in tank, saturated with Cascade, Chinook, Ahtanum, and Simcoe hops” by that summer, and Dickie ties that batch to the party himself: “I think the first batch must have been for that. We dry-hopped a few casks and cracked them on the day of the party.” Brown puts the appetite behind it plainly: the two brewers were “excited by new hops from America and New Zealand, which at that point had hardly been seen in Britain”, and Thornbridge “began brewing British cask ales with American hops, used American style”. Thornbridge list the beer at 5.9 per cent today, as Protz did in 2021. It was named after the city where Harrison had married. Dickie put the intention plainly to Garrett: “Jaipur was the first one where we tried to mimic the American IPA.” Brown, quoted by Garrett, says: “For me, Jaipur was the start of the British craft brewing scene.”

Jackson had a hand in this too. Early in 2006 Dickie and James Watt, who would found BrewDog with him, took an imperial stout they had aged in a Scotch whisky barrel to a London tasting Jackson was running, Dickie having met him a year before through Thornbridge. His account, given to Jon Henley ten years later, is that Jackson drank it and said: “Guys, you need to give up the day job.”

In June 2006 the White Horse hosted a celebration of American craft brewing with the Brewers Association and the USDA’s Foreign Agricultural Service. Dorber handled the food pairings; Jackson closed the evening. Almost fourteen years after Liberty Ale’s British draught debut, the same pub was welcoming American brewers with a US government agency as co-host. Doug Odell, one of five visiting brewery representatives, offered the evening’s verdict: “I sense we’re preaching to the choir.”

Dickie left Thornbridge at the end of that year, and BrewDog launched in 2007. Punk IPA arrived at about 6 per cent on a malt and hop bill similar to Jaipur’s, set apart by the New Zealand hop Motueka. Dickie, in the Henley interview, names a different New Zealand hop, Nelson Sauvin, as “what made this beer special”, but it is not in the published 2007 recipe. Mark Dredge has it in the beer in September 2009, which dates his report and not the change. The beer was dialled back to 5.6 per cent within a few years.

Garrett’s hop bill for the early Jaipur and the published original Punk recipe share Chinook and Ahtanum, though the Punk recipe does not establish its first batch. Cascade appears in BrewDog’s later dry-hopped recipe; the recipe book dates the equipment to 2010, not the hop’s addition. BrewDog’s own history takes the ambition further back, to “some old Cascade hops and a desire to re-create Sierra Nevada Pale Ale”. Cascade, the hop Protz singled out in Liberty Ale, is in all three beers as they are brewed now: Jaipur lists Chinook, Centennial, Ahtanum, Simcoe, Columbus and Cascade, Pale Rider lists it under Thornbridge, and Punk’s later published recipe includes it as a dry hop. The man who brewed Jaipur went and brewed Punk.

The leftovers

Bryan and Corbett were introduced to Citra on the 2009 trip, and what they were offered was what was left of “the third harvest of the hop”. Oakham say Barth Haas developed the variety, and that Sierra Nevada liked the early samples and asked for further development; Stan Hieronymus says “[Gene] Probasco made the cross in 1990 that resulted in the Citra seedling”. Oakham took about 450kg and Charles Faram took 900kg. Bryan rushed his share back by air and the first batch was brewed on 20 November 2009. Oakham’s history says “we can say with some certainty that Oakham was the first UK brewery” to brew a beer entirely with Citra.

What he did with it is the point. “John’s initial idea was to brew a strong, single hop IPA with Citra,” the brewery say, but “the stronger beers weren’t fast sellers for Oakham at the time”. So he brewed a 4.2 per cent beer with the hops he had flown over, and told Beer Today it was “my first sensible commercial decision at Oakham Ales”. Citra went to bi-monthly brews in 2010 and joined the permanent range in 2011. The strong IPA turned up that same year as Green Devil.

Thornbridge had faced the same question four years earlier and answered it the other way.

At Oakham, the hop crossed the Atlantic. The strength did not.

Back at Chiswick

Fuller’s is the brewery this story keeps touching: ESB west, Anchor’s beers back east in its own kegs, its yeast in a Californian fermenter. Its own American hop came earlier than its reputation suggests, and came quietly. Jackson had heard America in a Fuller’s IPA back in 1997 and named no hop for it. Discovery is the first American hop in a Fuller’s beer we can put a name to. Willard Clarke, reviewing for beer-pages, has Discovery, the blonde beer Fuller’s launched in May 2005, “brewed with barley, wheat, and Liberty and Saaz hops” and aimed at weaning the lager drinker onto ale. The Grocer trailed it that April as Fuller’s first blonde, 4.2 per cent in bottle, launched to compete with Belgian brands. In the same report Pete Brown, as a drinks consultant, said the United States was “looking at century-old British recipes” and that British brewers should take “a leaf out of their book by looking at other styles like Belgian beers”. Liberty is one of the four West Coast varieties Fuller’s would import for Wild River seven years later. Thornbridge first brewed Jaipur on 7 June 2005, with the same hop country and the opposite intention.

The loud beer took until 2012. Wild River went on the bar on 15 June 2012, nineteen years after Pale Rider and seven after Jaipur, and Protz reported it as a break with tradition at a brewery that had been “a firm believer in all-English ingredients”: 4.5 per cent, pale malt alone, and four American hops, Cascade, Chinook, Liberty and Willamette. John Keeling, Fuller’s head brewer, had found a precedent in the brewery’s own papers, old recipe books showing that Fuller’s imported Oregon hops and Californian barley in 1891. He used Cascade “sparingly”, for its “extreme bitterness and citrus character”, and leaned on the subtler Liberty, the hop Fuller’s had put in Discovery in 2005. His reason for the beer: “American micros, when they started, were influenced by British beer and now British brewers are being influenced by American craft brewers.”

Protz’s report ended on two conditions: the beer would run to the end of August, and if it went well Fuller’s might make it permanent and bottle it, stronger. A bottle duly appeared. The Beer Nut found one in Dublin in November 2014, past its date, and tasted an English beer under the American hops: “Under this there’s a lot of sugary malt, putting the whole thing much more in the English golden ale category than US-style pale ale.” Six years after Wild River, Fuller’s bought Dark Star.

What it became

By December 2018 Boak and Bailey were writing that “you can’t go wrong with anything with Citra in the name”, and calling Oakham Citra “the beer that effectively owns this unique American hop variety in the UK, and has done since 2009”. The variety had become a brand in the way a grape is a brand. By 2020 Thornbridge’s Rob Lovatt was saying that “the trend today is this Mosaic- and Citra-dominated profile, which means any muppet can make IPA”. The two remarks are the same observation from opposite ends.

The loop

The two men at the Sheffield end of this had read the same subject at the same university. Wickett built a British audience for pale and hoppy beer and gave Thornbridge the brief that became Jaipur. House sent Fuller’s west and brought Anchor back east. Between them the traffic ran both ways out of one city.

Alongside those American-style IPAs, Britain was building something lighter. Boak and Bailey describe the style as “the colour of pilsner, usually made with only pale malt”, carrying “extravagant, upfront New World hopping suggesting tropical fruits and aromatic flowers”, at a strength “somewhere between 3-5% ABV”. When they reach for an American comparison they name “the new breed of session IPAs and pale ales, such as Firestone Walker Easy Jack”.

Firestone Walker say Easy Jack is fermented with their house ale yeast, and David Walker’s own words for what that strain became are “still essentially a Fullers/London yeast”, which is the story we told in October. So the hop went east and the yeast went west, and the American beer Boak and Bailey reach for to explain the British session pale is fermented in California with a London yeast.
