The Return Crossing

How American hops came back across the Atlantic.

Some time after 1996, in Mark Tranter’s recollection, one of the owners of the Evening Star in Brighton went to California and came back with Cascade hop pellets. Dark Star was brewing in the pub at the time. Those pellets, with other American hops the merchants Charles Faram could supply in small quantities, became the basis of what the brewery’s founder Rob Jones called “The Hophead Club”, where each meeting tasted a different single-hopped beer. “Cascade was the customers’ and brewers’ favourite, so it was not long until that became the staple,” Tranter said.

That is how one brewery got its Cascade. Faram were already supplying American hops in small quantities. Then, in 2002, a British brewer went to the fields to choose for himself.

Gordon A. Ponce’s account of the Transatlantic Beer Bridge, published at The Craft Beer Finder in October 2026 and built on Jeffrey House’s research, follows the traffic west: Fuller’s ESB into America, London Pride into American distribution, Fuller’s yeast into a Californian fermenting tank. We have followed that span four times. This is the other carriageway.

Before the bridge

Michael Jackson’s World Guide to Beer appeared in 1977, and commenting under a 2012 timeline Martyn Cornell called it probably “the single most important event in the history of opening people’s minds in the UK to other types of beer”. Somewhere to buy the stuff came soon after. The Pitfield Street shop that became the Pitfield Brewery’s own “started life as the Two Brewers off-licence around 1980”, Cornell wrote in the same thread, “and was a vital source for beers from Belgium, especially, but also Germany, the US and elsewhere at a time when such beers were almost impossible to find in the UK”. Jackson’s Beer Hunter followed on Channel 4 in 1990.

Then the brewers went looking. Sean Franklin was experimenting with American Cascade “as far back as the early 1980s”. He left brewing, drove a taxi for five years, and came back to it in 1993. Brendan Dobbin established the West Coast Brewery at the Kings Arms in Chorlton-on-Medlock in 1989, the Brewery History Society records, and Beer52 say his Yakima Grande pale ale could well be the first example of American Cascade in British brewing. Dobbin moved the brewery to the King William IV in 1993 and it closed in August 1994.

Three beers arrived in 1993. Franklin’s Rooster’s Yankee was 4.3 per cent, a beer that “fit comfortably into British pub and beer festival culture” while being “a touch more aromatic than most UK drinkers were used to at the time”. Oakham brewed Jeffrey Hudson Bitter commercially for the first time on 13 September, and the brewery’s own history gives Mount Hood as the main hop with English Challenger for bittering, Willamette having been trialled first and not chosen: an American hop in the first batch of a beer voted “Supreme Champion Beer of Great Britain” in 2001. Boak and Bailey call JHB “a contemporary product developed quite independently”, inspired by Summer Lightning rather than by anything American. In Sheffield, Dave Wickett’s Kelham Island brewed Pale Rider, and Patrick Morton, who devised it, says that beer was American hopped as well. Three beers, three American hops, and two of the three went on to take the Champion Beer of Britain title.

The beers themselves were arriving too. In House’s account, which we set out in our piece on Fuller’s ESB in America, he brought Fritz Maytag and Anthony Fuller together at a distributor party at the Mark Hopkins Hotel in San Francisco, then encouraged Fuller’s to import Anchor in their own returnable 50-litre kegs. Fuller’s arranged a trial and the beer went into three London pubs in late 1991: the Mawson Arms, the Dove and the Churchill Arms. The 1993 Good Beer Guide independently records Fuller’s selling Maytag’s beers in Britain.

Liberty Ale reached a British handpump on 4 July 1992, at the White Horse at Parsons Green, and Jackson announced it that morning in The Independent under the headline “Take a long, serious draught of sweet Liberty”. It “appears on draught in Britain today for the first time”, he wrote. The hop is most of the column. Jackson called Cascade America’s traditional “aroma hop”, described the further charge added during cellaring as a final flourish “known as ‘dry-hopping’”, and gave the bouquet as “huge, flowery, piney, faintly citrus”. He ran it on Independence Day, about a beer he noted had first been brewed in 1975, for the anniversary of Paul Revere’s ride.

The White Horse was not one of the trial houses. Mark Dorber had looked after that cellar since 1981, and in 1990 had convened a seminar there on Burton pale ale which Roger Protz credits with the rebirth of IPA in Britain. The American beer landed in a bar already thinking hard about hops.

The road

In 2002 someone built a road. Oakham’s own history says John Bryan was “the first UK brewer to travel to the USA hop fields to select hops direct from the farms”, and that he went with Paul Corbett of Charles Faram. He returned for almost every harvest afterwards. Beer Today, writing when he retired in May 2022, says those visits “are widely acknowledged as a key factor in the increased availability of US-sourced hop varieties to UK brewers”.

Corbett is what joins the thing together, and he was in it nine years before the road was built. Morton has told House that he used whole American Willamette, that Corbett at Charles Faram could supply it, and that Cascade was not available to him at the time. Oakham had trialled Willamette for JHB that same year. Faram were the merchants behind the other American hops in Dark Star’s Hophead Club too, in the small quantities a tasting club needs. What 2002 changed was not whether a British brewer could buy American hops. It was whether he could stand in the field and choose them.

Sheffield

Wickett was one of the two Sheffield economists in the title of Ponce’s piece: an MA in economics at the university, then lecturing in it at the city’s polytechnic, now Sheffield Hallam. We set out those years in our Anchor piece. He bought a pub called the Alma and reopened it in 1981 as the Fat Cat, added the brewery in 1990, and the University of Sheffield’s obituary of him has that brewery as the city’s first new one in over 100 years. Pale Rider followed in 1993, and in 2004 it was named Champion Beer of Britain. James O’Hara, who led the group that rescued Kelham Island in 2022, called it “known and revered beyond Sheffield”. Jonny Garrett, writing in Good Beer Hunting, calls it “the first American-hopped beer to win the coveted prize”. Oakham’s own history has Mount Hood in the first JHB, which went on to win in 2001.

Three men had the recipe in eleven years. Morton devised it and handed over to Bob Lawson in early 1996, he says. Protz, writing the brewery up on 1 August 2004, names Paul Ward as the man who brewed the winning batch, “a part-timer and cask washer” when he arrived and head brewer by then, and gives Pale Rider at that point as 5.2 per cent, brewed from pale malt alone, with “American and German hops”.

Several things are true about that beer at once. Melissa Cole, in Let Me Tell You About Beer, says it was conceived to appeal to women drinkers. Protz’s same profile has the brewery listening to its customers and making Pale Rider the less bitter for it. Ponce has written the fuller Sheffield account and it is worth your time.

Asked by House about Liberty Ale’s influence on the movement, Protz answered, “Very influential”. His profile of Wickett names Timothy Taylor’s Landlord, first drunk on a visit to the Keighley brewery, as an inspiration. Aaron Gore, an Advanced Cicerone who writes as The Ale Chaser, understands Liberty Ale also influenced Wickett, but qualified it to House: “that would be overstating it, as I don’t have enough direct contact with Dave to confirm.”

Thornbridge to BrewDog

Jim Harrison founded Thornbridge “at the suggestion of his friend David Wickett”, with Wickett promising to help him “get a brewery off the ground in exchange for using the site to contract brew additional batches of Kelham Island beers”. Simon Webster has described how that worked. Thornbridge “started brewing in October 2004”, he said, helping Wickett with “the sudden demand for his beer” after the championship, and “brewing once a week for Kelham Island, which was more often than we were brewing our own”. The brewery at Thornbridge Hall had two young brewers, Stefano Cossi and Martin Dickie, meaning to use American hops at low strengths for a British drinker.

The brief came from Wickett, in Harrison’s own recollection, published by Protz in BEER magazine in the autumn of 2021: “Dave Wickett said we needed a beer that would turn people’s heads.” Harrison remembers him taking one sip of Jaipur and saying, “That’s a world beater”. Webster told House Dickie and Cossi devised the recipe, and that they knew Pale Rider.

The low strengths lasted until the summer. Jaipur was first brewed on 7 June 2005, by Thornbridge’s own account, an IPA made with American hops, and poured at a garden party at the hall. Garrett has the brewers with “a bold, 6% American IPA in tank, saturated with Cascade, Chinook, Ahtanum, and Simcoe hops” by that summer, which is the hop bill as he gives it rather than a record of that first batch. Thornbridge list the beer at 5.9 per cent today, as Protz did in 2021. It was named after the city where Harrison had married. Dickie put the intention plainly to Garrett: “Jaipur was the first one where we tried to mimic the American IPA.” Pete Brown, in the same piece, says: “For me, Jaipur was the start of the British craft brewing scene.”

In our reading, Wickett’s part runs through both beers: Pale Rider came from his brewery; Jaipur from the brief Dickie and Cossi answered.

In 2007 Dickie left Thornbridge to co-found BrewDog, where Punk IPA arrived at about 6 per cent on a similar malt and hop bill, set apart by the New Zealand hop Motueka, and dialled back to 5.6 within a few years. BrewDog’s own recipe book calls the original Punk “James and Martin’s original take”. The man who brewed the first one went and brewed the second.

The leftovers

Bryan and Corbett were introduced to Citra on the 2009 trip, and what they were offered was what was left of “the third harvest of the hop”. Oakham say Barth Haas developed the variety, and that Sierra Nevada liked the early samples and asked for further development; Stan Hieronymus says “[Gene] Probasco made the cross in 1990 that resulted in the Citra seedling”. Oakham took about 450kg and Charles Faram took 900kg. Bryan rushed his share back by air and the first batch was brewed on 20 November 2009. Oakham’s history says “we can say with some certainty that Oakham was the first UK brewery” to brew a beer entirely with Citra.

What he did with it is the point. “John’s initial idea was to brew a strong, single hop IPA with Citra,” the brewery say, but “the stronger beers weren’t fast sellers for Oakham at the time”. So he brewed a 4.2 per cent beer with the hops he had flown over, and told Beer Today it was “my first sensible commercial decision at Oakham Ales”. Citra went to bi-monthly brews in 2010 and joined the permanent range in 2011. The strong IPA turned up that same year as Green Devil.

Thornbridge had faced the same question four years earlier and answered it the other way.

At Oakham, the hop crossed the Atlantic. The strength did not.

What it became

By December 2018 Boak and Bailey were writing that “you can’t go wrong with anything with Citra in the name”, and calling Oakham Citra “the beer that effectively owns this unique American hop variety in the UK, and has done since 2009”. The variety had become a brand in the way a grape is a brand. By 2020 Thornbridge’s Rob Lovatt was saying that “the trend today is this Mosaic- and Citra-dominated profile, which means any muppet can make IPA”. The two remarks are the same observation from opposite ends.

The loop

Alongside those American-style IPAs, Britain was building something lighter. Boak and Bailey describe the style as “the colour of pilsner, usually made with only pale malt”, carrying “extravagant, upfront New World hopping suggesting tropical fruits and aromatic flowers”, at a strength “somewhere between 3-5% ABV”. When they reach for an American comparison they name “the new breed of session IPAs and pale ales, such as Firestone Walker Easy Jack”.

Firestone Walker say Easy Jack is fermented with their house ale yeast, and David Walker’s own words for what that strain became are “still essentially a Fullers/London yeast”, which is the story we told in October. So the hop went east and the yeast went west, and the American beer Boak and Bailey reach for to explain the British session pale is fermented in California with a London yeast.
