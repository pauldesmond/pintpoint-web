DispatchJ-dag, Friday 6 November 2026 · Beer history

Christmas starts at 20:59

A thousand years of Christmas ale, from a Norwegian king who made it compulsory to a San Franciscan who thought he was the only one left doing it.

At 20:59 tonight — one minute to nine, and not a minute before — Tuborg's Julebryg goes on sale across Denmark. Brewery staff leave their desks, put on blue elf hats, and reach more than four hundred bars in a single night. Horse-drawn wagons in blue finery go out into the streets of Copenhagen. It is called J-dag, it has run since 1990, and it is the closest thing European brewing has to a starting pistol.

Three nights in seven days, and not one of them is as old as it says. This is the third of three.

When brewing Christmas beer was the law

In tenth-century Norway, Christmas ale was not a seasonal treat. It was a legal obligation. Haakon the Good, bringing Christianity to a country that already had a midwinter feast it was not about to give up, bound the new Christmas to the old jól — and under the Gulating law every farmer was required to brew a measured quantity of strong ale from his own grain. Fail to do it three years running and the farm went to the king and the bishop, and you went into exile.

The ale was blessed to Odin, to Frey and to Njord, for peace and a good harvest. The Norwegian for celebrating the season is still drikke jul — to drink Yule. Not to keep it, or observe it. To drink it.

Why the winter beer was the strong one

Two things made midwinter the moment for the best beer of the year, and neither was sentiment.

The first was the grain. By late October the harvest was in, malted and dry, and a brewer had more and better material than at any other point in the calendar. The second was the cold. Before refrigeration, summer brewing spoiled — many regions simply banned it between spring and Michaelmas — while crisp autumn air chilled the wort and allowed long, clean fermentations. Brewing the strongest and most complex beer of the year in the dark months was good microbiology three centuries before anybody knew what a microbe was.

The monasteries had a third reason, and it is the best footnote in brewing. Advent meant fasting, but medieval doctrine held that liquidum non frangit ieiunium — liquid does not break the fast. So the men keeping long vigils in unheated stone rooms brewed something thick and sweet and full of calories, and drank their dinner. Under the Rule of St Benedict the cellar ran three tiers: the first, strongest runnings for the abbot and important guests, a middle beer for travellers and pilgrims, and a thin table beer for the brothers themselves. At Christmas, when the hospitality obligation peaked and half of Europe was on the road in the cold, the cellarer opened the good barrels.

England had a bowl

England's Christmas drink was not a beer so much as an event. The wassail bowl held strong spiced ale with sugar, nutmeg and ginger, and roasted crab apples dropped in hot until they burst — the pale froth they threw up gave the drink its name, lamb's wool. It went from house to house, and on Twelfth Night it went out to the orchards, where drinkers poured ale over the apple roots, shouted at the trees and fired shotguns into the branches to frighten off whatever might spoil next year's crop.

That is a tradition with a pulse. By the twentieth century it survived mainly as a line in a carol. The winter ales that came afterwards are a revival of the idea rather than a continuation of it — worth holding on to, because it means anyone looking for English Christmas beer in the nineteen-seventies was looking at the remains of something, not the thing itself.

England has no starting pistol. It has a horse. Winter begins here unofficially, on whichever late-October morning Young's sends the dray out — this year the twenty-third, a fortnight ago — carrying the first barrels of Winter Warmer, a ruby ale almost red in bright light, to The Lamb Tavern in Leadenhall Market. It arrives at ten, the first pint is poured on the Victorian cobbles, and a full English is laid on for anyone up early enough to drink it. The White Cross in Richmond runs its own from eight, sittings at eight, ten and midday: the dray horses come along the towpath, Morris dancers turn out in the road, and inside there is breakfast and Cockney carol singers — Carradine's Cockney Sing-a-long at an upright piano, under a banner promising a good old-fashioned kneesup. This October was the thirty-fifth time they have done it. Those two pubs are the only places in the country that get the beer first.

That is how a season starts in England: not announced, just noticed, by whoever happens to be in Leadenhall at ten on a Friday. Denmark leaves nothing to inference. Christmas begins at 20:59 tonight, everywhere at once, and not a minute before.

Everyone else

Belgium looks like the deepest tradition of the lot and is one of the youngest. The beer historian Roel Mulder went through the Belgian archives and found no ancient monastic kerstbier at all: what cafés in Brussels and Charleroi were advertising as "Christmas! Christmas!" in the 1890s and 1900s was imported British beer — MacEwan's and Younger's Scotch ales, Allsopp's Burton ales. Belgian drinkers liked them enough to ask Belgian brewers to make something similar, and the style everyone now thinks of as immemorially Flemish grew out of that request. The modern descendants are enormous — St Bernardus Christmas Ale at 10%, bottle-conditioned and brewed for the season alone; De Dolle's Stille Nacht and Bush de Noël pushing past 12%; Delirium Noël alongside them. The Essen Christmas beer festival pours a couple of hundred different winter ales at cellar temperature, in the proper glassware, because it would be unthinkable not to.

Switzerland produced the most extreme version of the idea. Samichlaus was first brewed by Hürlimann of Zürich in 1979, on 6 December — St Nicholas's day — and only ever on that date, then laid down for ten months before release the following Christmas. At 14% it held the record as the strongest naturally fermented beer in the world, and it is still cellared by people who treat vintages the way other people treat claret.

Germany brews Weihnachtsbier and winter bocks that are malt-forward and barely spiced at all. Norway never stopped: Norwegian beer writers count more than three hundred juleøl on sale there each winter, most brewed locally, a thousand years after Haakon made it compulsory.

And America built its own version of J-dag without noticing. Great Lakes of Cleveland launched a honey, cinnamon and ginger Christmas Ale in 1992; the annual First Pour Day now draws queues down the block in October cold, and bars plan a season's takings around how many barrels they can get hold of before Christmas Eve. One Danish brewery decided when Christmas begins by decree. One Ohio brewery had it decided for them by the people queuing.

The man who thought he was alone

In November 1975, in San Francisco, Fritz Maytag brewed a Christmas beer because he had gone looking for the tradition and concluded it was gone. As far as he could discover, he said, his would be "the only real Christmas brew in the world." It had "recently disappeared, I think, even in Scandinavia, where it had lingered."

He was right about Denmark, and wrong about almost everywhere else. Tuborg's Julebryg was six years away and J-dag fifteen — so the country with the starting pistol genuinely had nothing at the time. But Norway had never stopped, Belgium had never stopped, and Aass of Drammen had brewed a Christmas beer in 1877, nineteen years before the Anchor Brewing Company existed under that name.

He was wrong about his own country too, and for a reason worth knowing. America had Christmas beer well before Prohibition — Barmann of Kingston, New York may have the earliest claim — and Miller was still selling a Christmas Special Beer in the mid-1930s, under a label of a family round the fire. Then the lawyers arrived. As the beer historian Don Russell puts it, a few years after Prohibition most states banned any reference to Christmas or Santa Claus on a label, so the Christmas beers became Holiday ales. Ballantine's version got round it by not being for sale at all: executives sent it out as a gift. The American tradition had not died. It had been legislated into a euphemism, which from the far side looks much the same.

He had even been standing in the exception a month earlier. Maytag's October 1975 tour of English breweries began at Young's Ram Brewery in Wandsworth — and Young's had Winter Warmer. It had not been called that for long. It was a Burton ale, the old strong dark style that all but died through the sixties as drinkers went to bitter and lager, and by then Young's was more or less the last brewery in England still making one. In 1971 they renamed it Winter Warmer and gave it a season, which is what a brewery does to a beer it is trying to save. The category we now call winter warmers may date from that single decision. He started at a brewery with a winter ale and came home convinced nobody was making one. Though England was thinner than it looks from here: Samuel Smith's of Tadcaster, another stop on the same trip, had no Christmas beer at all and would not have one until the late 1980s.

None of which is a gotcha. It is what the world looked like from California in 1975, before anyone could check. He went looking with the tools he had, found nothing, and brewed one anyway — and then did something nobody else was doing: changed the recipe every single year and kept quiet about it, and put a different hand-drawn tree on the label each time — Jim Stitt has drawn them since the seventies and is in his nineties still. Not Christmas trees, either: Maytag chose them for the solstice rather than the Nativity, so the run includes a Joshua tree, a blue gum eucalyptus, birches from his daughter's wedding and a coconut palm copied from a photograph he took in Hawaii. The man who would not print "Season's Greetings" was not going to put a standard-issue fir on the bottle. Collecting them became a ritual of its own; there is a binder of them behind the bar at Anchor Public Taps. The spices that now define the beer only arrived in 1987, when Maytag wanted something special for his own wedding, threw a handful into the previous year's batch, and found he had started another tradition by accident.

Whose is it

A Norwegian king made it compulsory. Monks made it strong because they were hungry. English villagers poured it on apple trees. A Swiss brewery makes one batch a year and waits ten months. A Danish brewery turned it into a national event with a start time. An American brewed one because he thought the whole thing had died, and was wrong.

The argument was never whether the tradition survived. It is whose it is — and the answer is that it has never belonged to any of them. Tonight at 20:59, Denmark takes its turn.

Julebryg reaches a handful of British bars each year, and a fair few of the others turn up on UK taps between now and January. PINtPOINT will know before we do.
