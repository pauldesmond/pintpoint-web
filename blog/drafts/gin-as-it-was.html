Gin as it was

Two gin brands tell a story about where their recipe came from. Neither story survives being asked where it came from. Brewing had the same argument a century and a half ago and settled it with a bag of salts.

Every bottle of gin on the back bar is telling you where it came from. Almost none of them can show you.

This is a beer paper, so start with the beer. In the middle of the nineteenth century every brewer in Britain wanted the water that came out of the ground at Burton upon Trent, because beer brewed in it tasted better than the same beer brewed anywhere else. Hard, and loaded with sulphates, calcium sulphate above all, which sharpens the bitterness and dries the finish and pushes the hops to the front of the glass. We have written about what that did to Bass.

Nobody shipped their beer to Burton. They worked out what was in the water and put it in their own, and the process is still called Burtonisation. It is one of the least romantic solutions in the history of drink and it worked completely.

Gin went the other way.

The recipe book nobody has read

Jensen’s is the gin that tells the best story in London, and the fullest version of it is published by Difford’s Guide.

In 2001 Christian Jensen, an IT specialist at a bank, was posted to Tokyo, and after a hard day found his way into one of the city’s hidden bars: one dim room, an old plank for a counter, and a man behind it who introduced himself as Oda-San and said he looked like he could use a drink. What arrived was a Martini made with London gin and a twist of lime. Jensen went back almost nightly. On his last visit Oda-San gave him the final bottle, its label worn down until all that could be read was “don Gin”.

Back in London, Jensen set a concierge service to find more. What it found instead was a public records office holding the papers of the distillery that had made it, hundreds of books and documents running from the mid eighteen hundreds to the nineteen sixties, issued five at a time. Jensen worked through the lot, found a recipe book, went back with a camera, and photographed it. Then he took the photographs and the dregs of the Tokyo bottle to Charles Maxwell at Thames Distillers in Clapham, and after ten to fifteen trial distillations had something he was happy with. He ordered eight hundred cases, mostly for himself, and sold the rest to the Hide Bar on Bermondsey Street and Bedales in Borough Market so that he could drink his Martini out of the house.

The name of the original gin turned out to belong to a large drinks company that had not used it for years and would not let it go, so he called it Bermondsey instead.

It is a wonderful story and there is no reason to doubt any of it. The difficulty is what was done with it next.

Among the other recipes Jensen had photographed was one from a book dated to the late eighteen forties, for Old Tom gin. Simon Difford, who tells all this, looked at it and saw there was no sugar in it: only sweet botanicals. He reasoned on the page that sugar was an expensive commodity at that date and would not have been viable in a gin for the mass market. He asked Jensen to make it. Jensen made it, and Jensen’s Old Tom went out into a market where every other Old Tom was sweetened.

That single reading, of one recipe, in one book, on one afternoon, is now most of the reason the drinks trade believes that proper Old Tom has no sugar in it.

Two things are worth saying about it plainly. The first is that Difford is not a bystander in his own account: he lives in Bermondsey, he was drinking in the Hide Bar when Jensen was placing his first cases there, he tasted the gin, he was converted, he introduced Jensen to the distributor who took the brand on, and then he asked for the Old Tom. That does not make the account wrong. It does mean the most detailed history of Jensen’s gin is written by someone who helped put it on the shelf.

The second is that nobody else has seen the documents. We are not told the brand on the Tokyo bottle, nor which records office, nor whose distillery. The whole provenance rests on photographs in one man’s possession, and the conclusion drawn from them was drawn once.

It also runs against the best evidence there is. David Wondrich, in the Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails, has Old Tom as a retailer’s category rather than a distiller’s: publicans bought spirit from the rectifiers at fixed strengths and made their own cordial gin with sugar and water, and Old Tom was the strongest of them, with the least sugar and water in it, at around thirty-five grams of sugar a litre. That is not very sweet. It is not nothing either.

What Old Tom actually was

Not a brand, not a trademark and not a legal category. The Gin Guild says plainly that there are no strict regulations defining it. In Great Britain the spirit drinks rules know gin, distilled gin and London gin, and nothing else, so an Old Tom with sugar in it cannot be sold as London gin, which is held to a tenth of a gram of sweetening a litre.

The name does not appear in print until 1810 or 1812, depending on which historian found which newspaper first, which is about seventy years after the gin craze it is usually attached to. The black cat is not evidence of anything: the cat on a barrel is Boord’s trade device from about 1849, and no original gin-shop cat sign is known to survive.

The quest that was already a supply chain

Martin Miller’s Gin tells its own founding story on its own site, and it is a good one. A bar in Notting Hill, the spring of 1998, three friends served three bad gin and tonics. Miller challenges his two friends to do something about the state of gin. He works out that splitting the distillation, earthy botanicals in one run and bitter orange and lemon peel in another, and marrying the two afterwards, gives a better balance than doing it all at once. He is right, and it does.

Then comes the water. Gin is mostly water, so Miller goes looking for the best there is, and the quest takes him to Borgarnes, a village on the remote west coast of Iceland, seventy-five kilometres north of Reykjavik, where the spring water began as snow that fell more than eight hundred years ago, froze into dormant volcanic calderas, was melted from beneath by the volcano and stripped of its minerals on the way out through the rock. The spirit is distilled in England, shipped to Iceland, cut with that water, and shipped back.

Miller’s own page tells this as a search. It calls it his quest for perfection, and it does not name either of the two friends.

The obituary the Eccentric Club wrote for him names them, David Bromige and Andreas Versteegh, and explains Iceland rather differently. Miller turned to Bromige, who was already making vodka in Iceland, and asked why he did not make a gin. The vodka was called Pulsar, and William Grant bought it some years later and renamed it Reyka.

So the water was not found at the end of a search. One of the three men in the pub already had a distillery there.

That is worth putting fairly, because it does not make the water story false. Low-mineral water is genuinely different to work with, the two-part distillation is a real piece of craft, and a decision can be both convenient and correct at the same time. Miller himself was cheerfully straight about how odd it looked: shipping gin to Iceland at the height of the vodka boom, he said, was a crazy thing to be doing. He died on Christmas Eve in 2013, a year after being diagnosed, and by then the gin was turning over something like fourteen million pounds a year and getting the credit for starting the gin revival.

What is not true is the shape of the telling. A brand page that has a man scouring the earth for the purest water, and leaves out that his business partner was already distilling in the place he found, is not describing a discovery. It is describing a supply chain and calling it a pilgrimage.

What Burton settled

Which is where the beer comes back in, because brewing ran this argument first and came out the other side.

Burton water was the most valuable natural asset in British brewing and everybody knew it. The response was not to move the breweries or ship the beer. It was to find out what was in it and add the same things to the water in London, Edinburgh and Dublin, and the fact that we still call it Burtonisation rather than something grander tells you exactly how much mystique survived the process.

A brewer in 1880 would have found the idea of carrying spirit across the North Atlantic for the water on the other side completely intelligible as a marketing decision and completely unnecessary as a technical one.

In fairness, and this needs checking before it goes in, the chemistry does not run symmetrically. You can make soft water hard by adding salts to it. You cannot make hard water soft the same way, because you are taking things out rather than putting them in, and that is a harder and more expensive thing to do. So a distiller who wants very soft water does have a reason to go where very soft water already is, in a way that a brewer who wants hard water never did.

But that argument would be made on the label if it were the real one, and it is not the one being made. The label says eight hundred years of snow.

Why any of this matters to a beer drinker

Because the same thing is coming for beer, and in places it has already arrived.

Gin got there first because gin had further to go. It spent most of the twentieth century as a colourless industrial product with no story at all, and when the revival came there was nothing in the cupboard to be authentic about, so the heritage had to be assembled from whatever was lying around: a recipe book, a cat, a captain with a slot machine, a volcano. Beer did not have that problem, because beer never lost its paperwork. The brewing records survived. The pub names survived. Mayhew counted the men selling it.

That is the only real advantage we have, and it is worth noticing when it is being spent. A brewery that tells you its recipe came from a Victorian brewing book can usually show you the book. When it cannot, the right question is the same one that undoes both of these gin stories, and it is not a hostile question. It is the most ordinary question in the world.
