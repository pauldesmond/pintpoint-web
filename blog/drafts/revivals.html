These aren't revivals — they're hauntings

"I long for the nostalgia, and the thought I might be drinking something resembling something my Father may have drunk. But in reality the 70s offerings would probably not be to my taste buds anyway. Clearly, the choices would be closer to a Nicholson's Pale Ale or Greene King IPA than the heavily hopped west coasters of today. However, I for one fully support breweries attempting it, even if it's just a thinly veiled reason to sell crap beer to mugs like me."

When a beer returns, what exactly has been resurrected — the recipe, the badge, the strength, the package, or just the feeling?

Before the nostalgia gets too comfortable, an inconvenient voice. David Behrens, writing in the Yorkshire Post about the Sheffield revival of Stones Bitter, opens with a shrug: "I can think of little about the 1970s that I would wish to inflict upon the present generation." He calls that era the age of the beerage — the six-brewery cartel that owned the whole route from brewery to beer mat. Roger Protz made the same argument in 1978. In 2018 he had to make it again, forty years on. Plus ça change.

British beer revivals are happening inside a corporate landscape that has barely changed since the era being revived. That is worth stating before we get to Watney's Party Seven or the return of the little cartoon fellow. The interesting question is not whether to bring back the beer your dad drank. It is which version of it, and who is doing the work.

The 6-tier honesty spectrum

Revivals fall on a gradient. The further from a marketing department, the more credible.

Allsopp's vs Burton Bridge — the spine of the argument

Two revivals. Same broad heritage. Opposite philosophies.

Allsopp's Double Diamond (Tier 3) relaunched in October 2024 as a 3.8% pale ale "inspired by" the 1922 recipe. Hops: Citra, Columbus, Mosaic — the 2010s American craft palette. Jamie Allsopp's own launch text is candid, if not by intention: "Revived as a 3.8% pale ale that drinks like a lager." The original was a strong IPA. The 2023 alcohol duty reform, which taxes anything below 3.5% at a lower rate, was doing more of the editorial work than the archive was.

To be fair to Allsopp's, the older Double Diamond was not automatically beloved. Comments under the trade-press coverage of the revival are brutal on the original: "universally loathed", "sickly sweet", "full of caramel and sticky". The badge is iconic; the liquid may still have been bad. A revival that produces a better beer than the original is not a failure. A revival that pretends the original is what it is bringing back, when it isn't, is.

Burton Bridge DBA (Tier 6a) launched in March 2015. The brewers had worked on the original Ind Coope Draught Burton Ale before Carlsberg discontinued it. Their own framing is unmistakably humble: "It would be impossible to brew an exact replica, but we are sure it is as close as possible." 4.8%. Drinkers who remember the original describe it as "smooth, sweet, a little bitter", "absolutely gorgeous, rich and almost sticky on the palate". One revival is selling you a 1922 ledger and a 2024 hop bill. The other is saying: we worked on this once, we know what it tasted like, we got as close as we could.

The cleanest benchmark for Tier 1 is Hofmeister, of all things. Discontinued in 2003, revived from 2015 by two ex-Heineken employees who bought naming rights from Heineken and brewed a proper Bavarian Helles in Market Schwaben. It won Best Lager at IWSC 2017. And the brewery's own account of what it did is exactly the sentence Allsopp's should have used: "The permission to use the name and the bear's likeness was about all they wanted of the old Hofmeister: the beer itself was to be completely new." That is Tier 1 honesty done properly. Allsopp's failure is not that it made a modern beer. It is that it asks the archive to do more rhetorical work than the recipe can support.

Which era are you reviving?

This is the axis that saves the piece from soft-focus nostalgia. A 1970s brand can revive the 1970s recipe — usually cold, carbonated, industrial keg — or it can leapfrog the bad years and revive the beer that corporate brewing interrupted.

Behrens's Stones Bitter is the clearest case. He remembers the 1970s Stones as "ghastly" and "gassy", the "gaudy orange badge on the pump" that "embodied everything that was wrong with Britain at that time". But he also blesses the True North Brew Co revival in Sheffield, on precisely the ground that True North is not returning to the keg era. They are returning to the beer that pre-dated it: cask, drawn from the wood, with the recipe adjusted to replenish the salt Sheffield steelworkers lost sweating in the foundries.

"Its rebirth brings it full circle," Behrens writes. The pre-industrial version had a purpose. The industrial one did not. The revival worth having is the one that returns to purpose. That framing turns out to apply to several of the more credible current cases — Burton Bridge DBA reviving the recipe genealogy that predates DBA's own 1976 naming, Jennings reopening cask-first before Carlsberg-Marston's closed the brewery, Bass Pale Ale surviving as cask against the keg tide.

The corporate scaffolding

Protz's forty-year loop is not incidental. Most revivals happen inside the paperwork of the same handful of aggregators. Heineken inherited Watney's and Truman's from the 2008 Scottish & Newcastle acquisition. Carlsberg-Marston's holds the Ind Coope / Jennings / Banks's / Bombardier trail. AB InBev retains the Boddingtons brand. Molson Coors runs Carling. The aggregators are the gatekeepers, not usually the executors. The genuinely interesting work happens when the IP is licensed out — or abandoned — into the hands of people with a regional or technical reason to care.

Carlsberg is the recurring villain. It discontinued Ind Coope's Draught Burton Ale, and Burton Bridge picked it up. It closed Jennings Brewery in Cockermouth in November 2022, and the Canfields bought the site back and reopened. Its Marston's operation has also axed Banks's Mild and Bombardier in recent years. The pattern is uncomfortably neat: kill the brand, let it be rescued by people who do it better.

Truman's is the most architecturally honest of these hand-off cases. The Brick Lane brewery closed in 1989, was revived by two young professionals in 2013, and — per Des de Moor — genuinely retrieved the original Truman yeast from the National Collection of Yeast Cultures. That makes it more technically serious than a badge-only reboot: yeast-authentic, recipe-modern, ownership awkward (the brand was later sold on to the property company that owns the Brick Lane site).

Project Jurassic Beer

A newer case worth flagging. In August 2026 the Belgian diver Stefan Panis recovered an intact bottle of Guinness from the wreck of the sailing barque Mindoro, which sank in the English Channel about three miles off Dover on 27 November 1864. The bottle survived the sinking and 162 years underwater. Diageo has samples with KU Leuven for microbiological analysis, and has named the initiative Project Jurassic Beer — an attempt to DNA-sequence the yeast in the bottle and use the strain to recreate the original recipe. His fellow diver put it best: "we weren't just holding an old artifact. We were holding a flawlessly sealed time capsule of liquid history."

If the yeast recovery works, this becomes the first case in the whole revival category where a mega-corporate parent — not a hobbyist, not a regional independent — attempts genuine biological continuity. It is Truman's National Collection of Yeast Cultures move at Guinness scale. And the name of the initiative is the strongest editorial signal that whoever's running it inside Diageo knows what a revival actually is: not corporate-heritage boilerplate, not "reimagining a favourite", but a self-aware admission that the goal is to bring the actual thing back.

The duty band is the invisible editor

Since August 2023, UK alcohol duty is charged on strength rather than category, with anything below 3.5% taxed at a lower rate. The recipe rewrite has been following the spreadsheet, not the ledger. Belhaven Twisted Thistle IPA came back at 4.0% versus an original 5.6%. Carling Black Label at 4.7% versus an original around 4%. Allsopp's Double Diamond at 3.8% versus an original around 6%. Marketing text will call this "evolving the range" or "designed for today's beer market" or "drinks like a lager". The tax band is doing the real editorial work.

The hop list is the receipt

Within seconds of reading a revival's spec sheet, the hops tell you almost everything. Modern American C-hops — Citra, Centennial, Columbus, Cascade, Mosaic, Galaxy — are the 2010s craft-IPA palette. Classic English hops — Fuggles, Goldings, Challenger, Bramling Cross, Target, Pilgrim, plus Central European Celeia and Saaz — are the nineteenth- and twentieth-century British palette. A heritage revival that uses American C-hops while claiming "as it was in 1922" is either being honest about reinventing the beer or pretending the historical anchor is real when it isn't.

Compare the specs. Allsopp's Double Diamond, claiming 1922 inspiration: Citra, Columbus, Mosaic. Jennings, reviving its own 1990s core: Fuggles, Goldings, Challenger and Celeia hops, with Maris Otter malt and — non-negotiably — the brewery's own Cumbrian well water. That last part is not a marketing point. It is the recipe. Burton Bridge DBA: traditional Burton-Ale style. Modern hops are not bad; there is nothing wrong with a Citra/Mosaic pale ale. The problem is only rhetorical — if the archive is what is being sold, the hop list has to match the archive.

Jennings is worth pausing on because it does the disclosure gracefully. Sneck Lifter — Northern dialect for a last sixpence, the money to lift a pub-door latch. Cumberland Ale, the North West bestseller. Cocker Hoop, a cask-spigot joke. And "Back Yam", introduced in 2026 to mark the revival and openly labelled as new, not smuggled in as heritage. That single word is the discipline the revival category needs more of.

Boddingtons and the gateway-drug case

Boddingtons is the corporate-licensed regional hybrid in its cleanest form. AB InBev owns the brand. JW Lees brews the cask revival in Manchester. The editorial jewel of the licence agreement — described by JW Lees' production director Michael Lees-Jones — is that the brewery's own house yeast is a "non-negotiable" clause. Corporate IP, local biology, cask handpull. It is a specific answer to "which era": pre-corporate, pre-keg, pre-Interbrew acquisition.

The CAMRA argument for it is generous but not naive. Boddingtons is not reinventing the wheel. There are more interesting pints at any given handpull, JW Lees's own included. But a familiar name lowers the barrier for younger drinkers who find a row of unknown handpulls intimidating — and the numbers seem to back it. Bass Pale Ale has reportedly grown 19% year-on-year; early Boddingtons demand in Manchester outstripped supply. Heritage brands are turning out to be a cask-beer gateway drug.

The cost is real too. The world's largest brewer is muscling back onto bar space that would otherwise go to smaller independents. Two things at once: familiar names bring new drinkers to cask, and they crowd out the brewers who kept cask interesting while the big brands were away. The revival argument does not have to resolve that tension. It just has to name it.

The shelf-presence defence

None of this rules out simply wanting the badge on the shelf. A flawed Double Diamond next to a John Bull cartoon is a better Tesco end-cap than another row of identical craft-lager cans that nobody will remember in two years. That is a real editorial position, and it survives the honesty audit — as long as no-one is pretending the liquid is what it isn't.

But some beers will never get a licensed revival. No corporate owner will bring them back; no supermarket will make room for them. Those beers belong somewhere else: in archive recipes, old brewery booklets, homebrewers' sheds, and memory. Which is where Romford's John Bull Bitter — and a few other ghost beers — may have to live.

Verdict

What you actually want from a beer revival is honesty about which era is being revived, which part of the beer is being brought back, and who is doing the brewing. The 6-tier spectrum is the map. The hop list is the receipt. The duty band is the invisible editor. The corporate owner is usually the gatekeeper. And Behrens's Stones test is the hardest question: are you reviving the beer people remember, or the better beer that existed before the memory got industrialised?

The best current revivals — Burton Bridge DBA, Jennings under the Canfields, True North's pre-1970s Stones, maybe Boddingtons if the Manchester yeast does enough work — are the ones that say: we know we cannot bring the past back exactly, but we can get close enough that the feeling survives. Hofmeister makes the same peace by declaring the beer completely new, which is at least an honest deal.

The rest are hauntings. Nice packaging. Wrong beer. As Behrens closes his own piece: I might even try a half myself — in a glass with a handle, please, landlord.
