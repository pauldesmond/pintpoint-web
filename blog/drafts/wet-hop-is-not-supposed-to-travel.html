Wet hop is not supposed to travel

A beer defined by never leaving the farm is on at Borough Market for eighteen pounds a pint.

The price list at the Rake in Winchester Walk has Driftless Drummer at £18 a pint. Two thirds is £12, a half is £9, a third is £6. That is exactly pro rata, which is rarer than the headline figure: most bars make the small measure the expensive way to drink. The Tegernseer below it on the same list divides the same way, 7.50 and 5.00 and 3.75 and 2.50, and Somer Valley’s Furrow is pro rata rounded up to the nearest ten pence. Whatever else the Rake is doing, it is not punishing anyone for asking for a third.

What the £18 buys is a 6.5 per cent wet hop IPA from Humble Forager, a brewery in the Driftless Area of Wisconsin. Our tap list had it pouring there on 19 November.

A wet hop beer is the one style that is not supposed to be able to do that. Hops are normally kilned within hours of picking, because undried cones heat and spoil. A wet hop beer skips the kiln, which means the brewery has to be close enough to the hop garden to get the cones into the copper while they are still green and wet. That is why the British green hop season is a fortnight in September, mostly in Kent and Herefordshire, and why it has always been the nearest thing beer has to Beaujolais Nouveau: a drink you have to go to, made from something that cannot wait.

This one came 4,000 miles in a can.

The way it does that is in the small print on the label. The beer is made with krush and nelson sauvin as cold pressed juice: the cones are pressed at the farm and what travels is the liquid, not the cone. The kiln is skipped and the distance is not. Nelson Sauvin is a New Zealand variety, so the beer in Winchester Walk is a Wisconsin wet hop IPA carrying a hop grown on the other side of the Pacific.

Krush is newer than that. The Hop Breeding Company, a joint venture between Yakima Chief Ranches and John I. Haas, released it commercially on 13 August 2024 as Krush, having bred it as HBC 586. It is in Bermondsey already: the Kernel say they were the first brewery in the UK to get it in both pellet and whole cone, and brewed three beers with it. Their brewery is a fifteen-minute walk from the Rake’s door.

The name is not a drum kit. The Driftless Area is the corner of the upper Midwest the last glaciers went round instead of over, so it kept its hills and its valleys while everything about it was flattened. The male ruffed grouse that lives in those woods displays by beating its wings fast enough to make a low drumming you feel more than hear. Humble Forager brewed the beer with the Driftless Chapter of the Ruffed Grouse Society and put the profits into conservation in the region, including a grouse conservation plan on the Whitewater Wildlife Management Area.

So the eighteen pounds is buying a beer whose style is defined by not travelling, brewed in Wisconsin with a hop from New Zealand and a hop released two years ago, sold in a railway arch by Borough Market, with the margin going to woodland management four thousand miles away.
