The Third Thursday

A wine with a start time, and a beer festival that began as a wedding.

This year’s Beaujolais Nouveau goes on sale tomorrow, and the grower does not pick the day. Decanter dates the current rule to 1985, when the INAO, France’s appellation body, settled the release on the third Thursday of November. This year that is the nineteenth.

The shape rhymes with the Wiesn. A date announced in advance, nothing sold before it.

Munich, 12 October 1810

Oktoberfest began as a wedding. Crown Prince Ludwig married Princess Therese of Saxe-Hildburghausen on 12 October 1810, and by the festival’s own account the official festivities ran five days and ended on the seventeenth with a horse race on a meadow outside the city gates. The Historisches Lexikon Bayerns credits that race to the Munich civic militia, and the militia with the suggestion that the ground be named the Theresienwiese after the bride, which is why Munich calls the whole thing the Wiesn. The HLB records the race being run until 1913.

Major Andreas von Dall’Armi asked the royal household to do it again. King Max I Joseph agreed in 1811, and the agriculture arrived with the repeat: from 1811 to 1818 the Agricultural Association in Bavaria ran the festival and added a cattle market, the show that still exists as the Zentral-Landwirtschaftsfest.

Beaujolais, 19 November

Decanter dates the Nouveau tradition to the 1800s, when growers bottled just-fermented wine from grapes harvested, in its words, “just a few months prior, an unusually tight timeframe in winemaking terms”, and bar owners and restaurateurs in Lyon bought it by the barrel.

The date has moved more than once. The Beaujolais AOC was created in 1937 and nothing could be released until after 15 December. By 1951 it had become 15 November. In 1985 the INAO fixed the third Thursday, where it has stayed.

Le Beaujolais Nouveau est arrivé! has been adopted worldwide, Decanter says. As foreign interest grew, winemakers began sending the wine out earlier so the bottles would be sitting on international shelves in time for the third Thursday.

Some people still fetch it themselves. La Fromagerie of Marylebone lists a Beaujolais Nouveau supper for the nineteenth, and Londonist has the crates coming back from France in a Morgan.

Le Beaujolais in Covent Garden calls itself London’s oldest French wine bar, and says that since 1972 the third Thursday has been the most storied date on its calendar. It opens from noon to eleven on the nineteenth with the new vintage, French cheeses and charcuterie, the ground-floor wine bar on its usual walk-in basis. Gordon’s Wine Bar on Villiers Street opens at eight and serves a breakfast sitting until a quarter to eleven in the morning: a full English, coffee, freshly squeezed orange juice and the new vintage, bookings for breakfast only and twenty pounds a head held against the bill. They go on pouring the wine for the rest of the month.
