The Third Thursday

A wine with a start time, the race that carried it to the City, and what beer does with a harvest that will not wait.

This year’s Beaujolais Nouveau goes on sale tomorrow, and the grower does not pick the day. Decanter dates the current rule to 1985, when the INAO, France’s appellation body, settled the release on the third Thursday of November. This year that is the nineteenth.

Beaujolais Nouveau

Lyon’s bars once bought the young wine by the barrel. The growers’ own body dates the first commercial release of Beaujolais Nouveau to 15 November 1951. The date moved around before settling on 15 November in 1967, then shifted to the third Thursday in 1985 to keep it off the weekend.

Britain’s part in this was a race.

It started with a dinner in 1970. Joseph Berkmann and Clement Freud raced each other back to London with the new Beaujolais. Ian Mansfield supplies the detail that sticks: two Rolls-Royces, a case each, and Berkmann home first.

In 1973 the Sunday Times columnist Alan Hall threw the challenge at Fleet Street. Bring back the Beaujolais. For the first few years the target was the City Golf Club.

Sponsors turned up early, Reliant in 1975 and Ford in 1976. In 1980 two barristers, Barnaby Waylen and Oliver Ross, took motorbikes and won in nine hours and seventeen minutes carrying twenty-eight bottles.

By 1985, Mansfield writes, around a thousand vehicles were reported setting off at midnight. The race did not last. There were accidents and deaths on the French roads, and the French tired of the whole business. Mansfield quotes Le Figaro in 1988 calling the rush to put the wine on the table “the greatest marketing stroke since the end of World War II”. By his own measure, the number of newspaper articles it drew, the Run took off in 1986 and dropped off a cliff in 1993. The RAF eventually brought the wine back in a Harrier. The Run survives as a charity navigation rally.

Beaujolais uses the calendar, the third Thursday. Munich uses a hammer: no beer is poured at the Wiesn until the Lord Mayor taps the first barrel at noon, which this year took Dominik Krause two blows and a shout of “O’zapft is!”, as we wrote in September. Copenhagen uses a clock, J-dag at 20:59.

Wet hop harvest

Beer’s nearest thing to this is the wet hop beer, and the comparison dates from the mid-nineties. In Jeff Alworth’s telling, Bert Grant called his wet hop beer the brewing world’s Beaujolais Nouveau. Grant sent his brewery staff into the Yakima fields during the harvest with the mash tun already going, and had the flowers in the boil within minutes of picking.

Beaujolais waits for the third Thursday. Once the hops are picked, the brewer cannot wait. Chris Nemlowill of Fort George Brewery in Astoria, Oregon, gave the clock to Joshua M. Bernstein, writing in Bon Appétit in September 2014: “After the hops are picked, you only have 24 hours before they start turning into compost.” So he shut half the brewery for a week and waited on the farm’s call, then drove a sixteen foot truck four hours east to Yakima, loaded 2,200 pounds of Simcoe, got back to Astoria around midnight, and had his brewers at work by five in the morning. They did not stop for more than a day.

The hops had a deadline. Steve Indrehus wanted the beer to have a date. On the third Thursday of November 2010, Westword reported that the Tommyknocker head brewer wanted breweries to release their wet hop beers on a single day. He had brewed his own Colorado IPA Nouveau that October, with hops picked in Montrose and back at the brewery inside a day. A shared date, he said, would give drinkers “something to look forward to”. At the time, Westword put releases anywhere from August to November.

The labels are not fixed, and Sierra Nevada say so themselves: plenty of brewers use wet and fresh interchangeably. Their own division is a matter of hours. A wet hop is never dried, and for Northern Hemisphere Harvest they give themselves a twenty-four hour window from the hops coming down to the kettle, “in essence a ‘same day’ IPA”, which they date to 1996. A fresh hop is kiln-dried and pressed into two-hundred-pound bales in the ordinary way, and simply used within a few weeks: Celebration has been doing that since 1981, fifteen years before the wet one existed, on Cascade and Centennial raced from farm to brewery. The cost of the wet version is bulk. About four fifths of an undried hop is water, so they use about four times the weight of it to reach the same place, and this year’s was 6.7 per cent on wet Centennial.

The American Homebrewers Association calls it one of the most influential commercial wet hopped beers in America around 1996, and quotes Michael Jackson on it: “an astonishingly late, long finish of fresh, appetite-arousing bitterness”.

Writing in the Source in Bend this September, Brian Yaeger set out why fresh hop beer stays a Pacific Northwest business: “Hop degradation is measured in hours, not days,” and an American brewery more than hours from Yakima or the Willamette would be tipping brown, stale, mouldy cones into the kettle.

New Zealand flies its own. The cones go round the country by air and into tank on arrival, as Hannah Beaumont described for Glengarry in 2018, with the harvest done by March and the beers out at the start of April. There is a name for the day there, too. “The wine world has Nouveau Beaujolais Day and we have Fresh Hop Day,” Luke White of the online retailer Beer Jerk said in April 2020, describing annual events in Auckland and Wellington and a committee that organises one of them.

Kent’s version runs on twelve hours, from picking to the copper. Roger Protz was writing about it in September 2012, with twenty breweries taking their green hop beers to the Canterbury Food and Drink Festival at the end of the month, and he called them “the beery equivalent of Beaujolais Nouveau”. Ed Wray at Old Dairy, on a farm at Rolvenden, gave him the method, throwing “a pokeful into the copper at the end of the boil”, a poke being the sack the hops would have gone into had anybody dried them.

Yorkshire does it smaller. Treboom, at Shipton-by-Beningbrough, grow ten Progress plants up fourteen foot poles, enough for one brew a year, and in 2015 the lot went into their Kettle Drum bitter for thirty casks. The brewery gave it no French name. Deliciously Yorkshire, writing it up, supplied one: houblon nouveau est arrivé.

A note on the sources

The Run’s early history is thinly documented and the accounts differ. Beaujolais Day, the Run’s own site and the RAF Benevolent Fund all date the first race to 1970. Beaujolais Day dates Alan Hall’s public challenge to 1973 and the Run’s site gives the same year; Ian Mansfield uses it too, but notes that some references give 1972 and that the Sunday Times is not in the Times archive. The Harrier is in the Run’s own history, and Beaujolais Day puts it at 2006. We have not checked any of this against reports from the time.
