The Third Thursday

A wine with a start time, and the beer festival it keeps being compared to.

This year’s Beaujolais Nouveau goes on sale today, and the grower does not pick the day. Decanter dates the current rule to 1985, when the INAO, France’s appellation body, settled the release on the third Thursday of November. This year that is the nineteenth.

The shape rhymes with the Wiesn, which is why people keep calling Beaujolais Nouveau the Oktoberfest of the wine world. A date announced in advance, nothing sold before it, and several countries opening the same young thing on the same night. The comparison usually runs as a compliment to the wine, and run the other way it tells you more, because only one of the two has ever been about a harvest.

Munich, 12 October 1810

Oktoberfest began as a wedding. Crown Prince Ludwig married Princess Therese of Saxe-Hildburghausen on 12 October 1810, and by the festival’s own account the official festivities ran five days and ended on the seventeenth with a horse race on a meadow outside the city gates. The Historisches Lexikon Bayerns credits that race to the Munich civic militia, and the militia with the suggestion that the ground be named the Theresienwiese after the bride, which is why Munich calls the whole thing the Wiesn. The race itself was run until 1913.

Major Andreas von Dall’Armi asked the royal household to do it again. King Max I Joseph agreed in 1811, and the agriculture arrived with the repeat: from 1811 to 1818 the Agricultural Association in Bavaria ran the festival and added a cattle market, the show that still exists as the Zentral-Landwirtschaftsfest.

Munich, 1952

The beer is as local as the licence. Nine Munich breweries were licensed to supply the festival after the First World War, the state-owned Hofbräu was added in 1952, and the city has since held supply contracts with Augustiner, Hacker-Pschorr, Löwenbräu, Paulaner and Spaten. Those six are the only breweries allowed to pour, and what they pour now is a pale Festbier.

Beaujolais, 19 November

Decanter dates the Nouveau tradition to the 1800s, when growers bottled just-fermented wine from grapes harvested, in its words, “just a few months prior, an unusually tight timeframe in winemaking terms”, and bar owners and restaurateurs in Lyon bought it by the barrel.

The date has moved three times. The Beaujolais AOC was created in 1937 and nothing could be released until after 15 December. By 1951 that had become 15 November. In 1985 the INAO fixed the third Thursday, where it has stayed.

The slogan travelled further than the rule. Le Beaujolais Nouveau est arrivé! has been adopted worldwide, and Decanter says that as foreign interest grew, winemakers began releasing earlier so the bottles would be on international shelves in time for the official date.
