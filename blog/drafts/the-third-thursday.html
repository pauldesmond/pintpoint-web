The Third Thursday

A wine with a start time, and the race that carried it to the City.

This year’s Beaujolais Nouveau goes on sale tomorrow, and the grower does not pick the day. Decanter dates the current rule to 1985, when the INAO, France’s appellation body, settled the release on the third Thursday of November. This year that is the nineteenth.

Britain’s part in this was a race.

The City Golf Club, just off Fleet Street

It started with a dinner. Beaujolais Day, following the Run’s own account, has the restaurateur and wine distributor Joseph Berkmann and Clement Freud filling their cars with cases after midnight and challenging each other to be first to get them to London. The RAF Benevolent Fund, the Run’s chosen charity since 2017, dates the event to 1970.

Writing on ianVisits, Ian Mansfield supplies the detail that sticks: two Rolls-Royces, a case each, and Berkmann home first. They did it again the next year.

It went public in 1973, when the Sunday Times columnist Alan Hall threw the challenge at Fleet Street. Bring back the Beaujolais.

What the drivers aimed at was in the City. For the first few years, Mansfield writes, the target was the City Golf Club just off Fleet Street, and only later did the Run leave its Fleet Street origins behind and spread across the country. Sponsors turned up early, Reliant in 1975 and Ford in 1976. In 1980 two barristers, Barnaby Waylen and Oliver Ross, took motorbikes and won in nine hours and seventeen minutes carrying twenty-eight bottles.

By 1985, Mansfield writes, around a thousand vehicles of various sorts were reported setting off at midnight. That was about the top of it. There were accidents and deaths on the French roads, and the French tired of the whole business. He quotes Le Figaro in 1988 calling the race to put the wine on the table “the greatest marketing stroke since the end of World War II”. By Mansfield’s own measure, the number of newspaper articles it drew, the Run took off in 1986 and dropped off a cliff in 1993.

The last records were not set on the road at all. The Savoy is said to have got the time down to two hours by having its wine parachuted in by the Red Devils, and the records end in a Harrier jump jet. Beaujolais Day puts the Harrier at 2006 and says the RAF team broke every existing record and finished the racing for good. Mansfield gives the time as thirty-two minutes.

It survives as a charity navigation rally. The RAF Benevolent Fund describes drivers “bringing home the Beaujolais”, taking the shortest route back to the UK from France.

In 1989 the commune of Beaujeu started the Sarmentelles, partly, it says, to honour the English and Danish cars that used to leave loaded with bottles.

Beaujolais, 19 November

Decanter dates the Nouveau tradition to the 1800s, when growers sold the newly fermented wine from grapes harvested, in its words, “just a few months prior, an unusually tight timeframe in winemaking terms”, and bar owners and restaurateurs in Lyon bought it by the barrel.

The date took decades to settle. The growers’ own body dates the Beaujolais AOC to 1937 and the first commercial Beaujolais Nouveau to 15 November 1951. After that the date moved about from year to year, and the commune of Beaujeu has it fixed at 15 November only in 1967, and moved again in 1985 to the third Thursday so that it would not fall at a weekend.

Le Beaujolais Nouveau est arrivé! has been adopted worldwide, Decanter says. As foreign interest grew, winemakers began sending the wine out earlier so the bottles would be sitting on international shelves in time for the third Thursday.

Leadenhall, with a filofax

At the London end the wine had a job to do. Writing for the French Library in Boston in 2021, Elizabeth Beckett remembers wine bars opening across the country and especially in London, and the new craze serving as “the perfect excuse for young professionals to hit the wine bars of Leadenhall Market in the City for a little networking, filofax in hand, over a liquid lunch”.

That market still keeps a drink with an arrival date. The Lamb Tavern sends Young’s horses out with the first barrels of Winter Warmer one morning in late October, and pours it with a full English, which we wrote about in Winter is not Christmas.

Some people still fetch it themselves. La Fromagerie lists a Beaujolais Nouveau supper for the nineteenth, now sold out with a waiting list, and Londonist has the crates coming back from France in a Morgan.

Le Beaujolais on Litchfield Street calls itself London’s oldest French wine bar. It opened in 1972, thirteen years before the third Thursday became the rule, and keeps the date now: noon to eleven on the nineteenth with the new vintage, French cheeses and charcuterie, the ground-floor wine bar on its usual walk-in basis.

Gordon’s Wine Bar on Villiers Street opens at eight that morning and serves until 10.45am: a full English, coffee, freshly squeezed orange juice and the new vintage. Bookings are for breakfast only, and there is a twenty pound deposit a head, taken off the bill on the day. They go on pouring the wine for the rest of the month.

Two pubs, too. In Marylebone, the Royal Oak pours the new vintage all day on the nineteenth, from opening until last orders, free to walk into, with beef bourguignon on all week to go with it.

And in Essex the Run itself is still being told. The Ship on Broomfield Road in Chelmsford puts its own history on its events page: that the day took off in Britain in the 1970s, “where Landlords, Landladies and staff would race to France and back to see who could arrive with the wine back in their pub first (all probably fairly pointless but bragging rights are bragging rights)”. The pub keeps the third Thursday every year, opens at eleven, and runs a French menu with the staff, and a few of the customers, in costume. The founders got the Rolls-Royces. The trade got the bragging rights.

Three ways to mark the first pour

Beaujolais uses the calendar, the third Thursday. Munich uses a hammer: no beer is poured at the Wiesn until the Lord Mayor taps the first barrel at noon, which this year took Dominik Krause two blows and a shout of “O’zapft is!”, as we wrote in September. Copenhagen uses a clock, J-dag at 20:59.

Beer has no start time. It has a deadline

Beer’s nearest thing to this is the wet hop beer, and the comparison is older than most of the beers it gets used about. Jeff Alworth, writing on Beervana in September 2007, credits it to Bert Grant, who in the mid-nineties sent his brewery staff into the Yakima fields during the harvest with the mash tun already going and had the flowers in the boil within minutes of picking. The trade called it a gimmick. Grant, in Alworth’s account, called the results the brewing world’s Beaujolais Nouveau. Roger Protz brought the phrase to Kent in 2012, and by September 2014 Joshua M. Bernstein had it in Bon Appétit as common property: “Consider it the beer-world equivalent of Beaujolais nouveau, a young and fresh fall drink, best enjoyed while the leaves are still turning.” What the two share is perishability. What they do not share is a date.

Somebody did try to give it one. On the third Thursday of November 2010, Jonathan Shikes reported in Westword that Steve Indrehus, head brewer at Tommyknocker in Idaho Springs, wanted a wet hop day: every brewery putting its wet hopped beers out on a single date, set near the Great American Beer Festival so there was time to brew them. “It would be fun to have a day that the industry could get together,” he said, “and fun for consumers to have something to look forward to.” He had made his own Colorado IPA Nouveau that October, from hops picked at Montrose and back at the brewery inside a day, and he knew which coat he was trying on: “It’s a no-brainer for all of us if we coin this Nouveau concept.” In the same piece Shikes gives the obstacle without arguing it. Wet hopped beers, he writes, are released any time between August and November.

Beaujolais waits for the third Thursday. A wet hop beer cannot wait for anything. Chris Nemlowill of Fort George Brewery in Astoria, Oregon, gave Bernstein the clock: “After the hops are picked, you only have 24 hours before they start turning into compost.” So he shut half the brewery for a week and waited on the farm’s call, then drove a sixteen foot truck four hours east to Yakima, loaded 2,200 pounds of Simcoe, got back to Astoria around midnight, and had his brewers at work by five in the morning. They did not stop for more than a day.

The labels are not fixed, and Sierra Nevada say so themselves: plenty of brewers use wet and fresh interchangeably. Their own division is a matter of hours. A wet hop is never dried, and for Northern Hemisphere Harvest they give themselves a twenty-four hour window from the hops coming down to the kettle, “in essence a ‘same day’ IPA”, which they date to 1996. A fresh hop is kiln-dried and pressed into two-hundred-pound bales in the ordinary way, and simply used within a few weeks: Celebration has been doing that since 1981, fifteen years before the wet one existed, on Cascade and Centennial raced from farm to brewery. The cost of the wet version is bulk. Four fifths of an undried hop is water, so they use about four times the weight of it to reach the same place, and this year’s was 6.7 per cent on wet Centennial.

Which is why Britain got a Beaujolais Run and never a hop run. A car can carry wine from Burgundy to Fleet Street overnight and it is still wine in the morning. A wet hop cannot be driven anywhere worth driving to. What came the other way, to this country, came dried and baled, chosen in the field by people who had gone there to choose it. The crossing carried the hop. It did not carry the field.

Every hop-growing country has answered the same clock, and every answer is a map. Oregon and Washington stand next to the field and drive to it. New Zealand is small enough to fly: the cones go round the country by air and into tank on arrival, as Hannah Beaumont described for Glengarry in 2018, with the harvest done by March and the beers out at the start of April. The one ocean anybody crossed was Sierra Nevada’s, who flew New Zealand hops to California for Southern Hemisphere Harvest days after picking, at a price, and last made it in 2021.

Where the transport allows it, a date appears. “The wine world has Nouveau Beaujolais Day and we have Fresh Hop Day,” Luke White of the online retailer Beer Jerk said in April 2020, describing the annual Auckland and Wellington events the lockdown had just cancelled, and the committee that organises one of them.

Britain can neither drive to Yakima nor fly anything anywhere, so it did the third thing and moved the brewery to the hop. Protz was writing about it in September 2012, calling Kent’s green hop beers “the beery equivalent of Beaujolais Nouveau” as twenty breweries took them to the Canterbury Food and Drink Festival at the end of the month. The rule is a map rather than a clock: Kent beer, Kent hops, undried, in the copper within twelve hours of picking. Ed Wray at Old Dairy, on a farm at Rolvenden, gave Protz the method, throwing “a pokeful into the copper at the end of the boil”, a poke being the sack the hops would have gone into had anybody dried them. Yorkshire does it smaller and names it better. Treboom, at Shipton-by-Beningbrough, grow ten Progress plants up fourteen foot poles, enough for one brew a year, and in 2015 the lot went into their Kettle Drum bitter for thirty casks. Houblon nouveau est arrivé, and it arrived from the bottom of the garden.

In the valley they still think none of this travels. “Hop degradation is measured in hours, not days,” the Source in Bend put it this September, and anywhere more than hours from Yakima or the Willamette would be tipping brown, mouldy cones into the kettle. That is right about the hop and wrong about the world, because it assumes hops grow in one place. Kent was growing them centuries before Yakima was planted.

So Britain got the race and not the day. Beaujolais is the only one of these traditions built on the journey. The beer ones are all built on not having one.

A note on the sources

The Run’s early history is thinly documented and the accounts differ. Beaujolais Day, the Run’s own site and the RAF Benevolent Fund all date the first race to 1970. Beaujolais Day dates Alan Hall’s public challenge to 1973 and the Run’s site gives the same year; Ian Mansfield uses it too, but notes that some references give 1972 and that the Sunday Times is not in the Times archive. The dinner that started it is placed in Romanèche by the Run’s own site, which names the Hotel Maritonnes, and by Mansfield, and in Paris by Beaujolais Day. The Savoy’s parachute is Mansfield’s, the Harrier is in the Run’s own history, and the 2006 date is Beaujolais Day’s; we have not checked any of them against reports from the time.
