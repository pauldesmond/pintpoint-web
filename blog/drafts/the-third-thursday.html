The Third Thursday

A wine with a start time, and the race that carried it to the City.

This year’s Beaujolais Nouveau goes on sale tomorrow, and the grower does not pick the day. Decanter dates the current rule to 1985, when the INAO, France’s appellation body, settled the release on the third Thursday of November. This year that is the nineteenth.

Britain’s part in this was a race.

The City Golf Club, just off Fleet Street

It started with a dinner. Beaujolais Day, following the Run’s own account, has the restaurateur and wine distributor Joseph Berkmann and Clement Freud filling their cars with cases after midnight and challenging each other to be first to get them to London. The RAF Benevolent Fund, the Run’s chosen charity since 2017, dates the event to 1970.

Writing on ianVisits, Ian Mansfield supplies the detail that sticks: two Rolls-Royces, a case each, and Berkmann home first. They did it again the next year.

It went public in 1973, when the Sunday Times columnist Alan Hall threw the challenge at Fleet Street. Bring back the Beaujolais.

What the drivers aimed at was in the City. For the first few years, Mansfield writes, the target was the City Golf Club just off Fleet Street, and only later did the Run leave its Fleet Street origins behind and spread across the country. Sponsors turned up early, Reliant in 1975 and Ford in 1976. In 1980 two barristers, Barnaby Waylen and Oliver Ross, took motorbikes and won in nine hours and seventeen minutes carrying twenty-eight bottles.

By 1985, Mansfield writes, around a thousand vehicles of various sorts were reported setting off at midnight. That was about the top of it. There were accidents and deaths on the French roads, and the French tired of the whole business. He quotes Le Figaro in 1988 calling the race to put the wine on the table “the greatest marketing stroke since the end of World War II”. By Mansfield’s own measure, the number of newspaper articles it drew, the Run took off in 1986 and dropped off a cliff in 1993.

The last records were not set on the road at all. The Savoy is said to have got the time down to two hours by having its wine parachuted in by the Red Devils, and the records end in a Harrier jump jet. Beaujolais Day puts the Harrier at 2006 and says the RAF team broke every existing record and finished the racing for good. Mansfield gives the time as thirty-two minutes.

It survives as a charity navigation rally. The RAF Benevolent Fund describes drivers “bringing home the Beaujolais”, taking the shortest route back to the UK from France.

In 1989 the commune of Beaujeu started the Sarmentelles, partly, it says, to honour the English and Danish cars that used to leave loaded with bottles.

Beaujolais, 19 November

Decanter dates the Nouveau tradition to the 1800s, when growers sold the newly fermented wine from grapes harvested, in its words, “just a few months prior, an unusually tight timeframe in winemaking terms”, and bar owners and restaurateurs in Lyon bought it by the barrel.

The date took decades to settle. The growers’ own body dates the Beaujolais AOC to 1937 and the first commercial Beaujolais Nouveau to 15 November 1951. After that the date moved about from year to year, and the commune of Beaujeu has it fixed at 15 November only in 1967, and moved again in 1985 to the third Thursday so that it would not fall at a weekend.

Le Beaujolais Nouveau est arrivé! has been adopted worldwide, Decanter says. As foreign interest grew, winemakers began sending the wine out earlier so the bottles would be sitting on international shelves in time for the third Thursday.

Leadenhall, with a filofax

At the London end the wine had a job to do. Writing for the French Library in Boston in 2021, Elizabeth Beckett remembers wine bars opening across the country and especially in London, and the new craze serving as “the perfect excuse for young professionals to hit the wine bars of Leadenhall Market in the City for a little networking, filofax in hand, over a liquid lunch”.

That market still keeps a drink with an arrival date. The Lamb Tavern sends Young’s horses out with the first barrels of Winter Warmer one morning in late October, and pours it with a full English, which we wrote about in Winter is not Christmas.

Some people still fetch it themselves. La Fromagerie lists a Beaujolais Nouveau supper for the nineteenth, now sold out with a waiting list, and Londonist has the crates coming back from France in a Morgan.

Le Beaujolais on Litchfield Street calls itself London’s oldest French wine bar. It opened in 1972, thirteen years before the third Thursday became the rule, and keeps the date now: noon to eleven on the nineteenth with the new vintage, French cheeses and charcuterie, the ground-floor wine bar on its usual walk-in basis.

Gordon’s Wine Bar on Villiers Street opens at eight that morning and serves until 10.45am: a full English, coffee, freshly squeezed orange juice and the new vintage. Bookings are for breakfast only, and there is a twenty pound deposit a head, taken off the bill on the day. They go on pouring the wine for the rest of the month.

Two pubs, too. In Marylebone, the Royal Oak pours the new vintage all day on the nineteenth, from opening until last orders, free to walk into, with beef bourguignon on all week to go with it.

And in Essex the Run itself is still being told. The Ship on Broomfield Road in Chelmsford puts its own history on its events page: that the day took off in Britain in the 1970s, “where Landlords, Landladies and staff would race to France and back to see who could arrive with the wine back in their pub first (all probably fairly pointless but bragging rights are bragging rights)”. The pub keeps the third Thursday every year, opens at eleven, and runs a French menu with the staff, and a few of the customers, in costume. The founders got the Rolls-Royces. The trade got the bragging rights.

Three ways to mark the first pour

Beaujolais uses the calendar, the third Thursday. Munich uses a hammer: no beer is poured at the Wiesn until the Lord Mayor taps the first barrel at noon, which this year took Dominik Krause two blows and a shout of “O’zapft is!”, as we wrote in September. Copenhagen uses a clock, J-dag at 20:59.

A note on the sources

The Run’s early history is thinly documented and the accounts differ. Beaujolais Day, the Run’s own site and the RAF Benevolent Fund all date the first race to 1970. Beaujolais Day dates Alan Hall’s public challenge to 1973 and the Run’s site gives the same year; Ian Mansfield uses it too, but notes that some references give 1972 and that the Sunday Times is not in the Times archive. The Run’s own history places the dinner at the Hotel Maritonnes on 18 November 1970, followed by a departure from Romanèche after midnight. Mansfield places the dinner in Romanèche; Beaujolais Day puts it in Paris. The Savoy’s parachute is Mansfield’s, the Harrier is in the Run’s own history, and the 2006 date is Beaujolais Day’s; we have not checked any of them against reports from the time.
