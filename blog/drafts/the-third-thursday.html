The Third Thursday

A wine with a start time, and the beer festival it keeps being compared to.

This year’s Beaujolais Nouveau goes on sale tomorrow, and the grower does not pick the day. Decanter dates the current rule to 1985, when the INAO, France’s appellation body, settled the release on the third Thursday of November. This year that is the nineteenth.

The shape rhymes with the Wiesn, which is why people keep calling Beaujolais Nouveau the Oktoberfest of the wine world. A date announced in advance, nothing sold before it, and several countries opening the same young thing on the same night. The comparison usually runs as a compliment to the wine, and run the other way it tells you more, because only one of the two has ever been about a harvest.

Munich, 12 October 1810

Oktoberfest began as a wedding. Crown Prince Ludwig married Princess Therese of Saxe-Hildburghausen on 12 October 1810, and by the festival’s own account the official festivities ran five days and ended on the seventeenth with a horse race on a meadow outside the city gates. The Historisches Lexikon Bayerns credits that race to the Munich civic militia, and the militia with the suggestion that the ground be named the Theresienwiese after the bride, which is why Munich calls the whole thing the Wiesn. The race itself was run until 1913.

Major Andreas von Dall’Armi asked the royal household to do it again. King Max I Joseph agreed in 1811, and the agriculture arrived with the repeat: from 1811 to 1818 the Agricultural Association in Bavaria ran the festival and added a cattle market, the show that still exists as the Zentral-Landwirtschaftsfest.

Beaujolais, 19 November

Decanter dates the Nouveau tradition to the 1800s, when growers bottled just-fermented wine from grapes harvested, in its words, “just a few months prior, an unusually tight timeframe in winemaking terms”, and bar owners and restaurateurs in Lyon bought it by the barrel.

The date has moved more than once. The Beaujolais AOC was created in 1937 and nothing could be released until after 15 December. It later became 15 November. In 1985 the INAO fixed the third Thursday, where it has stayed.

The slogan travelled further than the rule. Le Beaujolais Nouveau est arrivé! has been adopted worldwide, and the date now has a logistics problem behind it. Decanter says that as foreign interest grew, winemakers began sending the wine out earlier so the bottles would be sitting on international shelves in time. The rule fixes the day they may be sold. It does not fix the day they leave the cellar.

Some people still fetch it themselves. La Fromagerie of Marylebone lists a Beaujolais Nouveau supper for the nineteenth, and Londonist has the crates coming back from France in a Morgan.

London has kept the date longer than most of the bars that now mark it. Le Beaujolais in Covent Garden has run it since 1972, calls the third Thursday the most storied date on its calendar, and opens from noon to eleven on the nineteenth with the new vintage, French cheeses and charcuterie, walk-in only. Gordon’s Wine Bar on Villiers Street opens at eight that morning and serves until a quarter to eleven: a full English breakfast, coffee, orange juice and the new vintage.

The two dates do different work. The third Thursday says when one year’s wine may be sold, and it exists because the wine is new. Munich’s fortnight says when a city holds its fair, and the beer is poured because the fair is on.
