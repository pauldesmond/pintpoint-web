The Third Thursday

A wine with a start time, and the beer festival it keeps being compared to.

At one minute past midnight this morning, by French rule, this year’s Beaujolais Nouveau went on sale. The grower does not pick the date. Decanter dates the uniform release to a 1985 ruling by the INAO, France’s appellation body, which put every bottle in the appellation on the third Thursday of November, this year the nineteenth.

The shape rhymes with the Wiesn, which is why people keep calling Beaujolais Nouveau the Oktoberfest of the wine world. A date announced in advance, nothing sold before it, and several countries opening the same young thing on the same night. The comparison usually runs as a compliment to the wine. Run the other way it is more interesting, because the harvest festival in the pairing is the one with the grapes in it.

Munich, 12 October 1810

Oktoberfest began as a wedding. Crown Prince Ludwig married Princess Therese of Saxe-Hildburghausen on 12 October 1810, and by the festival’s own account the official festivities ran five days and ended on the seventeenth with a horse race on a meadow outside the city gates. The Historisches Lexikon Bayerns credits that race to the Munich civic militia, and the militia with the suggestion that the ground be named the Theresienwiese after the bride, which is why Munich calls the whole thing the Wiesn.

Major Andreas von Dall’Armi asked the royal household to do it again. King Max I Joseph agreed in 1811, and the agriculture arrived with the repeat rather than the original: from 1811 to 1818 the Agricultural Association in Bavaria ran the festival and added a cattle market, the show that still exists as the Zentral-Landwirtschaftsfest.

The oldest beer in the cellar

The beer was not this year’s either. A Märzen was brewed in spring and held cold through the summer, which before refrigeration was the only way to have beer to serve in autumn. What the tents poured in October had been waiting since March, and that was the point of it.

They have stopped pouring it. The six Munich houses now serve a paler Festbier, and accounts of when that happened differ by decades, so the amber beer that gave the style its name is the part of this story that is genuinely historical.

Beaujolais, 19 November

Beaujolais Nouveau makes the opposite claim, and it is the whole product. The gamay in the bottle was picked in September and is drunk before Christmas, which is why the appellation needs a rule to stop anyone selling it early rather than a rule to make them keep it.

The merchant Georges Duboeuf is credited with the campaign that put the line on it in the 1970s: Le Beaujolais Nouveau est arrivé! Before the uniform date, the release had moved around for decades, and the fixed Thursday exists because 15 November kept falling on a weekend.

Release is one minute past midnight. Nothing in the appellation moves before it.
