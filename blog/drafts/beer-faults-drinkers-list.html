The list nobody writes for the drinker

Fifteen things that can be wrong with a pint, where each one comes from, when it is supposed to be there, and which of them you can do anything about. Most of them, you cannot.

Every list of beer faults is written for somebody, and the somebody is almost never you.

The Brewers Association’s list is a production problem. Thomas Barnes’s is a taster’s reference. The Draught Beer Quality Manual is a maintenance schedule, and the shortest of them is written for licensees by a firm that sells line cleaning. We have written about the four of them and about what separates them, which is less the contents than the reader each one had in mind. None of them is written for the person holding the glass, who cannot send the beer back to the fermenting vessel and cannot clean the line himself.

So here it is, in the only order that helps at the bar: the everyday word first, the chemistry second, because nobody has ever thought isovaleric acid before thinking old socks.

Each entry answers four questions. What it is like. Where it comes from. When it belongs, because a surprising number of these are deliberate somewhere. And what you can actually do, which is the question the other lists never ask.

Thresholds come from Thomas Barnes’s Complete Beer Fault Guide, version 1.4, and are there to show how little of something it takes to be noticed rather than to be measured against anything. Where Barnes gives a figure that does not square with the rest of his own guide, we have left the number out and said so.

The fifteen

Antiseptic, or TCP

A hospital note, sharp and chemical, sometimes with a numbing edge. Barnes files it under chlorophenols and puts the threshold at three to forty micrograms per litre in beer, which is why a trace of it ruins a whole glass. The Norfolk line cleaning firm Avani Solutions, who sell the service and should be read with that in mind, describe the mechanism for a British cellar: chlorine from cleaning products meeting phenols already in the beer, helped along by chlorinated tap water and hose that is not food grade.

When it belongs. Never. Barnes gives the answer in one word and then repeats it: off-flavours from chlorophenols are always a fault in beer.

What you can do. Tell the bar, and say which beer. This one is cleaning chemistry, it will be on every pint off that line, and it is fixable by somebody who works there.

Butter

Butter, buttered popcorn, butterscotch, sometimes a slick feel rather than a taste. This is diacetyl, and it is the most useful fault in beer because it arrives by two unrelated routes: a brewery where the yeast came off the beer early, or a draught line where bacteria make the same compound from scratch. Barnes puts the usual threshold at twenty to forty micrograms per litre and notes that the ability to taste it at all is genetic. Some people find it at ten. Others cannot find it anywhere.

When it belongs. Often. Barnes lists low levels as acceptable in Bohemian pilsner, English pale ales, Scottish ales, English brown ales, brown porters, robust porters, sweet stouts, oatmeal stouts, foreign extra stouts and English IPA.

What you can do. One buttery pint tells you nothing. Butter in two or three unrelated beers in the same pub is a line problem, and worth mentioning.

Cardboard, or wet paper

Stale, papery, the smell of a shoe box or a damp envelope. At low levels people reach for odd words instead: inky, library, old books. It is oxidation, and the compound usually named for it is trans-2-nonenal, which Barnes puts among the lowest thresholds in his guide at fifty to a hundred nanograms per litre. It shows up first in pale, light, weaker beers, where there is nothing to hide behind.

When it belongs. Never, as cardboard. Oxidation taken further can give sherry and leather notes that Barnes accepts in weizenbock, Flanders brown, old ale and English barleywine, but that is a different stage and usually a different beer.

What you can do. Check how long it has been open or on. A cask takes in air the moment it is broached, so it has days rather than weeks. A keg under gas does not, so a long time on sale is not the same kind of risk, although no beer improves by waiting.

Cheese, or old socks

Blue cheese, stale laundry, feet. Isovaleric acid, and it comes from hops that have oxidised in storage rather than from anything that happened in the pub. Barnes puts the threshold at 0.7 to 1 milligram per litre and adds that although everybody can taste it, sensitivity varies by orders of magnitude.

When it belongs. Never. Barnes allows the exception and then closes it: lambic brewers do use aged hops, but aged long enough that the cheese is gone.

What you can do. Nothing, except stop drinking it. This one was settled before the beer was brewed.

Clove, smoke and spice

Clove, pepper, sometimes bacon or band plaster depending on which phenol it is and how much. Phenols come from yeast, from certain malts, and from contamination, and the three are not distinguishable in the glass.

When it belongs. Very often, which is why it is on this list. Barnes expects low to medium clove-like phenols in German wheat and rye beers, in French and Belgian ales and in Belgian strong ales. A hefeweizen without it is the one with the problem.

What you can do. Check the style before complaining. Clove in a wheat beer is the yeast doing its job.

Damp cellar

Mushrooms, mildew, old books, the smell of a basement. Musty character, and in beer it is usually traced to fungi in lines left standing or in insulation that has failed. Barnes puts the threshold under ten to twenty-five nanograms per litre, lower than almost anything else he lists.

When it belongs. Never. Barnes notes that judges allow a faintly corked note in some bière de garde, then says plainly that it should come from the yeast and not from bad storage.

What you can do. Tell the bar. Like the antiseptic note, it is a cellar matter and it will not be confined to your glass.

Green apple

Green apple, bruised apple, sometimes emulsion paint. Acetaldehyde, which every fermentation makes on the way to alcohol and healthy yeast then takes back up. Finding it means the beer left before the yeast had finished. Barnes puts the threshold at five to twenty milligrams per litre.

When it belongs. Almost nowhere. Barnes allows very low levels in American light and standard lagers and treats it as a fault everywhere else.

What you can do. Nothing useful. It happened weeks ago and hundreds of miles away.

Metallic

Coins, blood, rust, a tinny edge that lingers. Barnes gives a threshold of one to 1.5 milligrams per litre and files the cause as contamination.

When it belongs. Never, in his guide, in one word.

What you can do. Mention it, although it is one of the harder ones for a pub to chase. Check it is not the glass, or the water you drank before it.

Nail varnish, or pear drops

The same chemistry read twice. Low down it is pear drops and fruit, high up it is solvent and nail varnish remover, and the line between them is degree rather than kind. Esters at sensible levels are a large part of what makes ale taste of anything.

When it belongs. As fruit, constantly, and Barnes expects esters across English, Belgian and German ales. As solvent, never. He allows that a trace can appear in a well made strong ale and says it should have been conditioned away.

What you can do. Nothing in the pub. It is worth knowing only so that pear drops in a Belgian ale does not get reported as a fault.

Onion and garlic

Exactly what it says, and unmistakable once noticed. The Brewers Association files it as an off-flavour and then notes it can come off the hops and is not always unwelcome, which is a fair summary of the whole subject.

When it belongs. Not at a level you can name. Barnes says detectable onion or garlic is never appropriate, although at very low levels the same chemistry feeds the sulphury note that some pale lagers are supposed to have.

What you can do. Nothing. If it is strong enough to argue about, it is strong enough to leave.

Rotten eggs

Eggs, drains. Hydrogen sulphide, from yeast or from infection, and the one fault on this list that sometimes walks away while you watch. Young beer can throw it off in the glass.

When it belongs. At the very bottom of the scale. Barnes accepts very low sulphury notes from yeast in some light lagers and sub-threshold levels in pale hoppy English ales, and calls anything noticeable a defect.

What you can do. Wait a minute. If it is gone, the beer was young. If it is still there, say so.

Skunk

The one fault in beer that is nothing to do with hygiene, brewing or age. It is light. Light below 520 nanometres breaks down hop compounds and liberates a mercaptan close to the one a skunk uses, and Barnes gives the threshold as four nanograms per litre, which is about as little of anything as a person can detect. He also puts the timescale at thirty seconds to two minutes.

When it belongs. Never, although Barnes adds the sting: it is so common in green-bottled imported lager that drinkers have learned to expect it and some believe it is the recipe.

What you can do. This is the one. Barnes measures the packaging: amber glass lets through about five per cent of the ultraviolet, green glass about eighty, and clear glass about ninety per cent of everything. A can lets through none. Writing for Hop Culture in 2020, Jess Keller Poole made the same point for drinkers. It is the only fact in this entire subject that is any use while standing in a shop.

Sweetcorn

Cooked sweetcorn, canned vegetables, sometimes tomato juice. Dimethyl sulphide, which comes out of the malt and is driven off by a vigorous boil. Barnes puts the threshold at twenty-five to fifty micrograms per litre in beer.

When it belongs. More often than people think. Barnes accepts low levels in light lagers, German pilsner, classic American pilsner, dark American lager, maibock and cream ale.

What you can do. Nothing, and in a pilsner you may be tasting the style rather than a mistake.

Tart, or yoghurt

Clean sourness, lemon, sour cream. Lactic acid, made by bacteria, and the clearest case in beer of a fault and a feature being the same molecule. Barnes gives a threshold figure for lactic that does not agree with the one in his own entry for sourness, so we have left the number out.

When it belongs. Constantly. Barnes accepts very low lactic sourness in dry stout, expects medium to high levels in Berliner weisse, and expects both lactic and a little acetic in Belgian sour ales.

What you can do. Ask what it is before deciding it is wrong. Sourness in a bitter is spoilage. The same sourness in a Flanders red is the reason it costs more.

Vinegar

Sharp, cidery, the back of the throat. Acetic acid, from bacteria and usually from air. Barnes puts the threshold at 130 milligrams per litre, which is high compared with most of this list, so by the time you can taste it there is plenty of it.

When it belongs. In a narrow but real set. Barnes expects low levels of acetic sourness in Belgian sour ales, naming Flanders red, Flanders brown and lambic.

What you can do. In anything else, send it back. Vinegar is one of the few on this list that nobody will argue with you about.

What the list is actually a list of

Read the fifteen together and the pattern is not chemical. Five of them belong somewhere. Three of them belong in beers that people pay a premium for. One of them, the skunk, is the only one a drinker can prevent, and the prevention is choosing a can over a green bottle in a lit fridge.

Barnes ends his entries with the same question, when are these notes appropriate? He asks it sixty-two times. Twenty-one of those answers are the single word Never. The other forty-one are conditional, and most of them name styles. That ratio is the subject. A catalogue of beer faults is really a catalogue of context, and context is the part a chart cannot hold.

It is also why the lists do not match each other. The Brewers Association keeps sixteen off-flavours with a fact sheet apiece. Three of the fifteen here are not on it at all, the antiseptic note, the cardboard and the damp cellar, and a fourth, the skunk, appears only under the broader heading of mercaptans, where the descriptor given is sewage rather than anything a drinker would recognise as skunk.

PINtPOINT records when a pub’s tap list was last confirmed. That is a dated sighting and not a measure of how the beer has been kept. Nobody publishes when a line was last cleaned.
