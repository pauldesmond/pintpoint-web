Davy's — 155 years of sawdust and candles

A family wine merchant that started off the Strand in 1870, invented the modern British wine bar in a Southwark cellar in 1964, and still trades nine sawdust-floored subterranean rooms across London. Two of the most-loved didn't make it out of the last decade.

In 2012, hanging under the BANGERS TOO pub sign at 1 St Mary at Hill, was a smaller sign in gold letters on black: Davys · Fine Wines and Eating. A year later the lease ran out, the pub closed, and 1 St Mary at Hill became a Bengali restaurant called Jamdani. The Davy's marque above the door came down with it.

That's the story of a lot of Davy's in the last decade. The chain has been quietly editing itself down — closing sites, letting leases run out, folding branches into offices and restaurants. But it isn't gone. Nine wine bars are still trading, some of them in the same cellars they've been in for forty years, most of them still following the house formula: stone floors, candle stubs, port bottles racked to head height, and — if you know to ask — Old Wallop pulled through a hand pump. This is where that formula came from and what's left of it.

1870 — Rising Sun, off the Strand

Francis Edwin Davy opened The Rising Sun just off the Strand in 1870, calling it a "wine house." The site was chosen for its proximity to the new Charing Cross railway station and the trade the trains would bring — commuting City workers, day-trippers into town from Kent. In its first decades the business was more merchant than bar: FE Davy became a major shipper of Port, particularly for the London club trade, and in 1902 introduced the first of what the family still call their "own-label" wines. Those own labels remain, per the company's own account, "the cornerstone of our range" today.

Davy's is now on its fifth generation. The second (JHH Davy) took the business through the First World War and the General Strike of 1926. The third (SJE Davy) ran it through the Second — during which, per the company history, "customers had to bring their own glasses," and the original Barking cellars were lost in the Blitz. There is a photograph on the Davy's site of a lady scrubbing the front step of The Alfred's Head, 140 Newington Causeway, while men repair bomb damage behind her. The family kept trading.

1964 — the Boot & Flogger, and the invention of the wine bar

The fourth-generation head of the firm, John Davy, opened The Boot & Flogger on Redcross Way near London Bridge in 1964. The company describes it as "the birth of what would become the UK wine bar phenomenon," and that's not entirely marketing. Davy needed a licence and the brewery-influenced licensing magistrates weren't going to give him one — one magistrate reportedly told the family the bar wasn't needed because "no wine bars existed at the time." Davy sidestepped the whole system by claiming the Vintners' Privilege, an ancient City freeman right to sell wine without a magistrate's licence at all. The Boot & Flogger opened, poured, and the phrase "wine bar" entered the general London vocabulary.

By the 1970s the wine-shop side of the business had been sold off to fund the bar rollout. The chain hit its stride in 1980, opening a site in newly-refurbished Covent Garden with, per the family history, "customers queued out of the door." Late-Thatcher London was a wine-bar city for about a decade — Davy's was the version of that scene that had actually been running the same room for a hundred years, not the version that had opened in a converted warehouse the year before.

The Davy's formula

Every Davy's writes the same short list on the tin: stone-flagged floors, dark wood fittings, bottle racks and candlelight — that's the CAMRA description of Davy's at St James's, and it's transferable. Sawdust used to be more literal than it is now; the phrase "sort of posh spit-and-sawdust," attributed to James Davy (fifth generation, currently at the helm) by Jancis Robinson, does the job of two adjectives.

Their house ale is called Old Wallop. It used to be cask; since the pandemic it's mostly been on keg, though a Londonist visit to Truckles of Pied Bull Yard in 2009 caught the tail-end of the older regime — "imbibe Old Wallop from pewter tankards" — and Truckles' handpump routine (Spitfire upstairs, Old Wallop down) was still holding then. The pewter is real, not stagecraft; the pots are the tankards Davy's use, and the beer is cheaper by the four-pint jug than by the pint — a survival of vintners' economics from an era when the price of the vessel was part of the pour.

The bar staff, at St James's and one or two of the others, still work in white shirts and ties. The room fills with what CAMRA politely calls a "largely besuited clientele." The visual signature — sawdust and candles, wood and bottle racks, port glasses on marble — has been so stable for so long that walking into one Davy's for the first time feels like walking back into a Davy's you've been to before, even if you haven't.

Bangers and Bangers Too — the two the decade ate

The Davy's brand ran two adjacent City sites for years, each named — with a house-style groan — "Bangers." Bangers Too at 1 St Mary at Hill was the Fenchurch Street one, running for the better part of two decades until its lease ran out in 2012; 1 St Mary at Hill became Jamdani the following year, and the shot above is from what was effectively the pub's farewell December. Bangers proper — Eldon House, 2–12 Wilson Street, EC2M — held on longer. It closed for the pandemic on 23 March 2020 and never reopened; the site was granted planning consent for office conversion and the pub was permanently closed in August 2021. A Tripadvisor review posted around then, mourning the closure, called it "one of the last 'real' Davys wine bars and restaurants in the City." That was the sound the brand made losing two of its bigger addresses in a decade.

Both are ghosts in PINtPOINT now, with the archive photography and a "then / now" toggle so you can see them as they were and see what stands there today.

The nine that survive

Davy's claim "over 19 locations" in current copy — a figure that includes their El Vino wine & tapas bars (acquired 2015) and one or two provincial sites. The pure-Davy's Davy's — the ones flying the family name over the door in central London — currently number nine:

  • Davy's at St James'sCrown Passage, SW1 · flagged floors, labyrinthine basement, formal dining above
  • The Boot & FloggerRedcross Way, SE1 · the 1964 original, still trading
  • Truckles of Pied Bull YardBury Place, WC1 · one of central London's few beer gardens
  • Champagne CharliesVilliers Street, WC2 · under the arches
  • Mug House1–3 Tooley Street, SE1 · near London Bridge
  • GrapeshotsArtillery Passage, E1 · off Bishopsgate
  • Davy's Wine Vaults161 Greenwich High Rd, SE10
  • Davy's at Plantation PlaceFenchurch Street, EC3 · the eastern City survivor
  • Davys Wine Bar201 Wood Lane, W12 · Hammersmith

Each of these carries a chain:davys tag in PINtPOINT, so filtering the Davy's cluster is one query — past, present, all together. The two ghosts (Bangers, Bangers Too) travel with them.

Davy's vs Firkin — two ways to run a chain

The Davy's story sits alongside the Firkin one as two very different pillars of London chain drinking. Both used sawdust on the floor; they used it for opposite reasons.

Davy's (from 1870) is a traditional wine and ale house — candle-lit tables, private alcoves, house Port, Old Wallop in pewter tankards. Sophisticated, timeless, atmospheric. A family business that's still a family business.

Firkin (1979–2001) was the brewpub revolution — unpretentious, loud, boisterous neighbourhood locals; puns for names (Frog & Firkin, Pheasant & Firkin); Dogbolter brewed downstairs. David Bruce built it from one South London pub to 44 in fifteen years; the chain died when the brewing did.

Two iconic, contrasting pillars of London drinking culture — one still holding its rooms; the other now remembered as much for what it lost as for what it built.

Davy's has never been in a hurry, which is part of why it's still here. A chain that opens two sites in a decade and closes two more in the next isn't chasing scale. It's tending a room that was old when the founder's grandson took it over, and passing it on. The stubs of candle in the wax rings on the shelf above the bar are the point, not the marketing. When a Davy's does close — as Bangers Too did in 2012, and Bangers in 2021 — the room usually goes with it: the sawdust vacuumed up, the tankards packed off to the next site, the sign taken down. The 2012 shot at St Mary at Hill caught the sign coming down, though nobody knew it yet.

The nine still trading are, between them, most of the argument for why the chain outlasted the wine-bar boom. Not because they refused to change, but because the change was inside the room, not visible from the door.
