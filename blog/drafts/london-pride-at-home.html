London Pride @home

Pinter and Fuller’s have put Pride in a pouch you brew on the kitchen counter. The label names Spark, the yeast Pinter introduced for its lager, and we have a few questions. Friendly ones.

At the end of October, Pinter launched London Pride Home Edition, a kit that turns a pouch of concentrate into ten pints of Pride in your own fridge. Pinter launched it as a collaboration with Fuller’s. We read the label, and the label raised the question anyone who knows Chiswick will ask first. Which yeast?

Ten pints and a dial

A Pinter is a pressurised keg you ferment in. You add water and the concentrate, pitch a sachet of yeast, leave it a week, then move it to the fridge to condition and pour it from the tap on the front. Pinter’s page for the Pride pack recommends seven days brewing and five conditioning, with three as the minimum for the second, and sets the carbonation dial to 5. On 5 November 2026 the pack was £24.99 and in stock.

Pinter’s launch post on 29 October talks about working “alongside a British brewing institution like Fuller’s” and about getting “that signature balance right”. The tasting note is “Malty. Amber. British.”, with “a rich, sweet backbone of toasted malt” and “a subtle whisper of fresh, piney herbs from the hops”, and it signs off “Clean, complex, and supremely well-rounded.”

The yeast question

Fuller’s has a famous yeast, and we have written about it at length, so we will keep this short. When we covered the strain Firestone Walker built Double Barrel Ale on, John Keeling wrote to us to say Fuller’s never had a separate ESB yeast. “All pitching yeast came from London Pride fermentations because it was the best and easiest yeast to use.” The house yeast is the Pride yeast. Every Fuller’s drinker knows what it brings to the glass, even the ones who could not tell you it was the yeast doing it.

The Home Edition’s ingredient list ends with “Yeast: Spark”. Spark is the yeast Pinter supplies. Pinter introduced it for Sunlit, its lager, in a post headed “the lowdown on our latest lager yeast”, as “a strain designed especially for lagers that produces very clean and crisp flavour characteristics over a wide range of fermentation temperatures”. The same post says it attenuates further than Balance, Pinter’s lager and ale hybrid strain, and copes “from cooler conditions to warmer ale fermentation temperatures”.

Pinter’s yeast line-up describes Spark more simply, as “a strain that produces very clean and crisp flavour characteristics over a wide range of fermentation temperatures”, with no mention of lager. The same list runs to five strains, and the first is an English ale yeast, Loyalty, “a mild english ale strain designed to accentuate malt or hop forward beers”. The Home Edition lists Spark rather than Loyalty.

Homebrewers would have gone the other way. Every Pride clone we found that names a yeast reaches for a London ESB ale strain. Brew Your Own offers Wyeast 1968, White Labs WLP002 or Lallemand London ESB, fermented at 20°C. The community recipe on The Malt Miller comes with a pack of Wyeast 1968. Homebrewers treat 1968 as the Fuller’s strain. Nobody has shown that it is, but the belief runs deep. Dark Rock, selling a Tribute to London Pride kit, admits the limits plainly: every commercial brewery has “its own water profile, brewing equipment, fermentation techniques and house yeast”. Which is why the Spark on the label caught our eye.

There is an obvious answer, and it is a fair one. The kit is malt extract and glucose-fructose syrup, not a mash, and fermenting that out cleanly is a different job from brewing Pride at Chiswick. A clean strain with high attenuation is a defensible choice for it. Loyalty, though, is the one Pinter describes as “designed to accentuate malt or hop forward beers”, and its own tasting note for Pride starts with “Malty”. So we would love to hear the thinking. What made Spark the right yeast for Pride?

Four hops, or none named

Two smaller questions sit behind the yeast. Fuller’s own account of the recipe names four hops for Pride: Northdown, Challenger, Target and Goldings. The homebrew clones use the same hops, give or take the Goldings. The Home Edition lists “hop extracts” and names no variety. Extracts are a perfectly respectable way to get bitterness and aroma into a concentrate. We would just like to know whether these come from the four Pride hops.

The other is the body. The tasting note promises “a rich, sweet backbone”, and we would like to know how Pinter matched Pride’s body and fermentation character from extract and syrup.

Then there is the strength. Pinter gives the Home Edition as “4.7% approx”. Cask Pride is 4.1%. Asahi’s 2021 notes to editors give Pride as “4.1% ABV (cask & keg), 4.7% (bottled)”. So which Pride was the reference? An approximate match with the bottle is a clue, and only a clue.

Pride without the fish

One small word on Pinter’s page caught our eye. It says Vegan.

Cask Pride is not. Barnivore keeps a separate record for Fuller’s Cask Beers, marked Not Vegan, and in May 2026 Fuller’s told it the cask “uses isinglass making the beer not suitable for vegans”. Back in 2013, in a reply filed under London Pride, it explained why: “All of our cask beer use isinglass finings to speed up the process of dropping bright in the cellar.” Isinglass is collagen from the swim bladders of fish. Cask beer leaves the brewery with live yeast still in it, finishes conditioning in the pub cellar, and has to drop bright on the stillage before anyone pulls a pint. The finings carry a positive charge, the yeast a negative one, and the yeast clumps and falls to the bottom of the cask. A good cellar does the rest.

Bottled Pride gets bright another way. In the same May 2026 email, Fuller’s said the bottles are cleared with a Stabifix product rather than isinglass, which is why the bottle is vegan and the cask is not.

So a vegan beer can still be clarified, as the bottle shows. At home, Pinter’s instructions say the longer conditioning in the fridge “will increase clarity”, and cold and patience let the yeast settle. What we cannot tell from the page is what happens before the pouch is filled. Has the concentrate been clarified, and are finings or any other clarification aids used in making it? The answer decides how close the next comparison is.

Pride on a centrifuge

Fuller’s has offered a different Pride before. In 2017 it launched London Pride Unfiltered, a keg Pride poured at 4 to 6°C, colder than the cask. We have told the whole story separately, so here is only how it was made. Keeling explained it in a video filmed that September. It was brewed like cask Pride, with the house yeast and a little extra hop in maturation, and parted company only at the end. Cask Pride went into the cask with finings. Unfiltered had none, and a centrifuge did that work instead. Every extra step, he said, strips something out. “It will leave the beer hazy but with more flavour.”

If the Home Edition concentrate is made without finings too, Unfiltered is the nearest precedent we know of. If it is clarified another way, the two sit further apart.

Kitchen counter, Chiswick

So Pride has gone without finings once before, on a centrifuge in Chiswick with the house yeast and the four hops. Now there is a Pride brewed on a kitchen counter and finished in a fridge, with Spark on the label and the brewery’s blessing.

Plenty of people would like a Pride poured fresh from their own fridge, and the cask will still be at the pub. We would love to hear how Pinter and Fuller’s got from Chiswick to the pouch, in their own words.
