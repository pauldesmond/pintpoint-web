Eight hundred years of snow

A gin brand ships its spirit to Iceland for water that fell as snow eight hundred years ago. The story is true. It is not told the way it happened, and brewing settled the same argument a century and a half earlier with a bag of salts.

In the middle of the nineteenth century, every brewer in Britain wanted the water that came out of the ground at Burton upon Trent.

Beer brewed in it was better than the same beer brewed anywhere else, and nobody could argue with the glass. Burton water is hard and loaded with sulphates, calcium sulphate above all, which sharpens bitterness, dries the finish and pushes the hops to the front. It made pale ale what pale ale is, and we have written about what it did to Bass.

Here is what the brewing trade did about it. It did not move to Burton. It did not ship beer to Burton. It worked out what was in the water, put the same things into the water it already had, and got on with the afternoon. The process is called Burtonisation, which is the least romantic word in the history of drink, and it worked completely.

Gin went the other way.

The quest that was already a supply chain

Martin Miller’s Gin tells its founding story on its own site, and it is a good one. A bar in Notting Hill, the spring of 1998, three friends served three bad gin and tonics. Miller challenges his two friends to do something about the state of gin. He works out that splitting the distillation, earthy botanicals in one run and bitter orange and lemon peel in another, then marrying the two, gives a better balance than doing everything at once. He is right, and it does.

Then comes the water. Gin is mostly water, so Miller goes looking for the best there is. The quest takes him to Borgarnes, a village on the remote west coast of Iceland, seventy-five kilometres north of Reykjavik, where the spring water began as snow that fell more than eight hundred years ago, froze into dormant volcanic calderas, was melted from beneath by the volcano, and was stripped of its minerals on the way out through the rock. The spirit is distilled in England, shipped to Iceland, cut with that water, and shipped back.

Miller’s page tells this as a search. It calls it his quest for perfection. It does not name either of the two friends.

The obituary the Eccentric Club wrote for him names them, David Bromige and Andreas Versteegh, and explains Iceland rather differently. Miller turned to Bromige, who was already making vodka in Iceland, and asked why he did not make a gin. The vodka was Pulsar. William Grant bought it some years later and renamed it Reyka.

So the water was not found at the end of a search. One of the three men in the pub already had a distillery there.

None of which makes the gin worse or the water story false. The two-part distillation is a real piece of craft. Miller was straight about how odd the rest of it looked: shipping gin to Iceland at the height of the vodka boom, he said, was a crazy thing to be doing. He died on Christmas Eve in 2013, a year after being diagnosed with oesophageal cancer, and by 2012 the obituary has the brand turning over some fourteen million pounds a year and getting the credit for starting the gin revival.

But a brand page that has a man scouring the earth for the purest water, and leaves out that his business partner was already distilling in the place he found, is not describing a discovery. It is describing a supply chain.

What the water actually does

The technical case for Iceland is weaker than it looks, and it is worth setting out properly rather than sneering at it.

Spirit comes off the still far stronger than anyone drinks it, so every distiller has to cut it with water to bottling strength, and what is dissolved in that water ends up in the bottle. Minerals can alter flavour and mouthfeel, and they can drop out of the spirit over time and leave a fine white deposit at the bottom. So distillers want water with as little in it as possible.

They do not generally go looking for it. They make it. Cutting with demineralised water, usually produced by reverse osmosis, is ordinary practice across the industry, for exactly the reasons above. Taking the minerals out of ordinary water is a solved problem with a machine attached to it.

Which means the argument that you have to go to Iceland to find water this clean does not hold in the way the label implies. You can reach the same place from a tap in Hammersmith.

Two ways to solve a water problem

Burton, 1870s. Analyse the famous water, find the sulphates, add gypsum to your own. Cost: a sack of mineral salts. The word for it, Burtonisation, is still in use and still sounds like a municipal procedure.

Iceland, 1999. Distil in England, tanker the spirit two thousand kilometres north, cut it with meltwater at Borgarnes, tanker it back. Cost: the shipping. The word for it is the brand.

Why a beer drinker should care

Because the same move is coming for beer, and in places it has arrived.

Gin got there first because gin had further to go. It spent most of the twentieth century as a colourless industrial product with almost no story attached, and when the revival came there was very little in the cupboard to be authentic about. So the heritage had to be assembled out of whatever was to hand, and a volcano is a great deal more use than an admission that your mate had a still.

Beer never had that problem, because beer never lost its paperwork. The brewing records survived. The pub names survived. When we wanted to know who sold hot beer off the boats in the Pool of London in 1851, Mayhew had counted them: thirty-five on the river, twenty-three of them in the Pool.

That is the real advantage, and it is worth noticing when it is being spent. A brewery that tells you its recipe came out of a Victorian brewing book can usually show you the book. When it cannot, there is a question worth asking, and it undoes the Iceland story in one move.

Where did you get it from, and can I see it?
