Eight hundred years of snow

A gin brand ships its spirit to Iceland for water that fell as snow eight hundred years ago. The story is true. It is not told the way it happened.

In the middle of the nineteenth century a great many brewers wanted the water that came out of the ground at Burton upon Trent.

Beer brewed in it was better than the same beer brewed elsewhere, and nobody could argue with the glass. Burton water is hard and loaded with sulphates, calcium sulphate above all, which sharpens bitterness, dries the finish and pushes the hops forward. It made pale ale what pale ale is, and we have written about what it did to Bass.

Some of the London houses did go to Burton, and built branch breweries there from the 1850s. But the solution that outlasted all of them was chemical rather than geographical. Work out what is in the water, put the same things into the water you already have, and carry on. The trade has called it Burtonisation ever since, which is the least romantic word in drink, and it worked.

The quest that wasn’t

Martin Miller’s Gin tells its founding story on its own site. A bar in Notting Hill, the spring of 1998, three friends served three bad gin and tonics. Miller challenges his two friends to do something about the state of gin. He splits the distillation, earthy botanicals in one run and bitter orange and lemon peel in another, and marries the two afterwards. He is right, and it works.

Then the water. Gin is mostly water, so Miller goes looking for the best there is, and the page describes a quest for perfection that leads him to Borgarnes, a village on the west coast of Iceland. According to the brand, the spring water there began as snow that fell more than eight hundred years ago, froze into dormant volcanic calderas, was melted from beneath by the volcano and stripped of its minerals on the way out through the rock. The spirit is distilled in England, taken to Iceland, cut with that water and brought back.

The page does not name either of the other two men.

One of them is David Bromige, and he has given a rather different account of how Iceland was chosen.

It would be nice to say we searched the world for the perfect water source, but it was pure luck that we used Icelandic water and it was perfect for the taste we wanted.David Bromige, co-founder, to Travel Distilled

Pure luck. From the man who was there, about the detail the brand has built its identity on.

The Eccentric Club’s obituary of Miller fills in why the luck was available. Miller turned to Bromige, who was already making vodka in Iceland, and asked why he did not make a gin. Separately, and not from the obituary, that vodka business is documented: William Grant & Sons bought Pölstar from the Reformed Spirits Company in 2002 and renamed it Reyka.

So the Icelandic water was not the end of a search. It was to hand, through a partner, and the man who chose it says as much.

What the water actually does

Spirit comes off the still far stronger than anyone drinks it, so every distiller cuts it with water, and whatever is dissolved in that water goes into the bottle. Which is why distillers want water with as little in it as possible.

They do not have to go anywhere to get it. Difford’s Guide, setting out how spirits are cut to strength, notes that it is common to find distilleries supplied by artesian wells or natural springs, and that much is made by marketing departments about the purity of the water used. Then it describes what the industry actually does: modern purification lets distillers demineralise water by reverse osmosis, producing pure, tasteless water that will not affect the flavour of the finished spirit.

That is the same trick as Burtonisation, run backwards. One trade adds the minerals it wants. The other takes them all out. Neither needs a volcano.

Two ways to solve a water problem

Burton. Analyse the famous water, find the sulphates, add the same salts to your own. The word for it is Burtonisation, and it still sounds like a municipal procedure.

Iceland. Distil in England, take the spirit to Borgarnes, cut it there, bring it back. The word for it is the brand.

Zamora took a majority stake in Martin Miller’s in January 2018. Its brand page still has the distillates travelling to Iceland to be blended.

What this is and isn’t

It is not a charge of dishonesty. The gin is good, the split distillation is real craft, the water is genuinely soft, and Miller was open about how strange the logistics looked: shipping gin to Iceland at the height of the vodka boom, he said, was a crazy thing to be doing. He died on Christmas Eve in 2013, a year after being diagnosed with oesophageal cancer. By 2012, the obituary has the brand turning over some fourteen million pounds a year.

What it is, is an order of events. A brand page that describes a search for the purest water on earth, and leaves out that a partner was already working in the place that was chosen, is not describing a discovery. It is describing a supply chain. Bromige, asked directly, says so himself and calls it luck.

For a beer drinker the useful part is the comparison. Brewers were every bit as romantic about Burton water, and the trade still ended up solving it with analysis and a sack of salts rather than a journey. When a drinks brand puts the place at the centre of the label, the question that separates provenance from decoration is a dull one, and Bromige has already answered it for his own.
