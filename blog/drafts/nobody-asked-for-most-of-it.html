Nobody Asked For Most Of It

Draft. Written in response to a Boak & Bailey post of 29 September 2026, with a second thread picked up from Business Insider's piece on Taste Labs the same week. Every claim in the closing section describes behaviour the app already has; nothing here is promised rather than shipped. Outstanding decision before this goes anywhere: whether to link the piece it answers.

A site that plans your pub crawl in a city you have never been to. A single-page app naming which world lagers are actually brewed in Burton. Press releases written by the same model that built the thing they are pitching.

The complaint is fair. These are answers hunting for a question, and nobody asked one.

But the line is being drawn in the wrong place. Was AI involved was never the test.

The Cooperage

Here is a better one. On Monday the person who built this app stood outside the Cooperage, near London Bridge, shut since 2021 — and his own app showed him a photograph of it trading. Awnings out, sandwich boards on the pavement, a woman walking past.

The database was right. The phone was wrong. The photograph had been replaced four days earlier at the same address, and because we serve images as permanent, every phone that had already seen the old one would keep showing it for a year. Nothing errored. Nothing looked broken. It was quietly wrong on every device that mattered.

You do not find that by generating a website. You find it by standing in the street using the thing you made.

Two confident fixes, both wrong

What happened next is the part worth reading. The fix was obvious, so we shipped it — and it was wrong. Then we shipped a second fix, reasoned from the data, and that was wrong too: it preferred the most recent photograph of each pub, which turned out to select a Tube platform, because Transport for London had re-photographed Bank station more recently than Google last drove down the street above it.

Two confident fixes. Both wrong. What caught them was a person looking at a picture and saying that is not a pub, that is the Central line.

It is tempting to call that a failure of taste, and to assume better data would fix it. We had the data. Every panorama arrives with the date it was taken, its distance from the pub, the name of whoever took it, and an identifier. We checked all four. Not one of them separates the street from the station: the platform was photographed more recently than the road above it, by the same company, and its identifier looks no different. The information is not in the metadata. It is in the picture.

There is a company in Silicon Valley with eighteen and a half million dollars betting the other way — that taste is a pattern you can learn from enough graded examples, and that a large enough taxonomy of quality will teach a model the difference between good and junk. It might. But what we needed was not a finer grading scheme. It was somebody who knew what the photograph was for.

That is the whole argument. The machine is very good at producing something plausible and very bad at noticing when plausible is false. Judgment, taste, and the willingness to keep looking after the status code says 200 stay human. Take those away and you get the pub crawl planner.

Ask what happens when it is wrong

So do not ask whether AI touched it. Ask what happens when it is wrong. A thing built to scratch its maker's own itch gets fixed, because they are still out there using it and it still annoys them. A thing built to prove the tools work gets abandoned the week the novelty does.

And ask for receipts. Not a claim of rigour — an artefact. A photograph taken by someone else carries their name; where we could not establish who took it, it carries nothing rather than a guess. Where we have no data at all, the app says nothing, instead of the reassuring shape of an answer.

AI has made it far cheaper to ship the plausible version. For anyone willing to keep standing in the street when it is wrong, it has made it cheaper to ship the true one.
