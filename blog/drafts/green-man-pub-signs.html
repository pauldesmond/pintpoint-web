The old gods are on the pub signs

England's most famous pagan survival was invented in 1939, and it was named after a pub.

There are nine Green Man pubs in our data, and every one of them is hanging out a face made of leaves. The foliate head is carved into English churches from the twelfth century onward — Exeter, Norwich, Southwell — peering out of the stonework with oak springing from its mouth. The standard reading is that he is a pagan god who survived the conversion, smuggled into the masonry by masons who had not quite let go of the old religion.

It is a wonderful story. It is also younger than the pubs.

Invented in 1939, named after a pub

The carvings had no agreed name until Lady Raglan gave them one, in an article called "The Green Man in Church Architecture" in the journal Folklore in 1939. She took the name from the pub signs, which were already everywhere, and attached it to the stonework. Then she argued that the foliate heads, the Jack-in-the-Green and Robin Hood were all the same thing: one ancient god of nature and fertility, living on under the noses of the clergy.

Folklorists have spent the eighty-odd years since taking it apart. There is no evidence the motif was pagan, no continuity anyone can demonstrate, and the Green Man as a mythological figure is now routinely described as twentieth-century folklore. One assessment of Raglan's original paper puts it plainly: her choice of the name was "based more upon inspiration than fact."

Which leaves the pub signs in an odd position. They are older than the myth they are supposed to illustrate. Whatever the landlords thought they were hanging up, it was not a fertility deity, because nobody had thought of that yet.

The unholy trinity

None of which slowed anybody down. British folk horror's three founding films — Witchfinder General in 1968, The Blood on Satan's Claw in 1971, The Wicker Man in 1973 — all turn on the same idea: that the old religion is still down there under the topsoil, and that the countryside is keeping something from you.

And the pub in The Wicker Man is The Green Man. Summerisle's entire pagan apparatus, the thing Sergeant Howie cannot get anyone to explain, is presented under a sign whose meaning was invented thirty-four years before the cameras rolled. The most ancient-seeming object in British folk horror is a coinage from 1939 hanging over a bar.

That is not a criticism of the film. It is the film's own point, arrived at from the outside: Summerisle's traditions are a Victorian landowner's invention, imposed a century earlier and believed in since. The set dressing was more honest than anyone intended.

Then they gave it to the children

The strange part is what happened next. The same decade that produced three films about rural human sacrifice handed the identical material to eight-year-olds as comedy.

Carry On Screaming! got there first, in 1966, guying Hammer so affectionately that Fenella Fielding's Valeria is still the definitive English vampiress. Then Shiver and Shake arrived in 1973 — two comics in one, the spooky half edited by a ghost, the other half by an elephant — carrying Scream Inn, a haunted pub nobody could survive a night in. Monster Fun followed in 1975 with Frankie Stein, Kid Kong, Draculass and Gums, a shark with false teeth. And from 1976, Rentaghost put a South Ealing agency on BBC1 that hired out the dead by the hour.

Anyone raised on that lot got their folklore twice: once as terror in the cinema their parents went to, and once as a joke in a comic that cost five pence. It is the specific British childhood the Scarred for Life books go through line by line — the public information films, the Tomorrow People, the sense that something in the woods was genuinely not right. The pagan and the pantomime arrived in the same week, from the same broadcaster.

Even the lantern is a pub story

While we are here: the jack-o'-lantern. The usual telling has an Irishman variously called Stingy Jack, Jack the Smith or Drunk Jack — "a knave and a trickster with a well-known taste for beer" — meeting the Devil on his way home. Jack asks for one last drink first, and in the pub he suggests a trick: the Devil turns himself into a silver coin to pay the landlord, then vanishes, and the two of them drink for nothing. The Devil obliges. Jack pockets him.

He is eventually condemned to walk the earth with a coal in a hollowed-out vegetable, and the vegetable in question was a turnip or a swede, because that is what nineteenth-century Ireland and Britain had. The pumpkin is what happened when the story emigrated and found something bigger and easier to carve.

Which makes Halloween's most recognisable object a souvenir of a man getting the Devil to buy a round.

So what do you actually drink

Not pumpkin, if you want the honest autumn beer.

Pumpkin ale is American and it began as poverty. The first written records of brewing with pumpkins are seventeenth century, and one of them describes beer made from "molasses, Indian corn, malt, and pompillons" — brewed, it says, by "the poor sort". A writer in 1771 complains about the "twang" of pompillon ale, conceding it improves if you cellar it two years. The beer historian Michael Ott puts it flatly: "Pumpkin beer used to be a drink of last resort. It wasn't something that was valorised at all." Pumpkin went in because barley was scarce and expensive. It was standing in for malt, not decorating it.

By the twentieth century the style was forgotten. It came back in 1985, when Bill Owens of Buffalo Bill's Brewery in California read that George Washington had used fruit and vegetables including pumpkins in his beers at Mount Vernon, and thought: why not a pumpkin ale? Every spiced, whipped-cream-rimmed pumpkin beer on a shelf this month descends from that moment.

Asked to confirm the Washington story, Ott couldn't verify it.

So the second-most-recognisable thing about American autumn rests on an anecdote nobody can source, revived forty years ago, in inversion of what the ingredient was originally for. Lady Raglan would have recognised the method.

Britain's real seasonal beer at the end of October is green hop. Hops picked and in the copper within hours, because undried hops rot; a beer that physically cannot be made at any other point in the year, and is gone by November. No spice, no pumpkin, no marketing calendar — just a constraint that has not changed since anyone started writing it down. If you want the autumn in a glass rather than the idea of autumn, that is where it is.

The signs stay up

Seventeen White Harts in our data. Sixteen Royal Oaks, twelve Moons, ten Dragons, seven Ravens, six Devils, six Cross Keys, one Hobgoblin. A white stag was a messenger from the otherworld long before it was Richard II's badge; the oak was venerated for centuries before Charles II hid in one; the keys are St Peter's and the moon is nobody's in particular.

The pub sign is where England files the things it has stopped being able to explain, and it does not much mind whether they were ever true. The Green Man got his name from a signboard and his religion from a magazine article, and he is still up there, dripping oak leaves over the door, on a building where people have been drinking since before anyone thought to ask what he meant.

Nine Green Man pubs, and a green hop beer has a fortnight to live. PINtPOINT knows which taps have it.
