At nine o'clock tonight, Denmark starts Christmas

Three Christmas beers, three cities, two dray horses — and an American who brewed his because he thought nobody else was left.

At 20:59 this evening — one minute to nine, and not a minute before — Tuborg's Julebryg goes on sale across Denmark. Brewery staff leave their desks, put on blue elf hats, and reach more than four hundred bars in a single night. Horse-drawn wagons in blue finery go out from the brewery into the pubs of Copenhagen. It is called J-dag, and it is the closest thing European brewing has to a starting pistol.

Four hundred miles away, Young's Winter Warmer arrives at Leadenhall Market each year the same way: a dray horse and cart, ten in the morning, the first barrel served on the cobbles. Young's kept twenty-seven dray horses in the 1870s, mostly black shires, running beer from the Ram Brewery in Wandsworth to pubs across the capital. Two countries, two Christmas beers, and both of them still think the right way to deliver the first barrel is behind a horse.

And in November 1975, in San Francisco, Fritz Maytag brewed a Christmas beer because he had looked for the tradition and concluded it was gone.

"The only real Christmas brew in the world"

Maytag's Anchor had just come back from England. In October 1975 he, his operations manager Gordon MacDermott and the photographer Lars Speyer had toured British breweries — Young's Ram Brewery first, then Wadworth, Donnington, Marston's, Samuel Smith's, and finally Timothy Taylor at Keighley. They came home, in David Burkhart's phrase, "at once elated and very disappointed." Maytag was blunter:

"We learned quickly that English brewing was not traditional at all, in the sense that they were all made with sugar… English ales were kind of a joke to us, just the way the English used to make fun of American beers. There's nothing wrong with either one of them."Fritz Maytag, in David Burkhart, The Anchor Brewing Story

A month later he brewed a Christmas beer anyway. And his reason for doing it is the sentence this whole piece turns on. As far as he could discover, he said, his would be "the only real Christmas brew in the world." The tradition "had recently disappeared, I think, even in Scandinavia, where it had lingered."

He was right about Denmark and wrong about the rest of Scandinavia. Tuborg's Julebryg did not launch until 1981; J-dag, the event, did not exist until 1990; the first Friday of November at 20:59 only became the fixed ritual in 1999. What Denmark does tonight is a revival, not a survival.

Norway had never stopped, and gloriously so. Aass, in Drammen, brewed a Christmas beer in 1877 — nineteen years before the Anchor Brewing Company existed under that name — and juleøl has been an unbroken national habit ever since. Norwegian beer writers count more than three hundred juleøl on sale there each winter, most of them brewed locally. While Maytag was in San Francisco concluding that nobody in the world was making a Christmas beer, a country of four million was making them by the dozen.

That is not a gotcha. It is what the world looked like from California in 1975, before anyone could check. He went looking with the tools he had, found nothing, and brewed one anyway — which is the more impressive version of the story, not the lesser one.

The beer that was not English

Here is where the story everyone tells goes wrong. Anchor's Christmas Ale is routinely described as Maytag's English homage, brought back from that Yorkshire tour. It wasn't.

Brew #151 went into the kettle on 17 November 1975 under the working title "2nd L. A." — a second Liberty Ale, after the revolutionary American ale Anchor had brewed that April for the anniversary of Paul Revere's ride. Burkhart is emphatic about what it was not: "not anything at all like Ballantine's Burton Ale, Koch's Holiday Ale, or Noche Buena." It was all-malt and dry-hopped with "a novel, distinctly American twist" — 100% Cascade, in place of the Hallertau the first Liberty Ale had used.

Even the dry-hopping came from home. Maytag knew the technique, he said, "from our Liberty Ale, although with the exception of a very few draught ales in England it was no longer done, and those very lightly." England was not dry-hopping. San Francisco was.

The rest was temperament. He bristled at calling it a holiday beer, and refused "Season's Greetings" and "Happy Holidays" on the label: the ale would not be watered down, and neither would its Christmas message. So it said "Merry Christmas and a Happy New Year", plainly. He vintage-dated the bottles like wine. It was sold only at the brewery. And until 1987 the bottles went out with blank die-cut neck labels, shipped loose, so customers could write their own greeting and glue it on by hand. Six hundred cases, hand-packed, with a Christmas tree Fritz sketched on a vertical oval and handed to Jim Stitt to finish.

The label went to the ATF on 12 December and came back approved in three days. The first Our Special Ale was bottled on 16 December 1975 at 6.5%, slightly hazy from the Cascade. Siebel's eight-strong tasting panel called it "robust and hoppy, with above average bitterness and afterbitter", and gave Fritz his first A+.

It became English later

And then the part nobody puts in the story. From 1983 to 1986, Our Special Ale was brewed as an all-malt traditional English brown ale — pale, caramel and Munich malts — evoking the brown ales Maytag had drunk in England. The English beer arrived eight years after the English trip.

Liberty Ale did the same thing. Maytag has long cited Timothy Taylor's Landlord as an inspiration for it, and Pete Brown records as much in the Oxford Companion to Beer. But Liberty Ale was first brewed on 18 April 1975, six months before Maytag set foot in Keighley. What resolves it is a parenthesis in Burkhart: the first brew was not all-malt, and subsequent brews would be. The beer changed. If Landlord shaped Liberty Ale, it shaped what the beer became rather than what it was.

Which is the real shape of Anchor's English inheritance, in both beers: not a recipe carried home in a suitcase, but an influence that arrived years late and worked backwards into a beer that already existed. It is a stranger claim than the tidy one, and it has the advantage of being what the sources say.

The spices — the thing that now defines Anchor Christmas Ale, undisclosed and changed every year — were never English at all. That was the departure from the tradition, not the inheritance of it.

The liquorice

One last turn. Tuborg's Julebryg — 5.6%, a dark Vienna-style lager on lager, Munich and caramel malt — is seasoned with English liquorice.

So the beer that starts Christmas in Denmark carries an English ingredient. The American who thought the tradition had died in Scandinavia was brewing his own, with an American hop, in the same winter. And the English brewery that still delivers its Christmas beer by dray horse is the first one Maytag walked into on the tour that changed his mind about tradition without changing his recipes.

Fifty years later

The thing to hold on to is how empty 1975 looks from here. Maytag went looking for a Christmas beer tradition and concluded there wasn't one worth the name — and he was not being careless. Julebryg was six years away. J-dag was fifteen.

Now the category is vast and nobody owns it. Belgium has its Noëls and its Christmas quadrupels, St Bernardus at 10% and Delirium Noël beside it. England kept the winter ale going and sells it under elves — Shepherd Neame's Christmas Ale, Ridgeway's Very Bad Elf. America, which had nothing at all in 1975, now has Tröegs' Mad Elf at 11% and a spiced-ale shelf in every bottle shop. Germany brews Weihnachtsbier and winter bocks, malt-forward and barely spiced at all. Norway's juleøl carry on as they have since before Anchor was Anchor, by the hundred. Japan has Sapporo's Winter Lager and Asahi's Special Winter Brew; Brazil has Baden Baden's Christmas beer. Denmark has a national event with a starting time. Our own catalogue holds a hundred and thirty-odd beers with Christmas, winter, yule, advent or festive in the name, and most of them are from breweries that did not exist when Maytag was hunting for company.

That is the measure of it. One man brewed a Christmas beer in San Francisco in 1975 because, as far as he could establish, nobody else in the world was bothering. Fifty years on, the argument is not whether the tradition survived but which country it belongs to — and the answer is that it never belonged to any of them. Tonight at 20:59, Denmark takes its turn.

Julebryg reaches a handful of British bars each year, and Anchor Christmas Ale used to. If either turns up on a tap near you this winter, PINtPOINT will know before we do.
