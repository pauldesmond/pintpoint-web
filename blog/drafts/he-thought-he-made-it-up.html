He thought he made it up

The first new American brewery since Prohibition was named after an Englishman’s claim on California, by a man who learned to brew from a kit bought in a Glasgow chemist.

One of Read the sign, five stories about what a name can tell you. The run starts with A cask called Father Christmas.

There is a ship on the label. It is sailing through hills, which no ship can do, and it is the Golden Hind.

The label belonged to the New Albion Brewing Company of Sonoma, California, the first new American brewery of the modern era, and the man who commissioned it was perfectly frank about what he was doing. History matters in brewing, Jack McAuliffe told the editor John Holl in 2011, and if you have not got one you can invent one. He had made English ale, porter and stout. He had taken the name of a brewery that closed with Prohibition. Name, logo and history, he said, and that was that.

Except that he invented none of it.

What the name already meant

On 17 June 1579 Francis Drake brought the Golden Hind into a cove on the northern California coast to careen her, having failed to find a short way home. He stayed five weeks, left on 23 July, and founded nothing whatever. The site is now identified as Drake’s Cove at Point Reyes, a National Historic Landmark since 2012, in the next county along from Sonoma.

Before he left he claimed the whole of it for Elizabeth and gave it a name. Two reasons are recorded in his own voyage narrative, and both of them are England: the white banks and cliffs along that shore looked like the ones at home, and Albion was the archaic name for Britain. Nova Albion. New Britain.

So the first new American brewery since Prohibition was named, by a man making English ale, after an Englishman’s claim on California, and carried an English ship on its label. That is not an invented history. That is the actual one, picked up whole.

And the brewery he borrowed was English too

There really had been an Albion in San Francisco. John Hamlin Burnell built it at Hunters Point over a set of natural springs, had the plant shipped out from England, and brewed ale and porter, bottling it in stoneware on site. By about 1908 the firm was trading as the Albion Ale and Porter Brewery. It closed around 1919 with Prohibition.

Its founding date is disputed, 1870 on the San Francisco landmark report and 1875 in the Brewery History Society journal, which is the kind of disagreement that tends to mean nobody checked the other one. The Norman tower Burnell put up over the springs is still standing at 881 Innes Avenue as San Francisco Landmark 60.

An English ale and porter brewery, with English kit, making the three beers McAuliffe went on to make. He thought he was borrowing a name. He was borrowing the lineage as well.

The chemist’s counter

John Robert McAuliffe was born in Caracas on 11 May 1945. He enrolled at Michigan Technological University, left it for the Navy in 1964, trained for thirty-eight weeks on Treasure Island and was posted to the submarine tender USS Simon Lake at Holy Loch, where he repaired submarine antennas. Tom Acitelli heads the chapter Dunoon, Scotland 1964 to 1968.

He drank in the pubs. Everybody writing about him says so, and what they agree he drank was British ale, porters and stouts above all. That part is never in doubt.

What happened next is the part the summaries keep getting wrong. In late 1966, running errands, he walked into a Boots in Glasgow and bought a pale ale kit and a plastic dustbin, took them back to a grey stone cottage in Dunoon and brewed over a coal fire. His Scottish neighbours drank the result.

Not on his return to America, which is where several accounts put it, including the illustrated histories. In Scotland, in 1966, two years into a four year posting, out of a shop on a Scottish high street.

And he could only do that because of a British budget three years earlier.

Why the kit was there to buy

Gladstone replaced the malt tax with a duty on finished beer in 1880, and the side effect was that a household brewing for itself needed a licence, with registration and inspection attached. It did not stop anybody. It simply drove it indoors. By 1961 and 1962, as Boak and Bailey set out, there were 250 licences to brew beer at home in the entire country.

On 3 April 1963 the Chancellor, Reginald Maudling, abolished the requirement in his budget speech.

What followed is the reason there was anything on that shelf in Glasgow. Ingredients and equipment that had been sold furtively came out from under the counter, were advertised openly and were made easier to use. Edme sold 300 tons of malt extract to British home brewers across 1966 and 1967, and launched pre-hopped extract in 1969, by which point a beginner with a bucket could make forty pints in a quarter of an hour.

The dustbin is the detail that gives him away. H. E. Bravery’s pocket guide of 1965, the make-do manual of that exact season, tells the beginner who needs a fermenting vessel to use a plastic dustbin. McAuliffe was not improvising from first principles in a foreign country. He was doing the ordinary British thing, with the ordinary British kit, in the year everyone else was doing it, because Parliament had just made it ordinary.

His own country took another fifteen years. Jimmy Carter signed the bill on 14 October 1978 and it took effect on 1 February 1979, exempting from tax a household making 200 gallons a year where two or more adults lived there, and 100 where there was one. By then New Albion had been open for two years and had two more to live.

The book he remembered buying

Acitelli has McAuliffe picking up Dave Line’s The Big Book of Brewing in that same Boots, and then concedes the problem in his own footnote.

The book is British and so is the date that matters. Amateur Winemaker published it at Andover in Hampshire in 1974, which is the year Boak and Bailey give for it and the year the Library of Congress catalogued it. McAuliffe left Scotland in 1968. There was nothing of Line’s on a Glasgow shelf in 1966 to pick up.

So he remembered buying it, and the print record says he cannot have. What a Boots sold him in 1966 was a kit with the instructions in the box. A famous title is an easy thing for a memory to reach for later.

The hop goes home

New Albion opened in a rented warehouse on the edge of Sonoma with a brewhouse McAuliffe welded together from dairy equipment and Coca-Cola syrup drums, three storeys high so it could run on gravity. He was not alone in it: Suzy Stern, later Suzy Denison, and Jane Zimmerman each put in fifteen hundred dollars and worked the hours, and the accounts that make him the solitary founder are leaving two people out. Its best year made 450 barrels. It closed in 1982.

It made an ale, a porter and a stout, and it used Cascade.

Which brings the traffic round. By 1980 Dave Line had written three books for Amateur Winemaker at Andover, and the last of them, Beer Kits and Brewing, has a chapter called North American Home Brew Practice. He finished it and died before it was bound; a note in the front, signed in March 1980, says he died suddenly while the book was at proof stage. He thanked his wife Sheila for typing it and his five year old son for not helping too much with the experiments.

The chapter is written to Americans, and it is written down to them a little. Britain had been free to brew at home since 1963 and America since 1978, and Line notes the signature and sets the two dates against each other. His British methods, he tells his new readers, hold good. The differences he allows are ingredients and measures: corn sugar, US gallons, local malts, American hops. British readers get a line saying they may need minor alterations, and a warning that corn sugar is not generally available here, which almost certainly went for the hops as well.

The one thing he grants the Americans as their own is a style. Steam beer, he writes, is about the only traditional American beer still brewed commercially. He does not say by whom. In 1980 he can only mean Anchor.

And the hop running through that chapter, in the house recipe and most of the others, is Cascade.

Anchor is the brewery Fritz Maytag remembers as the first to put Cascade to commercial use, a recollection he gave to Jeffrey House and one we have set out at length along with what complicates it. So the last book of the man who taught Britain how to clone its own beers is teaching America to brew, with the hop an American brewery had just rescued from the research plots, recommended back across the Atlantic to the country it came from, in a chapter about how much the Americans still had to learn.

They learned it. Within a decade the hop came back the other way and changed what British beer tastes like, which is a story we have told. Line did not live to see any of it.

Read the sign

McAuliffe said he made the history up, and what he actually did was pick up a real one and carry it without noticing the weight. An Englishman’s name for California, an English brewery’s identity, three English beer styles, a kit off a British shelf that a British chancellor had put there, and a hop that an American brewery would hand back to Britain inside ten years.

A name can tell you less than the sign claims, which is what the Nag’s Head and the Red Lion and Old Tom are for. This one told the truth while the man who chose it thought he was lying.
