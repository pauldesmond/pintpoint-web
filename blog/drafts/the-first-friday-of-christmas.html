The First Friday of Christmas

A thousand years of Christmas ale, from a Norwegian king who made it compulsory to a San Franciscan who thought he was the only one left doing it.

At 20:59 tonight — one minute to nine, and not a minute before — Tuborg's Julebryg goes on sale across Denmark. Brewery staff leave their desks, put on blue elf hats, and reach more than four hundred bars in a single night. Horse-drawn wagons in blue finery go out into the streets of Copenhagen. It is called J-dag, it has run since 1990, and it is the closest thing European brewing has to a starting pistol.

What it began as was a picture. Peter Wibroe’s blue snow landscape went out on a Tuborg postcard in 1979 and onto the sides of the beer lorries in 1980 — and the beer it now announces did not exist until 1981. The advertising film that has run almost unchanged ever since came later still, in 1984. The image came first, and the product was built to meet it. Julebryg is a 5.6% lager that appears at 20:59 on one named evening and then goes away again until the next November, which is most of the reason anyone queues for it. Until 1999 the pistol went off at one minute to midnight on the second Wednesday of November, and with a drinking age of eighteen that nobody enforced very hard, the country’s school classrooms and offices spent the Thursday accordingly; Tuborg moved it to a Friday. The start time is not a suggestion, either. Bars that tap the kegs early tend to be reported by their own customers, and go on Tuborg’s blacklist — which means that next year, they get the beer a day late.

Three nights in seven days, and not one of them is as old as it says. This is the third of three.

When brewing Christmas beer was the law

In tenth-century Norway, Christmas ale was not a seasonal treat. It was a legal obligation. Haakon the Good, who ruled from about 934, was bringing Christianity to a country that already had a midwinter feast and was not about to give it up. So he bound the new Christmas to the old jól. Snorri has him making it law "that Christmas should start at the same time as for the Christians, and that every man should brew from one measure of malt, or else pay fees" — and the Gulating law set the price. Miss a year and you owed the bishop three marks. Miss three winters running, or fail to pay, and you forfeited everything you owned: half to the king, half to the bishop. Some tellings add banishment on top.

The ale was blessed to Odin, to Frey and to Njord, for peace and a good harvest. The Norwegian for celebrating the season is still drikke jul — to drink Yule. Not to keep it, or observe it. To drink it.

Why the winter beer was the strong one

Two things made midwinter the moment for the best beer of the year, and neither was sentiment.

The first was the grain. By late October the harvest was in, malted and dry, and a brewer had more and better material than at any other point in the calendar. The second was the cold. Before refrigeration, summer brewing spoiled — many regions simply banned it between spring and Michaelmas — while crisp autumn air chilled the wort and allowed long, clean fermentations. Brewing the strongest and most complex beer of the year in the dark months was good microbiology three centuries before anybody knew what a microbe was.

The monasteries are the third reason, and they are where this piece has to slow down. The story everybody tells runs like this. Advent meant fasting, but liquidum non frangit ieiunium — liquid does not break the fast — so the brothers drank their dinner, something thick and sweet and full of calories to carry them through long vigils in unheated stone rooms; and the cellar ran three grades, the strongest runnings for the abbot and his guests, a middle beer for the lay brothers, a thin one for the pilgrims at the gate. It is a wonderful story and very little of it survives contact with a source. The Latin tag is real, but as a formal ruling it belongs to Pope Alexander VII in 1662, several centuries after the monks who are always said to have relied on it. The three-grade cellar comes from the Plan of St Gall, a ninth-century architectural drawing that sets aside separate brewing and baking for the monks, for the guests, and for the pilgrims and paupers — and as the beer historian Martyn Cornell spent years pointing out, a plan is not a building, this one was probably never built as drawn, and separate brewhouses are not evidence of three separate strengths of beer. Note also which way round it runs even in the telling: the pilgrim gets the thin stuff.

What survives the scrutiny is the part that actually mattered. Hospitality in a Benedictine house was not a courtesy but a rule — chapter 53 requires that every guest be received as Christ — and at Christmas, with that obligation at its peak and half of Europe on the road in the cold, the cellarer opened the good barrels. You do not need a fasting loophole to explain that.

England had a bowl

England's Christmas drink was not a beer so much as an event. The wassail bowl held strong spiced ale with sugar, nutmeg and ginger, and roasted crab apples dropped in hot until they burst — the pale froth they threw up gave the drink its name, lamb's wool. It went from house to house, and on Twelfth Night it went out to the orchards, where drinkers poured ale over the apple roots, shouted at the trees and fired shotguns into the branches to frighten off whatever might spoil next year's crop.

That is a tradition with a pulse. By the twentieth century it survived mainly as a line in a carol. The winter ales that came afterwards are a revival of the idea rather than a continuation of it — worth holding on to, because it means anyone looking for English Christmas beer in the nineteen-seventies was looking at the remains of something, not the thing itself.

Wales kept a horse of its own. The Mari Lwyd is a horse’s skull on a pole, dressed with ribbons and rosettes, its jaw sprung so that it snaps, carried under a white sheet by a party going door to door. The National Museum of Wales puts the earliest account at 1798 and the boom years at roughly 1850 to 1920, and places the custom mainly in the south, in Glamorgan, Monmouthshire and Carmarthenshire. It ran from Christmas Day through Twelfth Night to Hen Galan, the old new year on 13 January, and it called at every house or pub in the village. At each door came the pwnco, an improvised contest of verses in Welsh with the door still shut, which could last an hour. If the Mari party lost they left unfed, though the museum notes that rarely happened, because letting the Mari in was held to bring luck. What thinned it, in the museum’s account, was partly that fewer households could answer in Welsh and partly that the drinking had grown rowdy enough to set the chapels against it. Groups are performing it again across Wales.

England has no starting pistol. It has a horse. Winter begins here unofficially, on whichever late-October morning Young's sends the dray out — this year the twenty-third, a fortnight ago — carrying the first barrels of Winter Warmer, a ruby ale almost red in bright light, to The Lamb Tavern in Leadenhall Market. It arrives at ten, the first pint is poured on the Victorian cobbles, and a full English is laid on for anyone up early enough to drink it. The White Cross in Richmond runs its own from eight, sittings at eight, ten and midday: the dray horses come along the towpath, Morris dancers turn out in the road, and inside there is breakfast and Cockney carol singers — Carradine's Cockney Sing-a-long at an upright piano, under a banner promising a good old-fashioned kneesup. This October was the thirty-fifth time they have done it. Those two pubs are the only places in the country that get the beer first.

Young’s is not the only old English brewer still keeping a winter beer, only the one that gives it a morning. Fuller’s, across the river at Chiswick, pours Old Winter Ale, deep amber and 5.3%. Shepherd Neame in Faversham keeps its Christmas Ale to draught in December, 5% on cask and 7% in the bottle, with hints of spice and mulled fruit. Greene King’s Fireside is a 4.5% ruby ale hopped with First Gold and Cascade, which puts grapefruit in a winter beer. None of them arrives by horse.

Beyond the old brewers, British Christmas beer is mostly a matter of names. A twelve-beer British Christmas case runs from Ridgeway’s Bad Elf and Cairngorm’s Santa’s Sledgehammer to Purple Moose’s Merry X-Moose and a Swiss Roll Stout, with Samuel Smith’s Winter Welcome from Tadcaster in among them. Denmark has a starting pistol. Britain has puns.

That is how a season starts in England: not announced, just noticed, by whoever happens to be in Leadenhall at ten on a Friday. Denmark leaves nothing to inference. Christmas begins at 20:59 tonight, everywhere at once, and not a minute before.

Everyone else

Belgium looks like the deepest tradition of the lot and is one of the youngest. The beer historian Roel Mulder went through the Belgian archives and found no ancient monastic kerstbier at all: what cafés in Brussels and Charleroi were advertising as "Christmas! Christmas!" in the 1890s and 1900s was imported British beer — MacEwan's and Younger's Scotch ales, Allsopp's Burton ales. Belgian drinkers liked them enough to ask Belgian brewers to make something similar, and the style everyone now thinks of as immemorially Flemish grew out of that request. Gordon Xmas is that request still in its tartan: a dark, cherry-coloured 8.8% beer named after the Gordon clan, which its makers, Anthony Martin of Waterloo, say was created in the 1930s, label “Born in Scotland, Crafted in Belgium”, and bill as the original Christmas ale — some forty years after Brussels cafés were already chalking up “Christmas!” for the British imports. The modern descendants are enormous — St Bernardus Christmas Ale at 10%, bottle-conditioned and brewed for the season alone; De Dolle's Stille Nacht and Bush de Noël pushing past 12%; Delirium Noël alongside them. The Essen Christmas beer festival pours a couple of hundred different winter ales at cellar temperature, in the proper glassware, because it would be unthinkable not to.

Switzerland produced the most extreme version of the idea. Samichlaus was first brewed by Hürlimann of Zürich in 1979, on 6 December — St Nicholas's day — and only ever on that date, then laid down for ten months before release the following Christmas. At 14% it held the record as the strongest naturally fermented beer in the world, and it is still cellared by people who treat vintages the way other people treat claret.

Germany brews Weihnachtsbier and winter bocks that are malt-forward and barely spiced at all. Norway never stopped: Norwegian beer writers count more than three hundred juleøl on sale there each winter, most brewed locally, a thousand years after Haakon made it compulsory.

And America built its own version of J-dag without noticing. Great Lakes of Cleveland launched a honey, cinnamon and ginger Christmas Ale in 1992; the annual First Pour Day now draws queues down the block in October cold, and bars plan a season's takings around how many barrels they can get hold of before Christmas Eve. One Danish brewery decided when Christmas begins by decree. One Ohio brewery had it decided for them by the people queuing.

The man who thought he was alone

In November 1975, in San Francisco, Fritz Maytag brewed a Christmas beer because he had gone looking for the tradition and concluded it was gone. As far as he could discover, by his own account in David Burkhart's The Anchor Brewing Story, his would be "the only real Christmas brew in the world." It had "recently disappeared, I think, even in Scandinavia, where it had lingered."

He was right about Denmark, and wrong about almost everywhere else. Tuborg's Julebryg was six years away and J-dag fifteen — so the country with the starting pistol had not yet built it. But Norway had never stopped, Belgium had never stopped, and Aass of Drammen had brewed a Christmas beer in 1877, nineteen years before the Anchor Brewing Company existed under that name.

He was wrong about his own country too, and for a reason worth knowing. America had Christmas beer well before Prohibition — Barmann of Kingston, New York may have the earliest claim — and Miller was still selling a Christmas Special Beer in the mid-1930s, under a label of a family round the fire. Then the lawyers arrived. As the beer historian Don Russell puts it, a few years after Prohibition most states banned any reference to Christmas or Santa Claus on a label, so the Christmas beers became Holiday ales. Ballantine's version got round it by not being for sale at all: executives sent it out as a gift. The American tradition had not died. It had been legislated into a euphemism, which from the far side looks much the same.

He had even been standing in the exception a month earlier. Maytag's October 1975 tour of English breweries began at Young's Ram Brewery in Wandsworth — and Young's had Winter Warmer. It had not been called that for long. It was a Burton ale, the old strong dark style that all but died through the sixties as drinkers went to bitter and lager, and by then Young's was more or less the last brewery in England still making one. In 1971 they renamed it Winter Warmer and gave it a season, which is what a brewery does to a beer it is trying to save. The category we now call winter warmers may date from that single decision. He started at a brewery with a winter ale and came home convinced nobody was making one. Though England was thinner than it looks from here: Samuel Smith's of Tadcaster, another stop on the same trip, had no Christmas beer at all and would not have one until the late 1980s.

None of which is a gotcha. It is what the world looked like from California in 1975, before anyone could check. He went looking with the tools he had, found nothing, and brewed one anyway — and then did something nobody else was doing: changed the recipe every single year and kept quiet about it, and put a different hand-drawn tree on the label each time — Jim Stitt drew them for decades. Not Christmas trees, either: Maytag chose them for the solstice rather than the Nativity, so the run includes a Joshua tree, a blue gum eucalyptus, birches from his daughter's wedding and a coconut palm copied from a photograph he took in Hawaii. The man who would not print "Season's Greetings" was not going to put a standard-issue fir on the bottle. Collecting them became a ritual of its own; a binder of them used to sit behind the bar at Anchor Public Taps. The spices that now define the beer only arrived in 1987, when Maytag wanted something special for his own wedding, threw a handful into the previous year's batch, and found he had started another tradition by accident.

Noticing is getting harder. Jeff Alworth, writing on Beervana on 23 October this year, went looking for the cold wet afternoon that makes a drinker want a porter and found Oregon in the mid-seventies instead, summer having arrived in May and hung about towards November. He ordered the porter anyway. Which is the quiet argument for a date: a season you declare is one nobody can mistake, and a season you are meant to feel coming is one the weather can take away.

Whose is it

A Norwegian king made it compulsory. Monks opened the cellar because the rule said welcome the stranger. English villagers poured it on apple trees. A Swiss brewery makes one batch a year and waits ten months. A Danish brewery turned it into a national event with a start time. An American brewed one because he thought the whole thing had died, and was wrong.

The argument was never whether the tradition survived. It is whose it is — and the answer is that it has never belonged to any of them. Tonight at 20:59, Denmark takes its turn.

Julebryg reaches a handful of British bars each year, and a fair few of the others turn up on UK taps between now and January. PINtPOINT will show you where.

Footnote: Oktoberfest does not end in October, it relocates. Hyde Park Winter Wonderland opens on 19 November, thirteen days after J-dag, and runs to 3 January, and the Bavarian Village at the centre of it is sold by the organisers as "a taste of Bavaria in Hyde Park", promising "steins, bratwurst, live music, DJs and non-stop dancing once inside" Bavarian Hall. What the site does not say anywhere is whose beer fills the steins, which is the same gap we kept hitting on this autumn's UK Oktoberfest bills. The tents come to London for Christmas. The Julebryg stays in Denmark.
