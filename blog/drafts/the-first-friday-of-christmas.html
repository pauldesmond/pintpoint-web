The First Friday of Christmas

A thousand years of Christmas ale, from a Norwegian king who made it compulsory to a San Franciscan who thought his was the only real one left.

At 20:59 tonight, one minute to nine and not a minute before, Tuborg’s Julebryg goes on sale across Denmark. Carlsberg Danmark staff leave their desks, put on blue elf hats and go out in decorated minibuses to reach more than four hundred bars before midnight, by the brewery’s own count in 2019. It is called J-dag, it has run since 1990, and it is the closest thing European brewing has to a starting pistol.

What it began as was a picture. By Tuborg’s own account, Peter Wibroe’s blue snow landscape went out on a Christmas postcard in 1979 and became a label only in 1981, when the beer it now announces was launched. Julebryg is a 5.6% lager that appears on one named evening and goes away again until the next November, which is most of the reason anyone queues for it. Until 1999 the pistol went off on the second Wednesday, and the country’s classrooms and offices spent the Thursday accordingly; Tuborg moved it to a Friday. Carlsberg’s Jens Bekke told NPR’s Sidsel Overgaard in 2012 that bars tapping early generally get turned in by their own customers and go on Tuborg’s blacklist, which means that next year they get the beer a day late.

Three nights in seven days, and not one of them is as old as it says. This is the third of three.

When brewing Christmas beer was the law

In tenth-century Norway, Christmas ale was not a seasonal treat. It was a legal obligation. Haakon the Good, who ruled from about 934, was bringing Christianity to a country that already had a midwinter feast and was not about to give it up, so he bound the new Christmas to the old jól. Snorri has him making it law “that Christmas should start at the same time as for the Christians, and that every man should brew from one measure of malt, or else pay fees”. The later Gulating law set the price: miss a year and you owed the bishop three marks, and miss three winters running and you forfeited everything you owned.

The ale was blessed to Odin, to Frey and to Njord, for peace and a good harvest. The Norwegian for celebrating the season is still drikke jul: to drink Yule.

Charleroi, 1896

Belgium looks like the deepest tradition of the lot and is one of the youngest. The beer historian Roel Mulder went through the archives and found nothing older than 1896. What cafés were advertising as Christmas was imported British beer: the oldest mention he found runs in the Journal de Charleroi on 20 December 1896, and by 1911 Liège was selling Allsopp’s of Burton as “Scotch-Ale Christmas-Bier”. Belgian brewers imitated them, and the style everyone now thinks of as immemorially Flemish grew out of that, the first imitation dated by Mulder to 1903, when a brewery near Charleroi tried to sell its ordinary triple as a bière de Noël.

Samichlaus is the extreme case of a beer that is a date. It has only ever been brewed on 6 December, St Nicholas’s day, then laid down for ten months before release the following Christmas. Swiss-born at Hürlimann of Zürich, it has been brewed in Austria by Schloss Eggenberg since 2000, and the date went with it.

America built its own version of J-dag without planning one. Great Lakes of Cleveland launched a Christmas Ale in 1992 and now picks a Thursday in October for its First Pour, and Cleveland queues round the block for it.

The man who thought he was alone

In November 1975, in San Francisco, Fritz Maytag brewed a Christmas beer because he had gone looking for the tradition and could not find one he counted. By his own account in David Burkhart’s The Anchor Brewing Story (Ten Speed Press, 2022), his would be “the only real Christmas brew in the world”. It had “recently disappeared, I think, even in Scandinavia, where it had lingered”.

He was right about Denmark and wrong about almost everywhere else. Julebryg was six years away and J-dag fifteen, so the country with the starting pistol had not yet built it. But Norway had never stopped, nor Belgium, and Aass of Drammen had brewed a Christmas beer in 1877, nineteen years before the Anchor Brewing Company existed under that name.

He knew his own country better than that, mind. Burkhart has the new beer conceived as “not anything at all like Ballantine’s Burton Ale, Koch’s Holiday Ale, or Noche Buena”, so Maytag was not claiming nobody else brewed for the season. He was claiming none of them was the real thing, and the reason there were holiday ales to be unlike is itself worth knowing. America had Christmas beer well before Prohibition: the beer writer Tom Acitelli has Miller still selling a Christmas Special Beer in the mid-1930s. Then the lawyers arrived. As the beer writer Don Russell has it, a few years after Prohibition most states banned references to Christmas or Santa Claus, so, he reckons, the Christmas beers became Holiday ales. Labelling law had renamed the tradition rather than ending it.

His October 1975 tour of English breweries had begun at Young’s of Wandsworth, who brewed Winter Warmer. A winter beer is not a Christmas beer, which is a distinction with its own history.

England was thinner than it looks from here, mind. When Boak & Bailey went back through Frank Baillie’s Beer Drinker’s Companion of 1973 they found winter ales coming out in November and December and, as far as they could see, exactly one beer in the country with Christmas in its name: Shepherd Neame’s, in a bottle. By the Good Beer Guide published in 1986 there were around ten to count.

The spice arrived with them, on both sides at once. Reviewing that new British crop in What’s Brewing in December 1986, Danny Blyth found the strong dark Christmas beers were not spiced; Maytag threw his first handful into the Anchor batch the following year, wanting something special for his own wedding. The thing everybody now takes for granted as the definition of a Christmas beer is younger than the revival that carried it.

He knew the seasonal beers. What he wanted was a Christmas beer of his own, so he brewed one, and then changed the recipe every year and kept quiet about it, putting a different hand-drawn tree on the label each time, drawn for decades by Jim Stitt. Not always Christmas trees either: the run includes a Joshua tree and a coconut palm.

Whose is it

A Norwegian king made it compulsory. Belgian cafés imported it and Belgian brewers copied it. A Swiss brewery gave it one day in the year and ten months in the cellar. A Danish brewery turned it into a national event with a start time. An American knew the seasonal beers his own country already had and wanted one of his own.

And in the early 2000s a Finnish beer club, Olutseura Olviretki, sent a certificate to a brewery in Lewes, because Harvey’s Christmas Ale answered the beer described in Seven Brothers, the 1870 novel by Aleksis Kivi that Finland treats as its national book: “strong”, “dark red”, and good for “a slight dizziness in the brain”. The beer writer Will Hawkes tells that story. A Finnish drinking club recognised its own country’s Christmas in a glass of Sussex barley wine.

The argument was never whether the tradition survived. It is whose it is, and it has never belonged to any of them. Tonight at 20:59, Denmark takes its turn.

A fair few of these turn up on UK taps between now and January. When somebody logs one, its PINtPOINT page shows where it was poured and when.
