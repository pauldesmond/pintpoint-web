The First Friday of Christmas

A thousand years of Christmas ale, from a Norwegian king who made it compulsory to a San Franciscan who thought he was the only one left doing it.

At 20:59 tonight, one minute to nine and not a minute before, Tuborg’s Julebryg goes on sale across Denmark. Carlsberg Danmark staff leave their desks, put on blue elf hats and go out in decorated minibuses to reach more than four hundred bars before midnight, by the brewery’s own count. It is called J-dag, it has run since 1990, and it is the closest thing European brewing has to a starting pistol.

What it began as was a picture. By Tuborg’s own account, Peter Wibroe’s blue snow landscape went out on a Christmas postcard in 1979 and became a label only in 1981, when the beer it now announces was launched. Julebryg is a 5.6% lager that appears on one named evening and goes away again until the next November, which is most of the reason anyone queues for it. Until 1999 the pistol went off on the second Wednesday, and the country’s classrooms and offices spent the Thursday accordingly; Tuborg moved it to a Friday. Sidsel Overgaard, reporting from Denmark, found that bars tapping early tend to be turned in by their own customers and go on Tuborg’s blacklist, which means that next year they get the beer a day late.

Three nights in seven days, and not one of them is as old as it says. This is the third of three.

When brewing Christmas beer was the law

In tenth-century Norway, Christmas ale was not a seasonal treat. It was a legal obligation. Haakon the Good, who ruled from about 934, was bringing Christianity to a country that already had a midwinter feast and was not about to give it up, so he bound the new Christmas to the old jól. Snorri has him making it law “that Christmas should start at the same time as for the Christians, and that every man should brew from one measure of malt, or else pay fees”. Miss a year and you owed the bishop three marks; miss three winters running and you forfeited everything you owned.

The ale was blessed to Odin, to Frey and to Njord, for peace and a good harvest. The Norwegian for celebrating the season is still drikke jul: to drink Yule.

Why the winter beer was the strong one

Two things made midwinter the moment for the best beer of the year, and neither was sentiment. The first was the grain: by late October the harvest was in, malted and dry. The second was the cold. Summer brewing spoiled before refrigeration, and many regions banned it outright, while crisp autumn air chilled the wort and allowed long, clean fermentations.

The monasteries are the third reason, and the one to handle carefully. The story is that Advent meant fasting, but liquidum non frangit ieiunium, liquid does not break the fast, so the brothers drank their dinner; and that the cellar ran three grades, the strongest for the abbot and the thinnest for the pilgrims at the gate. The Latin tag is real, but as a formal ruling it belongs to a seventeenth-century argument about whether chocolate broke a fast, centuries after the monks who are always said to have relied on it. The three grades come from the Plan of St Gall, a ninth-century architectural drawing, and as the beer historian Martyn Cornell spent years pointing out, a plan is not a building.

What survives the scrutiny is narrower. Hospitality in a Benedictine house was not a courtesy but a rule, chapter 53 requiring that every guest be received as Christ, and at Christmas half of Europe was on the road in the cold. What came out of the cellar for them is not recorded.

England had a bowl

England’s Christmas drink was not a beer so much as an event. The wassail bowl held strong spiced ale with sugar, nutmeg and ginger, and roasted crab apples dropped in hot until they burst, the pale froth giving the drink its name, lamb’s wool. On Twelfth Night it went out to the orchards, where drinkers poured ale over the apple roots and fired shotguns into the branches to frighten off whatever might spoil the crop.

It had a commercial life after that. The beer writer Will Hawkes found the Phillips Brewery advertising its Christmas specialities in the Oxford Times in December 1888, and reckons Victorian Christmas beers often ran past 10%. By the twentieth century it survived mainly as a line in a carol, which matters: anyone looking for English Christmas beer in the nineteen-seventies was looking at the remains of something.

England has no starting pistol. It has a dray. Winter begins here unofficially, on whichever late-October morning Young’s sends the horses out, this year the twenty-third, carrying the first barrels of Winter Warmer to The Lamb Tavern in Leadenhall Market. The market’s own billing has it arriving at ten, the first pint poured on the Victorian cobbles and a full English laid on for anyone up early enough to drink it. The White Cross in Richmond runs its own from eight, Morris dancers in the road, for the thirty-fifth time this October.

Young’s is not the only old English brewer still keeping a winter beer, only the one that gives it a morning. Fuller’s pours Old Winter Ale at 5.3% and Shepherd Neame keeps its Christmas Ale to draught in December, while Greene King’s Fireside puts grapefruit in a winter beer.

Sussex has the ritual. Harvey’s of Lewes had been brewing Elizabethan Ale since 1953 when head brewer Anthony Jenner adapted it, in 1972, into something darker and dry-hopped for the season, and called the result liquid Christmas Pudding. When the brew is mashed the hops are laid around the mash tun and the beer is blessed, in words Miles Jenner’s favourite of which were written by the Rev. Judith Egar.

Beyond the old brewers, British Christmas beer is largely a matter of names, and the question is what the word on the label is claiming. The beer writer Phil Mellows, asked last December to pick ten Christmas beers off a supermarket shelf, proposed the shortest test anybody has offered: will it stir into a Christmas pudding. By that measure a cask ale a shade over 4% with a festive pump clip is, in his words, “just a marketing thing”. He also had to resort to Fortnum & Mason to find a single beer with Christmas in its name, brewed for them in Kent by Westerham at 5%. His test and Anthony Jenner’s nickname are the same sentence fifty-three years apart.

Everyone else

Belgium looks like the deepest tradition of the lot and is one of the youngest. The beer historian Roel Mulder went through the archives and found no ancient monastic kerstbier at all. What cafés were advertising as Christmas was imported British beer: the oldest mention he found runs in the Journal de Charleroi on 20 December 1896, and by 1911 Liège was selling Allsopp’s of Burton as “Scotch-Ale Christmas-Bier”. Belgian brewers imitated them, and the style everyone now thinks of as immemorially Flemish grew out of that, the first imitation dated by Mulder to 1903, when a brewery near Charleroi sold its ordinary triple as a bière de Noël.

America built its own version of J-dag without planning one. Great Lakes of Cleveland launched a Christmas Ale in 1992, and its First Pour Day now draws queues down the block in October cold. Denmark set its date by decree. Ohio had its set by the people queuing.

The man who thought he was alone

In November 1975, in San Francisco, Fritz Maytag brewed a Christmas beer because he had gone looking for the tradition and concluded it was gone. By his own account in David Burkhart’s The Anchor Brewing Story, his would be “the only real Christmas brew in the world”. It had “recently disappeared, I think, even in Scandinavia, where it had lingered”.

He was right about Denmark and wrong about almost everywhere else. Julebryg was six years away and J-dag fifteen, so the country with the starting pistol had not yet built it. But Norway had never stopped, nor Belgium, and Aass of Drammen had brewed a Christmas beer in 1877, nineteen years before the Anchor Brewing Company existed under that name.

He knew his own country better than that, mind. Burkhart has the new beer conceived as “not anything at all like Ballantine’s Burton Ale, Koch’s Holiday Ale, or Noche Buena”, so Maytag was not claiming nobody else brewed for the season. He was claiming none of them was the real thing, and the reason there were holiday ales to be unlike is itself worth knowing. America had Christmas beer well before Prohibition: the beer writer Tom Acitelli has Miller still selling a Christmas Special Beer in the mid-1930s. Then the lawyers arrived. As the beer historian Don Russell has it, a few years after Prohibition most states banned references to Christmas or Santa Claus, so the Christmas beers became Holiday ales. Labelling law had renamed the tradition rather than ending it.

He had walked past the other half of the answer a month earlier. His October 1975 tour of English breweries began at Young’s Ram Brewery in Wandsworth, and Young’s had Winter Warmer. It had not been called that for long. It was a Burton ale, the old strong dark style that all but died through the sixties, and by then Young’s was more or less the last brewery in England still making one. In 1971 they renamed it Winter Warmer and gave it a season, which is what a brewery does to a beer it is trying to save. That is a winter beer and not a Christmas one, so it does not catch him out. It is the category we now reach for instead, and it was four years old when he went looking.

England was thinner than it looks from here, mind. When Boak & Bailey went back through Frank Baillie’s Beer Drinker’s Companion of 1973 they found winter ales coming out in November and December and, as far as they could see, exactly one beer in the country with Christmas in its name: Shepherd Neame’s, in a bottle. By the Good Beer Guide published in 1986 there were around ten to count.

The spice arrived with them, on both sides at once. Reviewing that new British crop in What’s Brewing in December 1986, Danny Blyth found the strong dark Christmas beers were not spiced; Maytag threw his first handful into the Anchor batch the following year, wanting something special for his own wedding. The thing everybody now takes for granted as the definition of a Christmas beer is younger than the revival that carried it.

It is what the world looked like from California in 1975, before anyone could check. He went looking with the tools he had, found nothing, brewed one anyway, and then changed the recipe every year and kept quiet about it, putting a different hand-drawn tree on the label each time, drawn for decades by Jim Stitt. Not Christmas trees either: Maytag chose them for the solstice rather than the Nativity, so the run includes a Joshua tree and a coconut palm.

Noticing is getting harder. Jeff Alworth, writing on Beervana on 23 October this year, went looking for the cold wet afternoon that makes a drinker want a porter and found the thermometer in the mid-seventies instead. He ordered the porter anyway. Which is the argument for a date: a season you are meant to feel coming is one the weather can take away.

Whose is it

A Norwegian king made it compulsory. Monks opened the cellar because the rule said welcome the stranger. English villagers poured it on apple trees. Belgian cafés imported it and then copied it. A Danish brewery turned it into a national event with a start time. An American brewed one because he thought the whole thing had died, and was wrong.

And in 2004 a Finnish beer club sent a certificate to a brewery in Lewes, because Harvey’s Christmas Ale answered the beer described in Seven Brothers, the 1870 novel by Aleksis Kivi that Finland treats as its national book: “strong”, “dark red”, and good for “a slight dizziness in the brain”. Hawkes tells that story. A Finnish reader recognised his own country’s Christmas in a glass of Sussex barley wine.

The argument was never whether the tradition survived. It is whose it is, and it has never belonged to any of them. Tonight at 20:59, Denmark takes its turn.
