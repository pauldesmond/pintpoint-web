Everyone kept a different Oktoberfest

The 191st Wiesn is tapped tomorrow morning. Munich changed its beer decades ago, and everywhere else settled on a different answer — including two contradictory ones in Britain.

The 191st Oktoberfest opens in Munich tomorrow and runs to 4 October, tapped as always on the first Saturday after the fifteenth — which this year falls on the 19th. The name has travelled further than almost any other in beer — to Brazil, to Ontario, to Ohio, to a former cotton mill in Manchester. What has not travelled with it is the beer. Say "Oktoberfest" in four countries and you will be handed four different drinks, and the oldest of them is not the one in Munich.

Munich changed the beer, and kept the name

The festival beer used to be dark — closer to a Munich dunkel than to anything poured in the tents now. The turn came in 1872, during a hot summer, when a brewer ran out of dark lager and put out a stronger Vienna-style beer instead, somewhere around 8%. It sold. Bock-strength festival lagers held on until the First World War, and between the wars the beer wandered anywhere from 4% to 9%, settling into the reddish-brown colour the world now calls Märzen.

Then Munich moved again. Over the second half of the twentieth century its brewers lightened the festival beer into the pale, dry, aggressively drinkable Festbier the tents pour today — a beer designed to be drunk by the litre for sixteen days without ending you. It is an excellent beer and a genuine piece of engineering. It is also not what was in the Schottenhamel tent in 1890.

So the most traditional festival in beer is pouring its newest recipe, and has been for fifty-odd years.

Half of Britain drinks the modern one, straight from the source

The obvious assumption is that the export versions are the nostalgic ones — that Britain and America are out here preserving something Munich abandoned. For one half of Britain it is the other way round: the importers are the most Munich-faithful room of the lot.

Albert's Schloss opens its Oktoberfest tomorrow as well — the same morning as the Wiesn — and runs it through to 25 October across Manchester, Liverpool, Birmingham and Soho, pouring all six of the Munich Oktoberfest breweries: Spaten, Paulaner, Löwenbräu, Hacker-Pschorr, Hofbräu and Augustiner, delivered to tank. Not beers in the style of the Wiesn. The actual Wiesn beers, which means the pale modern Festbier, which means Manchester is drinking exactly what Munich is drinking.

London Oktoberfest lands in Finsbury Park on 1–3 October and advertises Bavarian Festbier too. Whatever else the marquee-and-oompah circuit is doing, it is not smuggling the nineteenth century back in.

And a second Britain, making it up as it goes

That is only the importing half. Alongside it is a whole British craft cohort brewing its own festival beer, and this is where the vocabulary stops meaning anything and starts being fun.

Polly's have gone out at 5.7% under the name Festbier – Märzen, which is both answers at once — the two beers this entire piece is about, printed on one can, with a note explaining they picked the Märzen because it is the historically significant one. Thornbridge's Zehn is badged a 6% Fest Bier and arrives amber, built on Pilsner and crystal malt with a robust sweetness — the Munich word on a beer shaped like the Milwaukee one. Rooster's of Harrogate call theirs "an almost authentic German lager", hop it with Fuggles from England, Crystal from America and Tettnang from Germany, and brewed it with a Californian brewery. Their festival is called An Almost Authentic Oktoberfest. Buxton's Volksbier is a 6% Fest Bier with a "contemporary twist"; Track in Manchester have a 5.7% Spezial Festbier on Hallertau Mittelfrüh and Tettnanger.

The events match the beer. RedWillow hold their annual Lager & Sausage festival on Park Green in Macclesfield on Saturday from noon, with a huge range of traditional lagers, a ceremonial cask tapping and music from the Oompah Stompers — which is Munich's opening ritual performed on a cask, the one vessel Munich would never use. BREWLDN run an Oktoberfest at Riverside East; Indie Rabble have their own. You can even buy a Prostmeister Oktoberfest lager to ferment in your kitchen, which is the name travelling about as far as a name can go.

None of it is preservation and none of it is replication. America kept a style; Munich engineered one; Britain treats the whole German festival vocabulary as a licence — borrow the words, brew what you fancy, tap a cask, book the oompah band. Given that the festival's own history is a story of quietly changing the recipe and keeping the name, that may be the most Bavarian behaviour on offer.

America kept the old beer

The country with no historical claim on the festival at all is the one that preserved its beer. The American Oktoberfest is a Märzen: amber to deep copper, malt-forward, bread crust and caramel and toffee, typically 5–6% — a shade stronger, sweeter and more caramelised than the German article, and often carrying more hop bitterness than German brewers would bother with.

The keepers of the flame are doing it properly. Jack's Abby's Copper Legend is built on imported Pilsner, Vienna and Munich malts and decoction-mashed, which is a laborious old-world technique nobody adopts by accident. Shiner's Oktoberfest runs Munich and caramel malts against Hallertau Tradition and Hersbrucker hops. Neither is chasing what Munich is pouring. Both are brewing what Munich used to pour, in a country that never had a Theresienwiese to lose it on.

And this year, two of the biggest switched sides

Here is the part that makes 2026 worth writing down. The two most visible American names in the category have both crossed over to the modern German style — and both did it by going to Bavaria for a partner.

Sierra Nevada's 2026 Oktoberfest is a golden Festbier brewed with Hoppebräu of Waakirchen, 6%, lightly dry-hopped for a lift of floral aroma over a biscuity Munich-malt base. Firestone Walker, whose Oaktoberfest was a Märzen aged in French oak wine barrels, has this year made a 6% Fest Bier with Mahrs Bräu of Bamberg instead.

Which leaves the map upside down from where most drinkers assume it sits. Munich pours the new beer. Britain imports the new beer by the tankful. America kept the old beer — and its biggest brewers are now, one by one, giving it up.

Three festivals, all the biggest outside Munich

The name has outgrown the beer entirely in places. Blumenau in Santa Catarina has run one since 1984 and draws somewhere between 500,000 and 700,000 — 634,700 in 2022. Kitchener–Waterloo in Ontario bills itself as Canada's Greatest Bavarian Festival and claims roughly 700,000. Oktoberfest Zinzinnati in Cincinnati, first held in 1976 and marking its fiftieth this year, posted a record 808,300.

All three are described, somewhere in their own literature or someone else's, as the largest Oktoberfest outside Germany. By attendance the Ohio one wins, which is not the answer any of the marketing wants. It is a fitting way for the thing to end up: several large rooms, in several countries, each convinced it holds the real version of a festival whose home city quietly changed the recipe.

The one thing that travelled intact

For all that, there is exactly one part of Oktoberfest that arrives everywhere unchanged, and it is not in the glass. Two or three times an hour — roughly every fifteen or twenty minutes — the band stops whatever it is playing and starts Ein Prosit der Gemütlichkeit. A few thousand people put down their conversations, pick up a litre of beer and sing a toast to Gemütlichkeit, a word English has never managed to import: warmth, ease, the specific contentment of being among people with no particular need to be anywhere else. Then comes the count — Oans, zwoa, drei, g'suffa! One, two, three, drink — and the room drinks together.

That happens in Munich. It also happens in Blumenau, in Kitchener, in Cincinnati, and in a converted Manchester bank at teatime. Wherever the name has gone, the choreography has gone with it, to the minute. The beer is the part that changed — the toast came through perfectly. Which suggests the festival was never really about a recipe. It was about the fifteen-minute interval at which strangers agree to become a crowd, and that exports beautifully, in any colour of lager you like.

There is no correct Oktoberfest any more, only rooms. If the pale Festbier or the copper Märzen turns up on a tap near you in the next fortnight, PINtPOINT will know before we do.
