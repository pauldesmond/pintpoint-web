Ghosts of Baddow Brewery

Baddow Brewery stopped brewing in 1927. Nearly a century later, 30 of the 61 pubs associated with it are still serving drinkers.

PINtPOINT maps where Essex drinks now. This post maps where it used to.

There is a modern echo too. Chelmsford Brewing Company now brews at Brewery Fields, a stone’s throw from the old brewing ground. The ghosts come first.

At 47 and 49 Church Street in Great Baddow stands The Old Brewery: the surviving shell of Baddow Brewery, a local brewing business whose story runs from the late eighteenth century through to the age of tied houses, consolidation and closure. Historic England listed the building Grade II in 1975, as a brewery of 1868 later used as a furniture factory.

The outline is simple enough. The Brewery History Society dates the brewery to 1798 and a Mr Crabb. In 1866 Frederick and Arthur Veley bought into it, along with 37 pubs and a beer shop. Thomas Smee was a partner by 1868, when the foundation stone of the new brewery was laid, and the firm traded as Crabb, Veley & Company until 1887. The 1868 building is not George Scamell’s work: Brewery History journal 111 and Historic England both credit Scamell with the additions of 1878, and place him in Truman, Hanbury, Buxton & Co’s engineering department “probably during the 1860s”. By 1902 Reginald Watney was managing director, the Society records, and Helen Gray adds that he was chairman when he died in 1923. In 1927 the company was bought by Seabrooke & Sons of Grays, and brewing at Great Baddow came to an end. Gray also sets down the local lore about why it sold, that the founder’s three granddaughters were staunch Temperance movement members. It comes from Allen Buckroyd’s Great Baddow Oral History, and is worth taking as lore rather than as a finding.

But the more interesting story is the network of pubs it left behind.

The answer: 30 still appear to be trading

After working through the estate pub by pub, the count, as of August 2026, is:

30Open*
14Replaced
15Closed
2Unknown

That gives us a beautifully clean, slightly melancholy headline: of Baddow Brewery’s 61 listed pubs, 30 still appear to be trading, though not always under the same name, interior, ownership or beer. One caveat on the number. The Brewery History Society records the 1927 Seabrooke acquisition with 53 tied houses while its own pub list runs to 61 entries, and this audit follows the full 61-name list, treating the 53 as the acquisition snapshot rather than the whole estate. Some of the 61 had already gone decades before 1927.

*Method note: “Open” includes renamed pubs, free houses, hotel bars and pub-restaurants such as Harvester, as well as traditional wet-led locals.

Another 29 have ceased trading as pubs. Two remain unknown because the evidence is too thin to call honestly.

The survivors

The living half of the estate is the joy of the thing. The transformations are the story.

Four living transformations

  • Coopers Arms → Hop Shop: the same Moulsham Street address, open since November 2014 as Essex’s first micropub.
  • Crown, Hatfield Peverel → The William Boosey: renamed, and still a pub.
  • Nags Head, Moreton → Dog & Pickle: a renamed survivor with its own modern character.
  • Five Bells, Vange → Harvester: now a pub-restaurant, and still licensed.

That is why the count matters. Baddow Brewery is dead as a brewery company, but its estate has not vanished. Half of it is still visible in the modern pub map. The other half survives as restaurants, shops, houses, names, rumours and awkward gaps in the record.

The changed and the gone

Some former Baddow houses survive physically but no longer function as pubs. Some are restaurants, shops, homes, holiday lets or housing developments. Some appear to be simply closed. Some have left only a name in a directory and a stubborn gap on the map.

Sugar Hut deserves the spotlight here. The White Hart at Brentwood did not survive as a pub. It became the nightclub that features in The Only Way Is Essex: a former Baddow tied house reborn as reality-TV shorthand. The Bull at Pitsea gave its name to Bull Mews housing. That is what the gone half looks like too, mutation as often as disappearance.

The two unknowns

The two awkward entries are Gardeners Arms, Kelvedon and New Inn, Pitsea.

Gardeners Arms is troublesome because ClosedPubs’ Kelvedon list and PubWiki’s both give fairly full Kelvedon lost-pub lists, and neither has a Gardeners Arms. Until a street, licensee or tithe entry turns up, it remains a name on a brewery list rather than a pinned house. New Inn, Pitsea is similarly polluted by other Essex New Inns, and the historic address is not yet nailed down tightly enough. So both stay marked unknown until better evidence turns up. That is the map being honest.

Baddow brews again

Chelmsford Brewing Company says that in 2017 it “took the leap into professional brewing at Brewery Fields in Great Baddow, helping to bring commercial brewing back to the village”. Chelmsford CAMRA’s visit write-up places it “only a stone’s throw from the old Baddow Brewery”, and records that the Blueshack it grew out of was started in 2014 by Hans Oelofse and his son Ben, and named after their garden shed. It is exactly the right local-brewery story: a father and son, a shed, and a hobby that outgrew it.

Chelmsford has other brewers too, including Radio City Beer Works and Brewhouse & Kitchen, but in a post about Baddow only Chelmsford Brewing Company works the old brewing ground, and the geography matters.
