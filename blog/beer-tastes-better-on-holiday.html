It's Not Just the Sunshine: Your Holiday* Beer Really Is a Different Beer

You've had this feeling. First Estrella of the holiday, six euros, terrace in the shade off Passeig de Gràcia. Perfect. Three weeks later you order the same beer at a pub in Manchester and it is, unmistakably, not the same beer.

There's a reason, and it isn't the sunshine. The Estrella poured in Barcelona is brewed by Damm at El Prat de Llobregat, beside the airport south-west of the city. Much of the Estrella poured in Manchester is brewed by Damm too, at the Eagle Brewery in Bedford, where Estrella had been packaged for the UK since 2010 and which Damm bought from Carlsberg Marston's in 2022, its first brewery outside the Iberian Peninsula. Damm has brewed there since spring 2024 and says it has put more than £70 million into the site.

The recipe is not the difference. Damm's UK managing director Luke White told just-drinks there is "no difference in recipe. It's exactly the same recipe, same raw materials. It's just a slightly different abv". The strength is the difference: 4.6% here, 5.4% in Spain. Same red star, same font, weaker beer.

The Bedford Estrella, and the rest

Bedford is not unusual. CAMRA's Beer in the UK report, edited by Tim Webb and published in July 2026, lists thirty-six lager brands it says are produced in the UK, "with many but not all, simplified to meet UK specifications, usually meaning lower strength". Estrella Damm, San Miguel, Mahou, Cruzcampo and Madri are all on it. A further four, including Estrella Galicia and Super Bock, CAMRA says are genuinely brewed abroad but diluted for the British market.

The Barcelona Estrella is 5.4%. The Bedford one is 4.6%. UK duty rises with every tenth of a per cent of alcohol, in a straight line across the whole 3.5% to 8.5% band, so there is no cliff to sit under and no band edge to aim at. At HMRC's draught rate of £19.45 per litre of pure alcohol, that 0.8% is worth about 9p a pint, or around 11p once VAT is added. Fifty-odd pence is roughly the whole duty bill on a 4.6% draught pint, not the saving. The saving is pennies, and it is every pint: when AB InBev trimmed Stella from 5% to 4.8% in 2012, the drinks business put the duty saving at nearly £8.6 million a year on off-trade sales alone.

CAMRA's report names four multinational subsidiaries, AB InBev, Heineken, Carlsberg and Molson Coors, which it says produce nearly 70% of UK-brewed beer, and calls them a "tight oligopoly". The ledger for the four Spanish names on CAMRA's list:

None of this breaks labelling law as it stands. That is CAMRA's complaint: it wants the law changed so a pump clip has to say where the beer was made and by whom. The pint in your glass is what the clip says it is. It is not the pint you drank on holiday.

And then there's Madrí

The brands in that table have a Spanish original you can go and drink. Fly to Barcelona and the Estrella is 5.4%. Fly to Seville and there is a Cruzcampo. Madrí is the awkward case, and not in the way the pump clip suggests.

Madrí Excepcional launched in the UK and Ireland in October 2020, developed by Molson Coors with La Sagra, a brewery near Toledo it has had a stake in since 2017. Molson Coors' own launch post says the beer is brewed at its Tadcaster brewery in Yorkshire, which the York Press summarised as "brewed not in Madrid but Tadcaster". So the Spanish lager on your local's bar is a Yorkshire one: "the Soul of Madrid" as the tagline, a chulapo, the nineteenth-century Madrid dandy, on the label, 4.6% in the glass.

What is not true is that there was never a Spanish Madrí. La Sagra says on its own site that it "fabrica y distribuye la marca Madrí en España desde 2016": it has made and sold a Madrí brand in Spain since 2016, four years before the British one arrived. The accent is part of the name, too.

The Stella parallel

Britain has been here before, with a beer from the country next door. Belgian Stella is 5.2%. UK Stella has come down in steps: to 5% in 2008, to 4.8% in 2012, and to 4.6% in October 2020. Same trademark, same red diamond, weaker beer each time.

The reasons given were never about tax. AB InBev said the 2012 cut brought the brand "in line with evolving UK category trends". In 2020, Budweiser Brewing Group said the change responded to "the growing wellness trend", and its brewmaster Dorien Nijs said drinkers would get "the same Stella Artois taste people know and love, with an ABV of 4.6%".

Madrí runs the same economics from the other end. Rather than shrinking an existing beer, Molson Coors built a new one for the British market and gave it a Spanish name, a Spanish partner and a Spanish story. Many of the big continental brands sold here follow some version of the pattern: keep the branding, keep the heritage story, brew in the UK, trim the strength. CAMRA's report is what joins them up.

"Most big brand 'foreign' lagers are made in the UK, often to diluted recipes; most of the largest 'craft' beer brands are made by corporate producers; and many 'local' beers come from industrial-scale national plants, far from the locality suggested by their names." — CAMRA, Beer in the UK Report, July 2026

Why the app cares

The gap between what the label promises and what the glass delivers is the problem PINtPOINT was built to close. It is the reason PINtDEXTER exists: to describe a beer by what it actually tastes like, from a database that doesn't care which multinational owns the pump clip.

There is nothing wrong with a Bedford-brewed 4.6% lager that says Estrella Damm on the tap. It is a perfectly drinkable pint, and it is not the one you had in Barcelona. CAMRA's argument is simply that the label should say so. Order it, enjoy it, know what it is.

* The caveat Beer tastes better on holiday. Unless you're Spanish, on holiday in the UK, ordering an Estrella.
