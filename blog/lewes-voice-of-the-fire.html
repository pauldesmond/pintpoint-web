Voice of the Fire

The bonfire is a Victorian revival, the brewery has stood beside the river since 1838, and in 2006 the regulars boycotted their own pub when Greene King took Harvey’s off.

On the fifth in Lewes, six of the town’s seven bonfire societies put their processions through a town of nearly seventeen thousand people, and dozens more come in from across Sussex to march with them. The seventh is a juvenile society, and it holds its own night in October. The shops board their windows, the railway station has been known to shut, and the district council asks people not to travel in for it.

A few minutes’ walk from where they pass, Harvey’s have been brewing in Lewes since about 1820 and on the Ouse since 1838. Every November they make Bonfire Boy, 5.8%, and it is poured in the town on the fifth.

This is the second of three posts.

Seventeen martyrs and a Pope

Seventeen Protestants were burned here between 1555 and 1557, under Mary I. They died in the High Street in front of the Star Inn, and tradition holds that ten of them were held beforehand in the inn’s undercroft. Lewes Town Hall, built in 1893, stands on the Star’s site, and the undercroft is still down there. The seventeen burning crosses carried through the streets on 5 November are for them, and they go past the spot. The seventeen are recorded in Foxe’s Book of Martyrs. The story of the cellar is tradition only.

The Pope is the other matter. Lewes has burned an effigy of Paul V since the middle of the nineteenth century, for reasons with nothing to do with 1605: it began as a response to the Oxford Movement and to prominent Englishmen going over to Rome. The historian Jeremy Goring, whose Burn, Holy Fire! covers religion in Lewes since the Reformation, makes the point that Paul V was a peaceable man who simply happened to hold the office in 1605, and cannot be held responsible for the plot.

Lewes was burning popes long before Paul V, mind. Lewes Borough’s own history has processions here in 1679, after the Titus Oates affair. The celebrations waned after that, though not to nothing: a diary records a fire on Gallows Bank in 1813. From the 1820s the Bonfire Boys started them up again with tar barrels, got rowdier every year, and by 1847 the same history has them going for the special constables, the Metropolitan Police sent down from London, and the Riot Act read from the steps of County Hall. The burning of Paul V dates from that nineteenth-century revival, not from 1605. The societies made their own traditions, in public, for reasons they would defend to your face, and have gone on arguing about them ever since. A tradition can be made up and still be real, so long as the people doing it are the ones who made it up and are still prepared to fall out over it.

Bridge Wharf, 1838

The Harveys were selling wine into Lewes in the 1790s and began brewing around 1820 in Bear Yard. They bought the Bridge Wharf site in 1838 and have been there ever since, in a Victorian tower brewery that the processions walk past. Greg Dickinson, writing in the Telegraph in November 2024, calls it Lewes Cathedral, and tells anyone who wants to see all the processions to stand on Cliffe High Street near the bridge, beside the brewery.

The tower is still in use. In 2019 the beer historian Ron Pattinson found a brewing journal in the East Sussex archives, catalogued under Beard’s, the Lewes brewery that closed in 1984, but written in Harvey’s name. It is believed to have belonged to Henry John Barrett, who trained at Harvey’s under his father, the brewery’s third head brewer, and took the journal with him when he moved to Beard’s. Harvey’s brewed one of the recipes in the same tower and put it out on 26 October as Lost Brew No.156, a 7.7% strong ale from 1890. Head brewer Peter Yarlett says the journal holds five more recipes, which Harvey’s hopes to brew.

Bonfire Boy dates only from 1996. By the brewery’s own account it was first brewed as Firecracker, to commemorate what they call the gallant work of the fire brigade during a fire at the brewery in July 1996, and became the beer they make each year for the bonfire boys and belles of Lewes. Nor is the name exclusive: a Polish brewery, Browar Monsters, makes an India export porter called Bonfire Boy. The smoky note in Harvey’s is roasted malt, not smoke.

The 9:56 from York

Harvey’s yeast came from Yorkshire, in October 1957. By Harvey’s own account, their supplier the British Pure Yeast Company stopped trading, and an analytical chemist paying a social call pointed the head brewer, Anthony Jenner, at the Tadcaster Brewery Company instead. In October 1957 the 9:56pm passenger train from York came in with a consignment of yeast, which went into that day’s brew. Jenner’s verdict was that it had settled down to the Sussex air.

It is still the house strain. In May 2026 it went across town, when Harvey’s and Beak brewed Spring at Beak’s brewery with a blend of both their yeasts.

The Lewes Arms boycott

In December 2006 Greene King took Harvey’s Best off at the Lewes Arms. The regulars picketed their own pub, a petition went round, the mayor and the town’s MP objected, and the loss of trade was reported at something like ninety per cent. The boycott ran into a fifth month. Greene King put the beer back in April 2007 and sold the pub to Fuller’s the year after. We wunt be druv, as Sussex has it and Harvey’s print on their own page.

In the days before this year’s fifth, Bonfire Boy was on cask at the John Harvey Tavern, the Rights of Man and the Gardeners Arms, and in bottle at the Black Horse. Every beer in the John Harvey Tavern’s list is a Harvey’s, from Dark Mild at 3.0% through Sussex Best and Armada to Bonfire Boy, with Lost Brew No.156 on cask on the thirty-first. The Black Horse carries Bonfire Boy and little else of theirs.

The Dorset on Malling Street, a Harvey’s house and, by the brewery’s own count, the Cliffe society’s headquarters for a hundred and sixty years, had ten Harvey’s in its recorded history on 2 November, the less obvious ones among them, Lewes Castle Brown and Olympia.

Harvey’s is still poured at the Lewes Arms. Its recorded tap history has Sussex Best in October and Old Ale in September.

Guy Fawkes Bitter, York

Fawkes was a York man, and the Guy Fawkes Inn on High Petergate trades on standing by his birthplace. Its house beer is Guy Fawkes Bitter at 3.6%, and the brewery on it is Joshua Tetley & Son. CAMRA, which has it among the pub’s regulars, files it as Tetley Bitter under another name.

The most recognisable English face on earth belongs to Guy Fawkes, and it was drawn in 1982 by David Lloyd for V for Vendetta, written by Alan Moore. Lloyd reached back to the Gunpowder Plot for a mask to put on an anti-fascist anti-hero. Four decades on it is worn by Anonymous, by Occupy, and at protests in countries that have never heard of Lewes.

Harvey’s list Bonfire Boy on cask for November, and the brewery shop sells it in 500ml bottles all year round.

On the fifth, the processions go past Harvey’s gates.

If you are going

Not on the fifth

Lewes is packed on the fifth, and many pubs are ticket-only or closed. For any other night of the year we have put the nine we would walk on their own page, from the station to the Greenwich meridian.
