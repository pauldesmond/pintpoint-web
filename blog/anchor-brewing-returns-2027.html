Anchor Brewing looks to be coming back. Stitt's labels with it.

Construction crews at Potrero Hill, an owner who says almost nothing in public, and federal paperwork that puts Jim Stitt’s original label art back on Anchor Steam.

The clearest signal of what kind of Anchor is coming back is not on the website. It is on a federal form. In July 2026 KQED reported that the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau had approved labels for Anchor Steam and Old Foghorn, and that both use the original art by Jim Stitt, who drew Anchor’s labels from 1974. Stitt’s illustrations are the single thing the 2021 rebrand threw out most completely.

That rebrand was on the brand for roughly two years before the brewery closed in July 2023. Whether the redesign caused the closure is an argument; the timing is not in dispute.

Hamdi Ulukaya, the Chobani founder who bought Anchor on 31 May 2024, has said almost nothing in public since. The physical signals are easier to read. Hoodline, reporting in June 2026, describes crews in hard hats across the Anchor campus, scaffolding and stripped paint on the De Haro Street facade, and work trucks from Kemper Industrial, an Idaho firm specialising in the ammonia and CO2 refrigeration systems large breweries need. The Chronicle also spotted Barnum Mechanical on site, the design-build outfit behind brewhouses for Sierra Nevada, Stone and Russian River, which counts Chobani among its clients. Anchor’s twenty-eight-year quality assurance manager Andrea Devries has been director of brewing and logistics since February 2025. Veteran 21st Amendment co-founder Shaun O’Sullivan is consulting. Shepherd Futures, Ulukaya’s family office, holds a California ABC permit to produce beer, and on 3 June 2026 a fictitious business name for “Anchor Brewing Company” was filed at 1705 Mariposa Street. None of it is brewing yet. Hoodline’s own summary is that the steam stacks are not billowing, and the 2027 date that has attached itself to this story is, in the same piece, “one industry executive’s educated guess”.

What the rebrand was for

The 2021 rebrand was Sapporo’s attempt to push Anchor’s identity onto a generic modern shelf. Defector put it bluntly: a campaign to “blandify their logo for frictionless consumer identification.” Pete Brown, the British beer writer, writing as the new packaging landed, was sharper still: “The real problem is not that it looks different from how it did, but that it looks too much like everything else, and is too easily replicable.”

What disappeared was the hand-drawn labels Jim Stitt had been making since 1974, the folksy, illustrated, vaguely Victorian register that signalled “independent” against the corporate templates. Brown’s verdict on the swap: “Updating your wardrobe is one thing. Throwing out a wardrobe of, say, bespoke Savile Row suits because they’re old and grey, and replacing them with a bunch of G-Star, Stone Island and Burberry, is quite another.” The new identity, he wrote, “succeeds in damaging existing brand equity, without providing enough new, ownable, distinctive memorable equity to replace it.”

The reaction was severe enough that Anchor issued a statement, which Brown read as telling in itself: “The fact that they felt the need to issue the statement underlines the depth of feeling around the change.” Two years later the brewery closed. The chronology is what it is: Stitt’s illustrated equity overwritten in 2021, distribution pulled back to California in early 2023, and then a closure that staff learned of from an email sent at one in the morning.

Which is what makes the label filings worth more than a website. A homepage can sit untouched for years, and anchorbrewing.com has: it still invites you in for “one last Anchor” before the taps close on 31 July, a sentence from 2023. A label approval is a thing somebody filed this year, for beer they intend to sell.

What Anchor was for

Why this matters more than another brewery reopening comes down to what Anchor was actually for. Fritz Maytag bought a majority stake in 1965, for $5,100, in a near-bankrupt brewery that was the last making Steam Beer. By his own account he did not know what he was getting into. The American craft brewing industry did not exist; he was inventing it as he went, and Sierra Nevada and the late-eighties microbrewery wave were built downstream of his bet.

As Jeff Alworth wrote on closure day in 2023, “Anchor was never just a brewery. It was wrapped up in the myth of America.” What Sapporo closed was the founding image as much as the beer. The ground, as Alworth put it, “may suddenly feel spongy under our feet.”

Reopening into a different room

So what does it mean to bring it back?

The honest answer is: less than people will want it to mean. Anchor closed in 2023 into a craft market that was already past peak, and it returns into a smaller one. US craft volume fell again in 2025, by 4% on the Brewers Association’s revised figure, although craft’s share of the market edged up. Berkeley’s Trumer Pils closed its brewery in May 2026 after 22 years and moved production to Firestone Walker in Paso Robles. Anchor’s own distribution had already been pulled back to California before the closure, so a national footprint would have to be rebuilt rather than resumed.

The geography of taste has also shifted. Anchor Steam was always a category of one, a lager-ale hybrid fermented at California Common temperatures, and it sits between the two ends the modern American drinker has moved toward: hazy IPA at the craft end, mass-market lager at the everyday end. Liberty Ale and Old Foghorn, the other two heritage strengths, sit in categories that today’s leaders define rather than follow.

A leaner Anchor, a heritage producer for a smaller and more loyal audience, is one strategic answer. Competing on the modern craft circuit, with hazy IPAs and a contemporary lineup, would be another. Two label approvals for Anchor Steam and Old Foghorn point at the first. The rest of the strategic picture is not public.

The labour question may matter most. Anchor’s workers voted 31 to 16 to join the ILWU in March 2019, one of the first craft breweries in the country to unionise, and Ulukaya told the Chronicle he planned to rehire as many former workers as possible. In July 2026 KQED reported that the former workers had still had no direct communication from him. Patrick Machel, a former employee and board chair of the Anchor SF Cooperative, which union members formed in 2023 in an attempt to buy the brewery from Sapporo, has said the group got the cold shoulder and has since moved on, as Hoodline relayed. Some institutional knowledge has been retained; the rehiring has not visibly started.

There is a precedent for reaching backwards here, though a messier one than it looks. After the 1906 earthquake destroyed the original brewery, its owners rebuilt to the old specifications rather than the modern German lager model the rest of the country was adopting. The American Conservative’s account has that earning a quick profit and leaving Anchor a thoroughly unremarkable local brewery, which is expedience rather than principle. The phrase that gets attached to it, “radically traditional”, is Anchor historian David Burkhart describing Fritz Maytag sixty years later, and it belongs to Maytag.

Two labels are not a brewery. But they are the first thing anyone in this revival has filed that says what they want Anchor to look like, and what they chose was the art the last owners threw away.
