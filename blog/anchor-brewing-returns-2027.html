Anchor Brewing looks to be coming back. Nobody will say when.

Construction crews at Potrero Hill, an owner who has said almost nothing in public, and a 2027 date that nobody official has given.

Hamdi Ulukaya, the Chobani founder who bought Anchor on 31 May 2024, has said almost nothing in public since: one statement in December 2025 knocking down a rumour that the brewery was leaving San Francisco, and otherwise nothing, through two years of the San Francisco Chronicle asking. The physical signals are easier to read than the owner.

Chronicle photographers found crews in hard hats across the Anchor campus this spring, scaffolding and stripped paint on the De Haro Street facade, and work trucks from Kemper Industrial, the Idaho firm that does the ammonia and CO2 refrigeration systems large breweries need. Barnum Mechanical was spotted on site too, the design-build outfit behind brewhouses for Sierra Nevada, Stone and Russian River, which counts Chobani among its clients. Anchor’s twenty-eight-year quality assurance manager Andrea Devries has been director of brewing and logistics since February 2025. Veteran 21st Amendment co-founder Shaun O’Sullivan is consulting. Shepherd Futures, Ulukaya’s family office, holds a California ABC permit to produce beer. None of it is brewing yet.

The 2021 rebrand was on the brand for roughly two years before the brewery closed in July 2023. Whether the redesign caused the closure is an argument. The timing is not in dispute.

What the rebrand was for

The 2021 rebrand was Sapporo’s attempt to push Anchor’s identity onto a generic modern shelf. Defector put it bluntly: a campaign to “blandify their logo for frictionless consumer identification.” Pete Brown, the British beer writer, writing as the new packaging landed, was sharper still: “The real problem is not that it looks different from how it did, but that it looks too much like everything else, and is too easily replicable.”

What disappeared was the hand-drawn labels Jim Stitt had been making since 1974, the folksy, illustrated, vaguely Victorian register that signalled “independent” against the corporate templates. Brown’s verdict on the swap: “Updating your wardrobe is one thing. Throwing out a wardrobe of, say, bespoke Savile Row suits because they’re old and grey, and replacing them with a bunch of G-Star, Stone Island and Burberry, is quite another.” The new identity, he wrote, “succeeds in damaging existing brand equity, without providing enough new, ownable, distinctive memorable equity to replace it.”

The reaction was severe enough that Anchor issued a statement, which Brown read as telling in itself: “The fact that they felt the need to issue the statement underlines the depth of feeling around the change.” Two years later the brewery closed. The chronology is what it is: Stitt’s illustrated equity overwritten in 2021, distribution pulled back to California in early 2023, and then a closure that staff learned of from an email sent at one in the morning.

None of which tells you which Anchor is coming back. The website will not tell you either: anchorbrewing.com still invites you in for “one last Anchor” before the taps close on 31 July, a sentence left standing since 2023, under the logo the 2021 rebrand gave it. A page nobody has touched in three years is not evidence of anything.

What Anchor was for

Why this matters more than another brewery reopening comes down to what Anchor was actually for. Fritz Maytag bought a majority stake in 1965, for $5,100, in a near-bankrupt brewery that was the last making Steam Beer. By his own account he did not know what he was getting into. The American craft brewing industry did not exist. He was inventing it as he went, and Sierra Nevada and the late-eighties microbrewery wave were built downstream of his bet.

As Jeff Alworth wrote on closure day in 2023, “Anchor was never just a brewery. It was wrapped up in the myth of America.” What Sapporo closed was the founding image as much as the beer. The ground, as Alworth put it, “may suddenly feel spongy under our feet.”

Reopening into a different room

So what does it mean to bring it back?

The honest answer is: less than people will want it to mean. Anchor closed in 2023 into a craft market that was already past peak, and it returns into a smaller one. US craft volume fell about 5% in 2025 on the Brewers Association’s first count. Bay Area trailblazer 21st Amendment closed in November 2025, though its beers came back in April under Evil Genius of Philadelphia, which cuts against the gloom as much as it feeds it. In March 2026 Berkeley’s Trumer Pils announced it was leaving the Bay Area. Anchor’s own distribution had been pulled back to California before the closure, so a national footprint would have to be rebuilt rather than resumed.

The geography of taste has also shifted. Anchor Steam was always a category of one, a lager-ale hybrid fermented at California Common temperatures, and it sits between the two ends the modern American drinker has moved toward: hazy IPA at the craft end, mass-market lager at the everyday end. Liberty Ale and Old Foghorn, the other two heritage strengths, sit in categories that today’s leaders define rather than follow.

A leaner Anchor, a heritage producer for a smaller and more loyal audience, is one strategic answer. Competing on the modern craft circuit, with hazy IPAs and a contemporary lineup, would be another. Nothing public says which, and the people hired so far are brewers and engineers rather than a brand.

The labour question may matter most. Anchor’s workers voted 31 to 16 to join the ILWU in March 2019, one of the first craft breweries in the country to unionise, and Ulukaya told the Chronicle he planned to rehire as many former workers as possible. Two years on it has not visibly started. Patrick Machel, a former employee and board chair of the Anchor SF Cooperative, which union members formed in 2023 in an attempt to buy the brewery from Sapporo, told the Chronicle the group got the cold shoulder despite repeated attempts to connect, and has since moved on. Some institutional knowledge has been kept. The promise has not yet been. There’s enough motion to take a return seriously. There isn’t yet enough public information to know whose Anchor it’ll be.

There is a precedent for reaching backwards here, though a messier one than it looks. After the 1906 earthquake destroyed the original brewery, its owners rebuilt to the old specifications rather than the modern German lager model the rest of the country was adopting. The American Conservative’s account has that earning a quick profit and leaving Anchor a thoroughly unremarkable local brewery, which is expedience rather than principle. The phrase that gets attached to it, “radically traditional”, is Anchor historian David Burkhart describing Fritz Maytag sixty years later, and it belongs to Maytag. Burkhart’s The Anchor Brewing Story (Ten Speed Press, 2022) is the long version of all of it.

The crews are real and the licences are real. The date is not: 2027 is one industry executive’s guess, and nobody who owns the place has said a word about when.

What has happened since

Added 3 November 2026. Everything above is the piece as published on 22 May 2026, corrected where it was wrong on the day. This is what has happened since, and it is kept separate so you can tell which is which.

29 May. The Brewers Association revised 2025 craft volume down 4% rather than 5%, with craft’s share of the market edging up. Berkeley’s Trumer Pils closed its brewery after 22 years, production moving to Firestone Walker in Paso Robles.

3 June. A fictitious business name for “Anchor Brewing Company” was filed at 1705 Mariposa Street, found by Hoodline in the county clerk’s records. The same piece puts the 2027 date where it belongs: “one industry executive’s educated guess”, and says in as many words that the steam stacks are not billowing yet.

2 July. The federal Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau approved labels for Anchor Steam and Old Foghorn, and both use the original art by Jim Stitt, who drew Anchor’s labels for 45 years. That is the first thing anyone in this revival has filed that says what they want Anchor to look like, and what they chose was the art the 2021 rebrand threw away. The same report says former workers had still had no direct contact from Ulukaya, and that he posted a photograph of himself in the Anchor taproom on 19 June.

September and October. The SF Standard reported no timetable and a relaunch adviser brought in, and SF Heritage described the site as dormant. As of today there is still no reopening date and no beer on sale.
