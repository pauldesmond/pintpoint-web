Why Firestone Walker chose Fuller’s yeast

Firestone Walker’s Double Barrel Ale took silver in English-Style Pale Ale at the Great American Beer Festival this month. The yeast it was built on came from Wyeast, a laboratory in Oregon, and Walker remembers it as a Fuller’s ESB strain.

The American influence on British beer is usually told through hops. This one runs the other way: a Californian brewery choosing an English yeast despite a cleaner alternative, and keeping something of its character ever since.

Why they chose it

David Walker, the English half of Firestone Walker, set out how the brewery picked the strain for Double Barrel Ale in a letter to Jeffrey House this October, forwarded to us with his permission to quote. The same passages appear in Gordon A. Ponce’s account of the transatlantic loop at The Craft Beer Finder. What decided it was beer trials.

“The number given to the original DBA yeast (Wyeast) we can’t recall at the moment, but it was a Fullers ESB yeast. Why did we use it? Because in beer trials, that’s the one we liked best even though the Chico yeast was cleaner.”

Chico, the Sierra Nevada strain and the workhorse of American craft, was the cleaner ferment, and they knew it. Cleaner is a flavour word here, not a hygiene one: a ferment that leaves less of itself behind in the beer. They chose the Fuller’s yeast anyway. They preferred the beer it made. The original catalogue number has not yet been confirmed. Wyeast wrote in September 2023 that their own 1968 London ESB Ale strain had been isolated for their culture collection thirty years earlier.

One thing in that quotation needs correcting, from Chiswick. Walker calls it a Fuller’s ESB yeast, and Wyeast’s catalogue name carries London ESB too, but Fuller’s never had an ESB yeast. John Keeling, who joined the brewery in 1981 and was its head brewer from 1999 until 2017, wrote to us and to Jeffrey House this October: “There was no such thing as ESB yeast. All pitching yeast came from London Pride fermentations because it was the best and easiest yeast to use.” One house yeast went into Pride, ESB and Chiswick Bitter alike. The ESB in the catalogue name is the beer the strain got attached to, not a strain the brewery kept apart from the others.

The yeast was not the only English thing they chose. DBA has been partially fermented since 1996 in what the brewery calls the Firestone Union, a set of individual 60-gallon oak barrels that Firestone Walker says was inspired by the 150-year-old Burton Union process and describes as “a nod to the traditional cask-fermented English ales that first inspired us”. So an English yeast went into an English fermentation method. The brewery’s own account of the barrels has commercial brewers abandoning the process for the additional care and expense it required.

Adapting the yeast to the brewery

A yeast that settles readily is easier to collect for the next brew. But if it settles before finishing its clean-up work, that advantage becomes a problem.

“The Fullers yeast was a prodigious producer and flocculator, which was beneficial when reharvesting yeast for future brews. But that same flocculation characteristic lead to Diacetyl issues.”

Diacetyl is the buttery, butterscotch note that most brewers spend their time getting rid of, and the way they get rid of it is to leave the yeast in the beer long enough to take it back up. A strain that flocculates hard can be at the bottom of the tank before that is done.

Walker describes what that meant in a young brewery’s laboratory:

“In the early days our lab, such as it was, could measure diacetyl in the tank and its reduction during maturation, but we couldn’t measure its precursor. Our issues with diacetyl occurred during packaging when the hidden precursor mixed with a little oxygen and turned into diacetyl.”

They could watch the fault fall away in the tank and sign the beer off. What they could not see was the compound waiting behind it, which, in Walker’s account, became diacetyl when exposed to oxygen during packaging.

“It was a great yeast when we could tame it, but a nightmare otherwise and we adjusted within a few years.”

Fuller’s in America

Fuller’s already had an American audience, and Jeffrey House had helped build it. House founded Thames America Trading Company at the turn of 1980 and brought London Pride and ESB to the United States, with draught ESB following in 1987. We have written before about the other direction of that trade: Anchor’s beers crossing back in Fuller’s returnable kegs for a London pub trial in late 1991.

Walker’s own introduction came earlier, and was more personal than the imports: Walker recalled his early encounters with London Pride and his visits to the Griffin Brewery at Chiswick to Gordon A. Ponce, noting how quietly Fuller’s had played its hand in their journey.

Fuller’s had already sent its yeast west once. In 1982 John Mitchell, an English expatriate opening Canada’s first craft brewery at Horseshoe Bay in British Columbia, asked larger breweries for yeast and, as Noëlle Phillips tells it for the BC Ale Trail, his appeals “had gone unanswered or were rejected. No one was interested.” Fuller’s said yes. John Ohler, who runs the John Mitchell Legacy Foundation in British Columbia, wrote to us this October to say Mitchell flew to England before opening in order to secure it, and had the yeast directly from the Griffin Brewery. With Frank Appleton designing the recipe and brewing it, the yeast shipped from Chiswick made Bay Ale, a beer in the style of London Pride, first tapped that summer. It is a separate crossing from the polypins Jeffrey House remembers going out at KQED’s beer festival in San Francisco in 1983, with homebrewers collecting the live yeast out of them afterwards, and it went to a different coast. Whether either of them has anything to do with the 1968 strain is what nobody can yet say, and Keeling’s point sharpens the problem: if one house yeast served every Fuller’s beer, then every route out of Chiswick carried the same strain, and the polypins stop being a special case.

The continuing Fuller’s influence

Within a few years, Firestone Walker moved to a more reliable derivative of the original yeast. Read what Walker says it kept:

“We eventually morphed to a derivation of this and more reliable for our needs; now our OG House Ale yeast. Still essentially a Fullers/London yeast.”

What has come in since

Added 10 November 2026. Everything above is the piece as published on 17 October 2026. This is what has reached us since, and it is kept separate so you can tell which is which.

A second American brewery on a Fuller’s strain. Jeffrey House, whose polypins appear above, has since put the question to John Hall, who founded Goose Island in Chicago in 1988. Hall’s account is that four yeast strains figured in the development of Honkers Ale, among them Wyeast 1968 London ESB and a Weihenstephan strain. Hall separately named London Pride and Anchor’s Liberty Ale as the beers he measured Honkers against. How the culture reached Wyeast in the first place is still an open question, which is where this piece left it too.

Fuller’s was the model, and Hall has never hidden it. He told the Brewers Journal in April 2016: “Look at Fuller’s. It’s a big brewery, based in London which is obviously a big city. It just resonated with me. The dream for me was to be a Fuller’s, to represent Chicago like Fuller’s does in London.” Chicago Beer Geeks, writing from the brewery’s thirtieth anniversary in May 2018, put it flatly: London Pride was Hall’s favourite beer and the inspiration for Honkers Ale. Hall’s wife Pat named it.

Then it ran back the other way. Goose Island’s 30th Anniversary Ale was a collaboration with Fuller’s and a tribute to ESB. John Keeling wrote about it on the Fuller’s blog on 23 April 2018, ahead of a trip that ended in Chicago for the anniversary, and said it was ESB that had inspired Hall to start brewing in the first place. Chicago Beer Geeks list the beer at 6%, on an all-English malt bill with American hops, and say the idea for it started about a year earlier with Keeling and Goose Island’s John Jankoski and Mike Siegel. The launch listing put it at The Hydrant, a Fuller’s pub by the Monument in the City, on 30 May 2018, said only a small amount was coming to Britain, and billed an exclusive tasting from Keeling and Goose Island’s Ken Stout. BeerViking, writing the following week, found kegs brought over specially and Stout on hand to talk about them.

Hall told Jeffrey House that Keeling brought Fuller’s yeast over from London for a beer the two of them made at Goose Island. That is the crossing this piece has been chasing, a Fuller’s culture going west by hand rather than through a laboratory’s catalogue. Whether it was the anniversary ale, Hall does not say.

Keeling has since described that beer from his own side. Fuller’s own video for Cask Ale Week 2022, filmed in the Hock Cellar with Keeling and Fuller’s head brewer Guy Stewart, has him saying that an ESB was what Hall wanted as the last beer he would make as the owner of Goose Island, and that Hall asked him to come across and brew it with him. The video adds the circumstance behind the admiration Keeling wrote about on the Fuller’s blog: Hall had drunk ESB in London pubs while working in the city. Whether that is the anniversary ale is still open, and the specifications do not settle it either way. Chicago Beer Geeks, quoted above, called that beer an ESB with expressive American hops and said it began with the Maris Otter malt bill of an ESB, bittered with Pilgrim, an English hop, and dry-hopped with the New Mexican 472 and Denali. That is a good deal closer to what Hall asked for than its Untappd listing as an ordinary bitter suggests. What sits against it is the origin: the same report has the idea arriving a year earlier with three brewers rather than with a founder asking for his last beer, and Goose Island’s own description of it calls it a pale ale combining styles from both sides of the Atlantic. Neither man closes the question. What has changed is that both participants are now on the record describing a beer the two of them made together, which is further than this piece could get in October.

Two yeasts at Chiswick, not one. Fuller’s wrote in March 2015 that it had two strains of yeast on site, the Fuller’s yeast and the Gale’s yeast, kept “at opposite ends of the brewery to avoid any potential cross contamination”. The house strain, it said, gives orange citrus and toffee, with marmalade notes at higher strengths, while the Gale’s yeast brings a soft fruit flavour with red berries coming through. Both were “stored off site by the Cara Technology, kept under liquid nitrogen and cannot be shared with any other brewer”.

And ESB is where you are meant to taste it. The same video puts the question directly, what Fuller’s yeast brings to a beer, and the answer Fuller’s gave in 2022 was an instruction rather than a tasting note: go and drink ESB. The orange is the yeast’s work, and it carries in ESB because the beer is brewed at a higher gravity than London Pride, which puts more of the fermentation’s fruit in the glass. It is in Pride as well and harder to find. That sits underneath what the brewery wrote in 2015 about marmalade notes at higher strengths and supplies the reason for it. One house yeast, two gravities, and it is the stronger beer that shows you the strain.

Homebrewers who have chased the strain are less sure than the catalogues are. On HomeBrewTalk, brewers who have used 1968 and WLP002 say neither tastes like Fuller’s beer, and one calls them “just another Whitbread yeast” whether or not they came from Chiswick. A yeast family tree built from published sequence data in 2018 does place 1968 in the Whitbread B group. That tells us which family the catalogue strain belongs to. It does not tell us where it came from, and we pass the rest on as the view of the brewers who said it.
